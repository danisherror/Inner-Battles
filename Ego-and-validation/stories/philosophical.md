**Epictetus and the laughing crowd**

Epictetus is not telling a story in *Enchiridion* 22, but he pictures a scene. "If you have an earnest desire of attaining to philosophy, prepare yourself from the very first to be laughed at, to be sneered by the multitude, to hear them say, 'He is returned to us a philosopher all at once,' and 'Whence this supercilious look?'"

He says what to do: "don't have a supercilious look indeed; but keep steadily to those things which appear best to you as one appointed by God to this station." He adds: "if you adhere to the same point, those very persons who at first ridiculed will afterwards admire you. But if you are conquered by them, you will incur a double ridicule."

**What it shows:** Epictetus says to expect mockery and to keep to what you think best, whatever the crowd says. The passage is about taking up philosophy and not about the need for approval; the link is ours, and it is one Stoic's advice.
