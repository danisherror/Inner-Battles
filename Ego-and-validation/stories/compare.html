Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how the need for validation works and how people loosen its grip.

Tomas's story

Tomas designs posters and posts one each week. For a year he checked how it was doing every few minutes. A good number lifted his whole day, and a quiet one sent him back to the file to change things that were fine.

He noticed it when a friend said the poster looked great and he found himself waiting for more. What helped:

  1. He noticed what he was checking. He kept a tally for a week and was shocked by how long it ran.
  2. He named the domain. His worth was tied to being seen as talented, and he saw how exposed that made him.
  3. He did some work unseen. He made a poster each week that he did not post, only to see what he liked.
  4. He spoke to himself as to a friend. After a flat week he said what he would have said to a colleague, not what he would have said to himself.
  5. He kept some approval. He still liked a kind word. He stopped steering his week by the count.
  6. He asked for help. The urge to refresh stayed strong, and he talked to his doctor, who suggested a counsellor.

The wish to be liked did not vanish. It stopped deciding his mood.

What it shows: Tomas did not have to stop caring what anyone thought. Putting some of his worth somewhere that no count could reach did what checking never could.

Hinduism

Arjuna and the pride that decides

The Gita has no tale of a person who needs praise, so this is a moment in the dialogue and not a story of validation. Near the end Krishna warns Arjuna: "If you always remember Me, by My grace you shall overcome all obstacles and difficulties. But if, due to pride, you do not listen to My advice, you will perish." And: "If, motivated by pride, you think, 'I shall not fight,' your decision will be in vain. Your own nature will compel you to fight" (18.58-59).

He goes on: "that action which out of delusion you do not wish to do, you will be driven to do it by your own inclination, born of your own material nature" (18.60).

Arjuna answers in 18.73: "O Infallible One, by Your grace my illusion has been dispelled, and I am situated in knowledge. I am now free from doubts, and I shall act according to Your instructions."

What it shows: A pride that decides for you can be set down, and Arjuna says so himself. The passage is about pride, duty and fighting and not about approval; the link is ours, and Arjuna's reply is about the whole teaching and not only about pride.

Upanishads

Nachiketas and the things on offer

In the Katha Upanishad the boy Nachiketas is offered all that people want. Death says: "Choose sons and grandsons who shall live a hundred years, herds of cattle, elephants, gold, and horses." He also says "choose wealth, and long life", and "I make thee the enjoyer of all desires" (1.1.23-24).

Nachiketas does not take it: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses. Even the whole of life is short. Keep thou thy horses, keep dance and song for thyself" (1.1.26). He adds: "No man can be made happy by wealth" (1.1.27), and returns to his question about what lies beyond death.

What it shows: Nachiketas is not bought by what is offered. The story is about death and knowledge and not about validation; the link is ours, and the text names wealth, long life and pleasures and not praise.

Christianity

The Pharisee and the tax collector

In Luke 18 Jesus tells a parable "to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and regarded others with contempt". Two men go to the temple to pray. The Pharisee prays "standing by himself", thanking God "that I am not like other people: thieves, rogues, adulterers, or even like this tax collector. I fast twice a week; I give a tenth of all my income" (18:11-12).

The tax collector, "standing far off, would not even lift up his eyes to heaven but was beating his breast and saying, 'God, be merciful to me, a sinner!'" (18:13).

Jesus says: "I tell you, this man went down to his home justified rather than the other, for all who exalt themselves will be humbled, but all who humble themselves will be exalted" (18:14).

What it shows: The Pharisee needs a lesser man beside him to feel good about himself. The parable is about prayer and justification and not about approval as such; the link is ours, and it does not say that every Pharisee was like this one.

Buddhism

Khemaka and the scent of the flower

In SN 22.89 senior mendicants ask the sick Khemaka whether he regards anything in the five grasping aggregates as self. He answers: "I do not regard anything among these five grasping aggregates as self or as belonging to self." They say that makes him a perfected one, but he replies: "I am not a perfected one, with defilements ended. For when it comes to the five grasping aggregates I'm not rid of the conceit 'I am'. But I don't regard anything as 'I am this'."

When they press him on what "I am" refers to, he says: "It's like the scent of a blue water lily, or a pink or white lotus. Would it be right to say that the scent belongs to the petals or the stalk or the pistil?" "No, reverend." "It would be right to say that the scent belongs to the flower."

He tells them that a noble disciple, though they have given up the five lower fetters, "still have a lingering residue of the conceit 'I am'", and that it is removed as they meditate "observing rise and fall in the five grasping aggregates." At the end, "the minds of sixty senior mendicants and of Venerable Khemaka were freed from defilements by not grasping."

What it shows: A subtle sense of "I am" can remain after the coarser views are gone, and it fades by watching. The sutta is about the sense of self and not about validation as such; the link is ours.

Philosophical

Epictetus and the laughing crowd

Epictetus is not telling a story in Enchiridion 22, but he pictures a scene. "If you have an earnest desire of attaining to philosophy, prepare yourself from the very first to be laughed at, to be sneered by the multitude, to hear them say, 'He is returned to us a philosopher all at once,' and 'Whence this supercilious look?'"

He says what to do: "don't have a supercilious look indeed; but keep steadily to those things which appear best to you as one appointed by God to this station." He adds: "if you adhere to the same point, those very persons who at first ridiculed will afterwards admire you. But if you are conquered by them, you will incur a double ridicule."

What it shows: Epictetus says to expect mockery and to keep to what you think best, whatever the crowd says. The passage is about taking up philosophy and not about the need for approval; the link is ours, and it is one Stoic's advice.
