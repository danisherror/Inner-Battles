Ego and validation

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here suggests costs when worth depends on approval, and its limits matter.

  • Long-run costs. Crocker and Park write that "when people have self-validation goals, they react to threats in these domains in ways that undermine learning; relatedness; autonomy and self-regulation; and over time, mental and physical health." They conclude that "the short-term emotional benefits of pursuing self-esteem are often outweighed by long-term costs." This is a review that proposes an account, and it is not one experiment.
  • Narcissism. In Neff and Vonk's first study (N = 2,187) "self-esteem (but not self-compassion) was positively associated with narcissism." This is a correlation in self-report data, and it does not show that high self-esteem leads to narcissism.
  • Less stable worth. The same study found that self-compassion "predicted more stable feelings of self-worth than self-esteem and was less contingent on particular outcomes."

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: saying yes to avoid disapproval, hiding opinions, being hurt by neutral feedback, and feeling empty after praise fades. If this is wearing you down, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Crocker and Park (2004), Psychological Bulletin 130(3), 392-414. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.392 (external link)
  • Neff and Vonk (2009), Journal of Personality 77(1), 23-50. doi:10.1111/j.1467-6494.2008.00537.x (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita says what ego-driven action costs. Gita 17.18: "Austerity that is performed with ostentation for the sake of gaining honor, respect, and adoration is in the mode of passion. Its benefits are unstable and transitory."

Krishna also warns Arjuna: "But if, due to pride, you do not listen to My advice, you will perish" (18.58).

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. 17.18 is about one kind of austerity, and 18.58 is a warning to one person about pride. Reading them as the cost of seeking approval is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads name the cost of choosing the pleasant. Katha 1.2.1: "It is well with him who clings to the good; he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."

Mundaka 3.1.2 describes the grief: "On the same tree man sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. The verses are about the good and the pleasant and about the self and the other lord, and do not mention approval. Reading them as the toll of living on applause is ours.

Christianity

The Gospels say that praise sought from people is all the reward there is. Matthew 6:2: "So whenever you give alms, do not sound a trumpet before you, as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, so that they may be praised by others. Truly I tell you, they have received their reward."

Matthew 23:5-7 describes the habit: "They do all their deeds to be seen by others... They love to have the place of honor at banquets and the best seats in the synagogues and to be greeted with respect in the marketplaces and to have people call them rabbi."

And Luke 6:26: "Woe to you when all speak well of you, for that is how their ancestors treated the false prophets."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These verses are about giving, religious leaders and prophets. Reading them as the cost of living on approval is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts say what chasing praise costs. AN 8.6: "Being so full of favoring and opposing, they're not freed from rebirth, old age, and death, from sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress." The phrase comes after "They favor praise and oppose blame."

Dhp 75 puts the choice: "For the means to profit and the path to quenching are two quite different things. A mendicant disciple of the Buddha, understanding what this really means, would never delight in honors, but rather would foster seclusion."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. AN 8.6 is about eight worldly conditions and Dhp 75 is addressed to a mendicant. Reading them as the cost of needing approval is ours, and the texts are not describing everyday social life.

Philosophical

Epictetus says what it costs to live for others' approval. Enchiridion 23: "If you ever happen to turn your attention to externals, so as to wish to please anyone, be assured that you have ruined your scheme of life."

Marcus Aurelius turns to the wish to be remembered. Meditations 4: "He who has a vehement desire for posthumous fame does not consider that every one of those who remember him will himself also die very soon". The passage goes on to ask what even immortal remembrance would be "to thee".

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. Epictetus is writing about a philosopher's life and Marcus about fame after death. Reading them as the cost of needing approval is ours.
