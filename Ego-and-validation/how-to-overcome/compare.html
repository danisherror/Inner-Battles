Ego and validation

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here suggests that how you relate to yourself matters, and says little about method. The rest is common advice.

  1. Notice what you are checking. Count how often in a day you look for a reaction, such as a like, a reply or a nod. That is common advice.
  2. Ask whose approval you want. Crocker and Park say the pursuit works through "domains in which their self-worth is invested". Naming the domain, such as looks, work or being liked, shows where you are exposed. Naming it is our suggestion, not their method.
  3. Treat yourself the way you would a friend. Neff and Vonk found that self-compassion "predicted more stable feelings of self-worth than self-esteem and was less contingent on particular outcomes." It entails "treating oneself with kindness, recognizing one's shared humanity, and being mindful when considering negative aspects of oneself." This was a correlation, and the abstract does not test a fix.
  4. Do some things unseen. Write, practise or help without telling anyone. That is common advice.
  5. Keep some approval. Wanting to be valued is normal, and these papers do not say otherwise.
  6. Talk to someone. If your sense of worth feels wholly dependent on others, a doctor or therapist can help. That is common advice.

Sources

  • Neff and Vonk (2009), Journal of Personality 77(1), 23-50. doi:10.1111/j.1467-6494.2008.00537.x (external link)
  • Crocker and Park (2004), Psychological Bulletin 130(3), 392-414. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.392 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. Crocker and Park's abstract says that "alternatives to pursuing self-esteem, and ways of avoiding its costs, are discussed", and those were not read, so none is described here. Steps 1, 2, 4, 5 and 6 are common advice, not tied to a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita's counsel is to see through the doer and to hold praise and blame alike.

  1. See that you are not the sole doer. Gita 3.27: "All activities are carried out by the three modes of material nature. But in ignorance, the soul, deluded by false identification with the body, thinks of itself as the doer."
  2. Let go of egoism. Gita 2.71: "That person, who gives up all material desires and lives free from a sense of greed, proprietorship, and egoism, attains perfect peace."
  3. Meet honour and dishonour evenly. Gita 12.18-19 describes those "equipoised in honor and dishonor... who take praise and reproach alike". And 14.24-25: "who accept both blame and praise with equanimity; who remain the same in honor and dishonor".
  4. Cultivate humility. Gita 13.8 begins its list with "Humbleness; freedom from hypocrisy" and includes "absence of egotism".

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These verses are about doership, devotion and equanimity, and the Gita sets them within its own path. Reading them as a way through the need for approval is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to the witness and to the good over the pleasant.

  1. Choose the good over the pleasant. Katha 1.2.1: "It is well with him who clings to the good; he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."
  2. Look to the other lord. Mundaka 3.1.2: when man sees "the other lord" "contented and knows his glory, then his grief passes away."
  3. Be unmoved by what is offered. In the Katha, Death offers Nachiketas long life, wealth and pleasures, and he answers: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses" (1.1.26). And: "No man can be made happy by wealth" (1.1.27).

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These verses are about the Self, the good and the pleasant, and wealth. Reading them as a way through the need for approval is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Christianity

The Bible's counsel is to do good quietly and to look to a different judge.

  1. Give in secret. Matthew 6:3-4: "But when you give alms, do not let your left hand know what your right hand is doing, so that your alms may be done in secret, and your Father who sees in secret will reward you."
  2. Pray unseen. Matthew 6:6: "But whenever you pray, go into your room and shut the door and pray to your Father who is in secret".
  3. Serve. Matthew 23:11-12: "The greatest among you will be your servant. All who exalt themselves will be humbled, and all who humble themselves will be exalted."
  4. Ask whom you are trying to please. Galatians 1:10: "Am I now seeking human approval or God's approval? Or am I trying to please people? If I were still pleasing people, I would not be a servant of Christ."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These verses are about giving, prayer and service, and they do not tell anyone to avoid all company or praise. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts counsel seeing every condition as passing.

  1. Reflect on each condition. AN 8.6: "A learned noble disciple encounters gain. They reflect: 'I've encountered this gain. It's impermanent, suffering, and perishable.' They truly understand it." The same is said of praise and blame.
  2. Stop favouring and opposing. AN 8.6: "They don't favor praise or oppose blame."
  3. Remember nobody is all one thing. Dhp 228: "There never was, nor will be, nor is there today, someone who is wholly praised or wholly blamed."
  4. Be steady like a rock. Dhp 81: "As the wind cannot stir a solid mass of rock, so too blame and praise do not affect the wise."
  5. Watch the "I am" rise and fall. SN 22.89 says the lingering residue is removed as the noble disciple meditates "observing rise and fall in the five grasping aggregates."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These passages are about the eight worldly conditions and the grasping aggregates, and they sit within the Buddhist path. Reading them as a way through the need for approval is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Philosophical

The philosophers' counsel is to judge by your own standard.

  1. Be your own audience first. Epictetus, Enchiridion 23: "Be contented, then, in everything with being a philosopher; and, if you wish to be thought so likewise by anyone, appear so to yourself, and it will suffice you."
  2. Accept being laughed at. Enchiridion 22: "prepare yourself from the very first to be laughed at, to be sneered by the multitude".
  3. Count dishonour fairly. Enchiridion 24: "if dishonor is an evil, you can no more be involved in any evil by the means of another, than be engaged in anything base."
  4. Ask whose opinion you weigh. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 12: "So much more respect have we to what our neighbours shall think of us than to what we shall think of ourselves."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These passages are written for a philosopher's life and are one school's discipline, not a rule for everyone. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.
