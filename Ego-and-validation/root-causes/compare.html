Ego and validation

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here point to how approval can come to steer us more than to one origin.

  • Worth that is staked on a domain. Crocker and Park argue that "in domains in which their self-worth is invested, people adopt the goal to validate their abilities and qualities, and hence their self-worth", and they consider "cultural roots of the pursuit of self-esteem". This is a review of research, and the abstract does not say what those roots are.
  • Peer endorsement. Sherman and colleagues scanned adolescents while they viewed photos ostensibly posted to Instagram. They "were more likely to like photos depicted with many likes than photos with few likes", and viewing photos with many likes "was associated with greater activity in neural regions implicated in reward processing, social cognition, imitation, and attention." This was a laboratory simulation with adolescents, and it does not show that likes cause a need for approval.

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: being praised for results more than for effort, growing up where love felt conditional, and social media that shows other people's response as a number.

Sources

  • Crocker and Park (2004), Psychological Bulletin 130(3), 392-414. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.392 (external link)
  • Sherman, Payton, Hernandez, Greenfield and Dapretto (2016), "The power of the like in adolescence: effects of peer influence on neural and behavioral responses to social media," Psychological Science 27(7), 1027-1035. doi:10.1177/0956797616645673 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita's root is the ego that sets itself above others. In 16.13-15 Krishna describes people who think, "I am like God Himself, I am the enjoyer, I am perfect, I am powerful, and I am happy. I am wealthy and I have highly placed relatives. Who else is equal to me?" The verses end: "In this way, they are deluded by ignorance."

Verse 16.4 lists "hypocrisy, arrogance, conceit, anger, harshness, and ignorance" among the qualities of a demoniac nature.

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. The Gita describes pride and self-importance, and not a craving to be admired. Reading the first as a root of the second is ours.

Upanishads

In the Katha Upanishad Death names a root: choosing what pleases over what is good. Katha 1.2.1: "The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man."

And 1.2.5: "Fools dwelling in darkness, wise in their own conceit, and puffed up with vain knowledge, go round and round, staggering to and fro, like blind men led by the blind."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. The verses are about the good and the pleasant and about conceit in knowledge, and do not mention approval. Taking applause as one of the pleasant things is ours.

Christianity

The Gospels name the roots as trust in oneself and love of human glory. Luke 18:9 introduces a parable "to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and regarded others with contempt".

John 12:42-43: "Nevertheless many, even of the authorities, believed in him. But because of the Pharisees they did not confess it, for fear that they would be put out of the synagogue, for they loved human glory more than the glory that comes from God."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. The verses are about faith, self-righteousness and fear of expulsion, and the text does not describe what the authorities felt inside. Taking their fear of losing standing as a form of the need for approval is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist root is craving for status and the grasping of "I am". Dhp 74 describes the fool who wants to be the one in charge: "Let both layfolk and renunciants think the work was done by me alone. In anything at all that's to be done, let them fall under my sway alone." The verse ends: "So thinks the fool, their greed and pride only growing."

AN 8.6 names what keeps the pull alive: "So gain and loss, fame and disgrace, blame and praise, and pleasure and pain occupy their mind."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. Dhp 74 is about wanting authority and recognition, and AN 8.6 is about eight worldly conditions. Reading both as roots of needing approval is ours.

Philosophical

Marcus Aurelius notices a strange imbalance. Meditations 12.4: "I have often wondered how it is that every man loves himself more than all the rest of men, but yet sets less value on his own opinion of himself than on the opinion of others."

Aristotle gives a reason for seeking honour. Nicomachean Ethics 1.5: "men seem to pursue honour in order that they may be assured of their goodness". He adds that they seek to be honoured "by men of practical wisdom" and "among those who know them, and on the ground of their virtue".

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. Marcus is describing a habit, and Aristotle is describing why people value honour. Putting them together as roots is ours, and this passage does not say whether wanting honour is a fault.

Sources

  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 12 (external link) (George Long's translation)
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.5 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
