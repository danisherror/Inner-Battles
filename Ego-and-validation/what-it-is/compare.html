Inner battle

Ego and validation

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The need for validation is relying on other people's approval, praise or admiration to feel that you are worth something. "Ego" is used loosely here: the self you display and defend. Wanting to be liked is ordinary; the trouble starts when your sense of worth rises and falls with the response you get.

Crocker and Park describe it as a goal: "in domains in which their self-worth is invested, people adopt the goal to validate their abilities and qualities, and hence their self-worth." They propose that "the importance of self-esteem lies more in how people strive for it rather than whether it is high or low."

Neff and Vonk compared self-esteem with self-compassion in 2,187 people and found that self-compassion "predicted more stable feelings of self-worth than self-esteem and was less contingent on particular outcomes."

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: checking how a post is doing, rehearsing a remark that was not praised, shaping an opinion to the room, and a day that feels ruined by one cool reply. It overlaps with self-criticism and self-doubt.

This is not a diagnosis. If the need for approval is wearing you down, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Crocker and Park (2004), "The costly pursuit of self-esteem," Psychological Bulletin 130(3), 392-414. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.130.3.392 (external link)
  • Neff and Vonk (2009), "Self-compassion versus global self-esteem: two different ways of relating to oneself," Journal of Personality 77(1), 23-50. doi:10.1111/j.1467-6494.2008.00537.x (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The Neff and Vonk sample size is the abstract's. The signs listed and the overlaps are a standard summary and not drawn from a source.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for needing approval, but it names the ego-sense behind it. Gita 3.27: "All activities are carried out by the three modes of material nature. But in ignorance, the soul, deluded by false identification with the body, thinks of itself as the doer."

The page's word-by-word gloss renders the compound for "deluded" as "those who are bewildered by the ego". A self that believes it is the doer will also want credit for what is done.

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. The verse is about doership and false identification, and does not mention praise. The step from doer to wanting credit is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not speak of validation, and the nearest picture is the two birds. Mundaka 3.1.1: "Two birds, inseparable friends, cling to the same tree. One of them eats the sweet fruit, the other looks on without eating."

Mundaka 3.1.2: "On the same tree man sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. The verses are about the individual self and the other lord, and do not mention approval. Reading the bird that eats as the self that feeds on praise is ours.

Christianity

The Gospel of Matthew names the habit of doing good in order to be seen. Matthew 6:1: "Beware of practicing your righteousness before others in order to be seen by them, for then you have no reward from your Father in heaven."

John 12:43 says of leaders who would not confess their belief: "they loved human glory more than the glory that comes from God." Galatians 1:10 asks: "Am I now seeking human approval or God's approval? Or am I trying to please people?"

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. These verses are about righteousness and faith, and nothing here says that wanting any approval is wrong.

Buddhism

In Buddhist texts the nearest term is conceit, the sense of "I am". In SN 22.89 the mendicant Khemaka says he does not regard anything in the five grasping aggregates as self, and adds: "I'm not rid of the conceit 'I am'. But I don't regard anything as 'I am this'."

AN 8.6 names the pull of approval among the eight worldly conditions: "Gain and loss, fame and disgrace, blame and praise, pleasure and pain." And of the ordinary person: "They favor praise and oppose blame."

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. SN 22.89 is about a subtle sense of self and AN 8.6 is about eight conditions, so reading them as the same thing is ours.

Philosophical

Aristotle asks whether honour could be the good for a person and decides it is too thin. Nicomachean Ethics 1.5: "it seems too superficial to be what we are looking for, since it is thought to depend on those who bestow honour rather than on him who receives it, but the good we divine to be something proper to a man and not easily taken from him."

For Aristotle the point is where the good lives: in us or in others' hands. If your worth depends on others' response, someone else holds it.

This is related to the need for validation and not identical to it. Aristotle is asking about the highest good for a person, and does not describe a habit of seeking approval. Applying it so is ours.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.5 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
