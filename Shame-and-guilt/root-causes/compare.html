Modern

The sources read here point to a few causes, and say little about how they work.

  • Early harm. The abstract of the Tangney review lists research on "the link between childhood abuse and later proneness to shame", and on "styles of coping with shame". It does not give the strength of the link or its mechanism.
  • Doing harm. Guilt follows an act: breaking a rule you hold, or hurting someone. That is a standard account and not drawn from the papers.
  • High standards and harsh judging. Many people describe the same pattern as in perfectionism and self-criticism: a standard set too high, then a verdict on the self when it is missed. That is common description, not a finding here.
  • Secrecy. What is hidden tends to grow, as people commonly describe it.

Shame that began in childhood harm is not a personal failing, and a therapist can help with it.

Sources

  • Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek (2007), Annual Review of Psychology 58, 345-372. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070145 (external link)

Read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. The other three points are a standard summary and common description, not tied to a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita shows distress rising from fear of wrongdoing and from attachment. In Arjuna's first speech he says: "Even though they may be aggressors, sin will certainly come upon us if we slay them" (1.36). Before that he says: "my limbs are giving way and my mouth is drying up" (1.28).

The translator's commentary on 1.28 gives a cause: "He was filled with remorse and fearful of performing his duty of fighting this war. The cause for these sentiments was his attachment towards his bodily relatives."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Arjuna dreads a wrong he has not yet done, and the cause named is attachment and forgetting that he "was not just the body", in the commentary's words. Krishna's answer comes in the chapters after and was not read here. Reading Arjuna's anguish as a root of guilt is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the root of grief in not knowing the Self. In Chandogya 7.1 Narada comes to Sanatkumara knowing the Vedas and many sciences and says: "with all this I know the Mantras only, the sacred books, I do not know the Self. I have heard from men like you, that he who knows the Self overcomes grief. I am in grief."

Sanatkumara answers: "Whatever you have read, is only a name."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Narada's grief is not named as guilt or shame, and the root given is not knowing the Self. Reading it as a source of those feelings is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

In Genesis 3 the hiding is followed by passing the blame. God asks: "Who told you that you were naked? Have you eaten from the tree of which I commanded you not to eat?" The man says: "The woman whom you gave to be with me, she gave me fruit from the tree, and I ate." The woman says: "The serpent tricked me, and I ate."

The text shows fear, hiding and blame running together after a wrong.

1 John 3:19-20 speaks of the heart's own verdict: "whenever our hearts condemn us, for God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to it. The causes in these verses are an act and the fear that follows it. The sentence "this is how guilt and shame begin" is ours, since the passages do not say it. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts trace remorse to the deed, and the deed to heedlessness. Dhp 15: "Here they grieve, hereafter they grieve, an evildoer grieves in both places. They grieve and fret, seeing their own corrupt deeds."

In MN 86 the former bandit Aṅgulimāla says: "He who once was heedless, but turned to heedfulness, shines upon this world, like the moon freed from clouds."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Dhp 15 speaks of grief at one's own deeds, and the Aṅgulimāla verse sets heedlessness before and heedfulness after, and neither describes shame as a feeling about the self. Reading them as roots of those feelings is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

The Stoics place the root in our judgments. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."

He goes on: "When therefore we are hindered, or disturbed, or grieved, let us never attribute it to others, but to ourselves; that is, to our own principles."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. The passage is about being disturbed in general. Applying it to shame is ours, and it should not be read as saying that remorse for real harm is a mistake in judgment. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.
