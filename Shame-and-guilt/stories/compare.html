Shame and guilt

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how shame and guilt work and how people ease them.

Rohan's story

At a dinner, Rohan made a joke about his friend Leela's failed business, and the table laughed. Leela went quiet. For the next week he rehearsed it each night and did not answer her messages. The sentence he kept telling himself was "I am a terrible friend".

What helped:

  1. He split the sentence. "I said something cruel" was about what he did, and could be dealt with. "I am terrible" was a verdict, and it kept him hiding.
  2. He repaired what he could. He rang Leela, said what he had said and that it was unkind, and did not ask her to say it was fine.
  3. He told someone. His brother listened and did not rush to excuse him.
  4. He spoke to himself as to a friend. He asked what he would say to a friend who had done the same.
  5. He let it do its work. The guilt had pointed him to Leela. Once he had called, he let it go.
  6. He asked for more help. The "terrible person" thought stayed for weeks, and he spoke to a counsellor.

Leela took time to answer, and the friendship did not snap back at once.

What it shows: Rohan did not need to feel nothing. Separating the deed from the verdict let him act, and the repair did more than the hiding had.

Hinduism

Arjuna on the battlefield

In Gita 1.28-30 Arjuna sees his own kinsmen arrayed for battle. "O Krishna, seeing my own kinsmen arrayed for battle here and intent on killing each other, my limbs are giving way and my mouth is drying up." "My whole body shudders; my hair is standing on end. My bow, the Gāṇḍīv, is slipping from my hand, and my skin is burning all over."

He reasons that "sin will certainly come upon us if we slay them" (1.36). The teaching of the rest of the Gita answers this distress, and the last verse read here is 18.66: "I shall liberate you from all sinful reactions; do not fear."

What it shows: Arjuna is overwhelmed by the thought of a wrong not yet done, and what he is given is not a rule but a teaching. This is a moment in a dialogue and not a tale of shame, and linking it to shame and guilt is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

Narada and Sanatkumara

In Chandogya 7.1 Narada comes to Sanatkumara and says: "Teach me, Sir!" Sanatkumara asks what he already knows. Narada lists the four Vedas and many sciences, then says: "with all this I know the Mantras only, the sacred books, I do not know the Self. I have heard from men like you, that he who knows the Self overcomes grief. I am in grief. Do, Sir, help me over this grief of mine."

Sanatkumara's first reply is: "Whatever you have read, is only a name." A long teaching follows in the chapter.

What it shows: Narada does not cover his grief with what he knows. He names it and asks for help. This is a moment in a dialogue and not a tale of shame or guilt, and linking it to them is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

The son who came to his senses

In Luke 15 a younger son takes his share of the wealth, travels to "a distant region" and "squandered his wealth in dissolute living." A famine comes, and he feeds pigs and "would gladly have filled his stomach with the pods that the pigs were eating".

Then: "when he came to his senses he said, 'How many of my father's hired hands have bread enough and to spare, but here I am dying of hunger! I will get up and go to my father, and I will say to him, "Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; I am no longer worthy to be called your son."'"

"While he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him." The father orders a robe, a ring and sandals, and says: "this son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found!"

What it shows: The son's sense of unworthiness leads him to go home and say what he did, and the father does not wait for the speech to be finished. The story is about a father's welcome and not about shame as such; the link is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

Aṅgulimāla

In MN 86 Aṅgulimāla, a former bandit who has become a monk, says of himself: "I have intentionally killed many living creatures." He is sent to a woman in a difficult labour with words that begin "Ever since I was born in the noble birth" and end: "By this truth, may both you and your baby be safe." She and the baby are safe.

Later someone throws a stone, a stick and gravel at him. he goes to the Buddha "with cracked head, bleeding, his bowl broken, and his outer robe torn". The Buddha says: "Endure it, brahmin! Endure it, brahmin! You're experiencing in this very life the result of deeds that might have caused you to be tormented in hell for many years".

He then speaks verses: "I used to be a bandit, the notorious Aṅgulimāla." "My name is 'Harmless', though I used to be harmful." And: "Someone whose bad deed is supplanted by the good, shines upon this world, like the moon freed from clouds."

What it shows: Aṅgulimāla does not deny what he did, and does not stay defined by it. The sutta is about a murderer who becomes a monk and is not a tale of shame as such; the link is ours. It involves killing and harm to a body, and may be hard to read. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Alcibiades and Socrates

In Plato's Symposium Alcibiades praises Socrates in a speech. He says that Socrates "makes me confess that I ought not to live as I do, neglecting the wants of my own soul, and busying myself with the concerns of the Athenians; therefore I hold my ears and tear myself away from him."

Then: "he is the only person who ever made me ashamed, which you might think not to be in my nature". He cannot answer Socrates, and "when I leave his presence the love of popularity gets the better of me. And therefore I run away and fly from him, and when I see him I am ashamed of what I have confessed to him."

What it shows: Alcibiades' shame shows him what he is neglecting, and he runs from it. The scene is a speech of praise and not an account of a remedy, and reading it as a picture of shame is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.
