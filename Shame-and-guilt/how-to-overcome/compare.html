Inner battle

Shame and guilt

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes shame and guilt and tests no remedy. The rest is common advice.

  1. Say which it is. Try the sentence "I did something I regret" before "I am bad". The first points at what can be repaired. That is common advice.
  2. Repair what you can. If you hurt someone, say plainly what you did and apologise without excuses, where it is safe to. That is common advice.
  3. Tell someone you trust. Shame tends to want hiding, and saying it aloud to a kind person often loosens it. That is common advice.
  4. Speak to yourself as to a friend. A harsh verdict on yourself repairs nothing; see self-criticism. That is common advice.
  5. Let guilt do its job, then put it down. Once you have understood and repaired what you can, the feeling has done its work. That is common advice.
  6. Get help. If shame is heavy or long-lasting, is tied to abuse, or comes with thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor or therapist, and in an emergency contact your local emergency services.

Sources

  • Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek (2007), Annual Review of Psychology 58, 345-372. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070145 (external link)

Read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full; it says nothing of remedies. Every step is common advice, not tied to a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita offers comfort for those who feel they have fallen. Gita 9.30-31: "Even if the vilest sinners worship Me with exclusive devotion, they are to be considered righteous because they have made the proper resolve. Quickly they become virtuous, and attain lasting peace."

4.36: "Even those who are considered the most immoral of all sinners can cross over this ocean of material existence by seating themselves in the boat of divine knowledge."

18.66: "Abandon all varieties of dharmas and simply surrender unto Me alone. I shall liberate you from all sinful reactions; do not fear."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. These verses are about devotion and knowledge and not about the feeling of shame, and they do not speak of making amends to people harmed. Reading them as comfort for guilt is ours. How the three verses fit with each other was not checked. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads answer with knowledge, and not with a technique. Taittiriya 2.9: "He who thus knows these two (good and bad), frees himself. He who knows both, frees himself."

In Chandogya 7.1.3 Narada asks: "Do, Sir, help me over this grief of mine." The first reply is: "Whatever you have read, is only a name."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Neither passage turns into steps, and the teaching that follows Sanatkumara's reply was not read here. The site makes no claim about how to practise it, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Christianity

The Christian texts counsel confession and receiving forgiveness.

  1. Name it. Psalm 51:3: "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me."
  2. Ask for a fresh start. Psalm 51:10: "Create in me a clean heart, O God, and put a new and right spirit within me."
  3. Confess. 1 John 1:9: "If we confess our sins, he who is faithful and just will forgive us our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness."
  4. Hear the verdict that follows. Romans 8:1: "Therefore there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."
  5. Answer a heart that condemns. 1 John 3:19-20: "whenever our hearts condemn us, for God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to it. These verses are about sin and forgiveness before God, and they do not describe amends to people harmed, which other texts of the tradition may discuss. Reading them as a way through shame and guilt is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts counsel recognising a mistake, and turning to good.

  1. Recognise it. AN 2.21: "There are two who are astute. What two? One who recognizes when they've made a mistake. And one who properly accepts the confession of someone who's made a mistake."
  2. Turn to heedfulness. Dhp 173: "Someone whose bad deed is supplanted by the good, shines upon this world, like the moon freed from clouds."
  3. Build conduct that leaves no regret. AN 10.1: "having no regrets is the goal and benefit of skillful ethics."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. AN 2.21 is about the wise and the foolish and says nothing of making amends, and Dhp 173 is about good done after a bad deed and does not describe a feeling. Reading them as a way through shame and guilt is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Philosophical

The philosophers' counsel is to look at your judgments, and to be accurate about blame.

  1. Check the judgment. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."
  2. Be accurate about blame. Same section: "An uninstructed person will lay the fault of his own bad condition upon others. Someone just starting instruction will lay the fault on himself. Some who is perfectly instructed will place blame neither on others nor on himself."
  3. Aim at not needing the feeling. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.9: "the good man will never voluntarily do bad actions."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Epictetus is writing about being disturbed in general, and Aristotle is saying shame is not a virtue, so neither gives a method for the feeling. Step 3 is a standard that does not help once something has gone wrong, and it should be read beside Self-criticism. Reading these as a way through shame and guilt is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion (external link), section 5 (Elizabeth Carter's translation); Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)

The quotations were read on those pages on 2026-11-15. The Aristotle sentence is cut from a longer one, and the Enchiridion quotation in step 2 joins three sentences. The caution is ours.
