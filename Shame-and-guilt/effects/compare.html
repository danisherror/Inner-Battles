Inner battle

Shame and guilt

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes costs and benefits, and mostly for shame. In two studies of undergraduates, Tangney, Wagner and Gramzow report that "Shame-proneness was strongly related to psychological maladjustment in general" and that "shame accounted for substantial variance in depression, above and beyond attributional style." Guilt-proneness was "only moderately related to psychopathology", and the correlations "were ascribable entirely to the shared variance between shame and guilt." These are correlations from questionnaires.

The review by Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek weighs "the relative advantages and disadvantages" of the two emotions, and the abstract does not give its conclusions.

Common effects, as people describe them and not findings from these papers: hiding from people, snapping at them, over-apologising, a tight chest, and sometimes, with guilt, repair and a changed course.

Heavy shame can feed low mood. If shame is heavy or long-lasting, is tied to abuse, or comes with thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor or therapist, and in an emergency contact your local emergency services.

Sources

  • Tangney, Wagner and Gramzow (1992), Journal of Abnormal Psychology 101(3), 469-478. doi:10.1037/0021-843X.101.3.469 (external link)
  • Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek (2007), Annual Review of Psychology 58, 345-372. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070145 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. The list of common effects is a standard summary and not drawn from a source.

Hinduism

The Gita shows what distress does to a body. Arjuna says: "My whole body shudders; my hair is standing on end. My bow, the Gāṇḍīv, is slipping from my hand, and my skin is burning all over. My mind is in quandary and whirling in confusion; I am unable to hold myself steady any longer."

The same chapter lists the other side. Among the divine qualities of 16.1-3 is hrīḥ, translated "modesty", which the commentary explains as "sense of guilt in performing actions contrary to the injunctions of scriptures and society". An inner check is counted as a gain.

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Arjuna's distress is about killing his kinsmen, and the commentary calls it remorse and fear. Reading it as an effect of guilt is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads name self-torment as a cost, and freedom from it as a result of knowledge. Taittiriya 2.9: "He does not distress himself with the thought, Why did I not do what is good? Why did I do what is bad?" The sentence that follows says: "He who knows both, frees himself."

In Chandogya 7.1.3 Narada puts the cost plainly: "I am in grief. Do, Sir, help me over this grief of mine."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. The Taittiriya verse is about the knower, and Narada's grief has no named cause. Reading these as effects of guilt or shame is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Taittiriya Upanishad (external link), Second Valli, Ninth Anuvaka; Chandogya Upanishad, Seventh Prapathaka (external link) (Max Müller's translations)

The quotations were read on those pages on 2026-11-15. The Narada quotation joins two sentences of 7.1.3. Müller's notes and the Sanskrit were not examined.

Christianity

Psalm 32 describes what hidden guilt does to a body: "While I kept silent, my body wasted away through my groaning all day long. For day and night your hand was heavy upon me; my strength was dried up as by the heat of summer."

Then it turns: "Then I acknowledged my sin to you, and I did not hide my iniquity; I said, 'I will confess my transgressions to the Lord,' and you forgave the guilt of my sin."

Genesis 3:8 shows the hiding that comes with it: the man and woman "hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God".

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. The Psalm connects silence with wasting and confession with forgiveness. It does not claim that every ache of conscience has this cause, and a footnote on the page says the Hebrew of verse 4 is uncertain. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts set grief and joy side by side. Dhp 15-16: "Here they grieve, hereafter they grieve, an evildoer grieves in both places." and "Here they rejoice, hereafter they rejoice, one who does good rejoices in both places."

AN 10.1 gives a chain of effects that starts with conduct: "having no regrets is the goal and benefit of skillful ethics" and "Joy is the goal and benefit of having no regrets."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Dhp 15 is about the grief of an evildoer, and AN 10.1 is about the benefit of ethical conduct, one step of which is having no regrets. Neither describes shame as a feeling about the self, and reading them as effects of guilt is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Dhp 1-20 (external link), AN 10.1 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-15. The Dhp quotations join lines, and the rest of the chain in AN 10.1 (rapture, tranquility and on to knowledge and vision of freedom) was left out. The Pali words were not examined.

Philosophical

Aristotle describes what shame does. It "produces an effect similar to that produced by fear of danger; for people who feel disgraced blush, and those who fear death turn pale." It is a bodily condition, and so "characteristic of feeling rather than of a state of character."

He also names a use: "young people should be prone to the feeling of shame because they live by feeling and therefore commit many errors, but are restrained by shame". And a limit: "the sense of disgrace is not even characteristic of a good man, since it is consequent on bad actions".

This is related to shame as people use the word now and not identical to it. Aristotle is describing disgrace before others, not shame as a belief about the self, and he says nothing of long-term effects. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book IV (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation), 4.9

The quotations were read on that page on 2026-11-15. The first quotation joins two sentences, and the last is shortened at its end.
