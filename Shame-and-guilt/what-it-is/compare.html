Inner battle

Shame and guilt

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Guilt is the painful sense of having done something wrong. Shame is the painful sense of being wrong, of being bad or small as a person. A common way to put it is that guilt says "I did something bad" and shame says "I am bad". That is a standard summary and not a quotation from the papers below.

Researchers treat them as two "moral emotions". Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek's review covers "the distinction between shame and guilt, and the relative advantages and disadvantages of these two moral emotions." In two studies of undergraduates, Tangney, Wagner and Gramzow found that "Shame-proneness was strongly related to psychological maladjustment in general", while "Guilt-proneness was only moderately related to psychopathology." Those are correlations from questionnaires and do not show that shame causes distress.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: wanting to hide or disappear, replaying a moment, going quiet around the person involved. Guilt often points toward repair, and it can be a fitting response to real harm. It overlaps with self-criticism, where the harsh voice turns a deed into a verdict on the person.

Neither is a diagnosis. If shame is heavy or long-lasting, is tied to abuse, or comes with thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor or therapist, and in an emergency contact your local emergency services.

Sources

  • Tangney, Stuewig and Mashek (2007), "Moral emotions and moral behavior," Annual Review of Psychology 58, 345-372. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.56.091103.070145 (external link)
  • Tangney, Wagner and Gramzow (1992), "Proneness to shame, proneness to guilt, and psychopathology," Journal of Abnormal Psychology 101(3), 469-478. doi:10.1037/0021-843X.101.3.469 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The "I did / I am" summary and the list of everyday signs are a standard summary and not drawn from a source; the abstract of the review says it covers the distinction but does not state it in those words.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for the pair of shame and guilt, but it names an inner check on wrongdoing as a virtue. Gita 16.1-3 lists qualities of "those endowed with a divine nature", among them "fearlessness", "compassion toward all living beings", "forgiveness" and "modesty".

The Sanskrit word behind "modesty" is hrīḥ, and the translator's commentary explains it as a "sense of guilt in performing actions contrary to the injunctions of scriptures and society." It says the saintly nature "is imbued with a ruthless inner conscience". In this reading a sense of having done wrong is counted among the good qualities and not as a flaw.

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. The verse itself says "modesty", and the meaning of guilt comes from the commentary. It speaks of a check before and during action and not of the long ache after a wrong, and it says nothing of judging oneself to be bad. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads speak of the same distress without using a word for shame. Taittiriya Upanishad 2.9 says of the one who knows the bliss of Brahman: "He does not distress himself with the thought, Why did I not do what is good? Why did I do what is bad? He who thus knows these two (good and bad), frees himself."

The verse names the habit of tormenting oneself over the good left undone and the bad done, and says that knowledge frees a person from it.

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. The verse describes a person who has already realised Brahman, and it does not say that wrongs do not matter or what to do about harm caused. How the knowledge acts on the deeds is not explained in the passage read. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

Christianity has words for both. Guilt is the awareness of a wrong done, and the Bible also shows the urge to hide. In Genesis 3 the man and woman "knew that they were naked", and "hid themselves from the presence of the Lord God among the trees of the garden." Asked where he is, the man says: "I heard the sound of you in the garden, and I was afraid, because I was naked, and I hid myself."

Psalm 51:3 shows the other side: "For I know my transgressions, and my sin is ever before me." In verse 4 the speaker says: "Against you, you alone, have I sinned and done what is evil in your sight."

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to it. Reading Genesis 3:7-10 as an account of shame, and Psalm 51 as guilt, is ours. The verses read do not use the words "shame" or "guilt" of the man and woman, and the Psalm is about sin before God and not a general feeling. What both show is a person who knows something is wrong and is aware of being seen. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts treat a sense of wrongdoing as a protection. AN 2.9: "These two bright things, mendicants, protect the world. What two? Conscience and prudence." Without them, "the world would become dissolute, like goats and sheep, chickens and pigs, and hounds and jackals."

The sutta says these two qualities are bright, and that without them there would be no recognition of the status of mother, aunts, or wives and partners of tutors and respected people.

This is related to shame and guilt and not identical to them. Bhikkhu Sujato renders the pair as "conscience and prudence", and the root text reads "Hirī ca ottappañca". They are commonly explained as a sense of moral shame and a wariness of doing wrong, but that explanation was not checked here. Nothing in the passage read speaks of shame as a verdict on oneself. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Aristotle gives shame a place, and says what it is not. In the Nicomachean Ethics (4.9): "Shame should not be described as a virtue; for it is more like a feeling than a state of character. It is defined, at any rate, as a kind of fear of dishonour, and produces an effect similar to that produced by fear of danger; for people who feel disgraced blush, and those who fear death turn pale."

For him it is a bodily feeling about disgrace in the eyes of others, and not a settled quality of character.

This is related to shame as people use the word now and not identical to it. The translator's "shame", "disgrace" and "dishonour" point at how one appears to others, and the passage says nothing of shame as a belief that one is bad. The Greek was not examined. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and shame that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.
