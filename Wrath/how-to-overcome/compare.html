Deadly sin

Wrath

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

The aim is not to never feel anger, but to respond without being run by it.

  1. Spot the early signs: tight jaw, heat, faster heartbeat.
  2. Pause. Step away, breathe slowly (longer out-breath), or count to ten. Wait until the peak has passed before you speak.
  3. Reappraise. Ask, "Is there another way to read this?" Research finds that reframing is more effective than suppressing.
  4. Find the feeling underneath (hurt, fear, shame) and say that instead of attacking.
  5. Say what you need, using "I" statements, calmly, once you are calm.
  6. Don't just vent. Exercise or a walk can help use up arousal. Hitting things tends to keep anger high.
  7. Look after the basics: sleep, food, alcohol, stress.
  8. Get help. Anger-management programmes and therapy work, especially if anger leads to violence or fear at home.

Sources

  • Gross & John (2003), "Individual differences in two emotion regulation processes," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 85(2), 348-362. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.85.2.348 (external link)
  • Bushman (2002), PSPB 28(6), 724-731. doi:10.1177/0146167202289002 (external link)

Steps are general advice, not a tested programme. Citations were from memory and not re-checked. If anger has led to violence, seek help urgently.

Hinduism

Hindu practice answers anger with forgiveness, equanimity and ahimsa.

  • Kshama. Forgiveness is a divine quality (Gita 16.3). The Mahabharata (Udyoga Parva, Vidura's counsel) is often quoted: "conquer anger by freedom from anger" (akrodhena jayet krodham).
  • Equal-mindedness. Gita 12.18-19: equal to friend and foe, in honour and dishonour, in cold and heat.
  • Dear to Krishna. Gita 12.13-14: hating no being, friendly, compassionate, forgiving and content.
  • Ahimsa. Yoga Sutras 2.35: in the presence of one firmly established in non-harm, hostility ceases. Practise non-harm in thought, word and deed.
  • Pratipaksha-bhavana. Yoga Sutras 2.33: when harmful thoughts arise, cultivate the opposite.
  • Mantra, prayer and breath practices to settle the mind.

  • Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Vidura Niti); not linked.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic path to freedom from anger is the training of the self and the vision of unity.

  • Da, da, da. Brihadaranyaka 5.2.3 reports the thunder's three teachings: damyata (control yourselves), datta (give), dayadhvam (be compassionate).
  • Calm, controlled, patient. Brihadaranyaka 4.4.23: shanto danta uparatas titikshuh samahito.
  • See the Self in all. Isha 6: one who sees all beings in the Self does not hate.
  • Hold the reins. Katha 1.3.3-9: let the intellect be the driver and the mind the reins, and guide the senses.
  • Ask for calm. Katha 1.1.10 shows a wish that a father's anger be calmed, a model of asking for peace.
  • Study and meditate. Later Vedanta teaches reflection on unity as a way to weaken the grounds of enmity.

Christianity

Christian practice answers anger with gentleness, forgiveness and love of enemies.

  • Be slow to anger. James 1:19: "quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger."
  • Answer softly. Proverbs 15:1: "A soft answer turns away wrath." Proverbs 16:32: "One who is slow to anger is better than the mighty."
  • Settle things the same day. Ephesians 4:26-27, and Matthew 5:23-24 (be reconciled before bringing your gift).
  • Forgive. Ephesians 4:32 and Colossians 3:13: forgive as you have been forgiven. In the Lord's Prayer: "forgive us our debts, as we also have forgiven our debtors" (Matthew 6:12).
  • Do not repay evil. Romans 12:17-21: "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."
  • Love enemies and pray for them (Matthew 5:44).
  • Confession and prayer, and where needed counsel.

Buddhism

Buddhism has detailed practice for anger.

  • Non-hatred. Dhammapada 5 and 223: conquer anger by non-anger, the bad by good, the miser by giving, the liar by truth.
  • Metta. Loving-kindness practice (Sn 1.8) wishes well for all beings, including those you resent. Start with yourself and a friend, and move toward the difficult person.
  • The saw simile. The Kakacupama Sutta (MN 21) says that even if bandits were sawing you apart limb by limb, anyone who gave way to hatred would not be following the teaching.
  • Five ways to remove resentment. AN 5.161: cultivate loving-kindness, compassion or equanimity toward the person, forget or ignore them, or reflect on karma as one's own.
  • Patience. Khanti is called the highest austerity (Dhammapada 184).
  • Notice and let go. Mindful attention to the feeling in the body without acting on it.

Philosophical

Philosophy offers practices for catching anger early.

  • Delay. Seneca (On Anger 2.29): "The greatest remedy for anger is delay." Wait before you act.
  • Examine the judgment. Epictetus (Enchiridion 20): the insult is in your opinion. Ask whether the claim is true, and if so, why be angry.
  • Evening review. Seneca (On Anger 3.36) describes the custom of the philosopher Sextius, who asked his spirit each night, "What bad habit of yours have you cured to-day? what vice have you checked? in what respect are you better?" Seneca says he follows the practice himself.
  • Assume ignorance. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 2.1 and 11.18): people act from ignorance of good and evil, and we also offend. Remember we are all going to die soon.
  • Aim at mildness. Aristotle (NE 4.5): train toward the right anger at the right time, which takes practice and judgment.
  • Avoid the company of the irritable, and choose calm company (Seneca 3.8).
