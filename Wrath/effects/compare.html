Wrath

Lenses to show

Modern

Unchecked anger costs health, relationships and judgment.

  • Heart and body. A meta-analysis found that outbursts of anger were followed by a short-term rise in the risk of heart attack and stroke, though the absolute risk in any one episode is small.
  • Relationships. Rage, contempt and silent treatment erode trust. Words said in anger are hard to take back.
  • Work and legal. Angry outbursts can end careers and lead to violence or legal trouble.
  • Judgment. Anger narrows attention and makes people more certain and less careful.
  • Venting doesn't help. Experiments suggest that "letting it out" (for example hitting a pillow) tends to keep anger up, not reduce it.
  • Children. Living with frequent rage can harm those around you, children especially.

Sources

  • Mostofsky et al. (2014), "Outbursts of anger as a trigger of acute cardiovascular events," European Heart Journal 35(21), 1404-1410. doi:10.1093/eurheartj/ehu033 (external link)
  • Bushman (2002), "Does venting anger feed or extinguish the flame?", Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 28(6), 724-731. doi:10.1177/0146167202289002 (external link)

Hinduism

The Gita describes how anger undoes a person.

  • Ruin. Gita 2.63: from anger comes delusion, from delusion loss of memory, from loss of memory loss of reason, and from that destruction.
  • Gate to hell. Gita 16.21 calls desire, anger and greed the three gates to self-destruction.
  • Loss of peace. Gita 2.66 says that there is no peace for the unsteady mind.
  • Harm to others. In the Mahabharata, Duryodhana's hatred, and Draupadi's vow of revenge after her humiliation, run through the whole war. The epic treats both the wrong done and the anger it raised as feeding the catastrophe.
  • Freedom in the opposite. Gita 5.23 says the one who can resist the impulse of desire and anger before leaving the body is a yogi and is happy. 5.26 says those free of desire and anger find liberation.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 5.23, 5.26 (external link)

Upanishads

In the Upanishads, anger belongs to the life of separateness, and its cost is sorrow and unrest.

  • Fear and sorrow. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.2 links the sense of a second with fear, and Isha 7 says that for the one who sees unity there is no sorrow, so for the one who does not, there is.
  • Wandering from death to death. Katha 2.1.10 says the one who sees multiplicity here goes from death to death.
  • Broken households. Katha 1.1.10 shows the cost in a family: a son sent away in his father's anger, and the later wish for the father's anger to cool.
  • No peace. Katha 2.2.13 gives peace only to those who perceive the one Self.

Sources

  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (external link) 1.4.2
  • Isha Upanishad (external link) 7
  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.1.10, 2.1.10, 2.2.13

Christianity

Scripture lists the damage anger does.

  • Foolishness. Proverbs 14:29: "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding, but one who has a hasty temper exalts folly." Ecclesiastes 7:9 says anger lodges in the bosom of fools.
  • Strife. Proverbs 15:18: "Those who are hot-tempered stir up strife."
  • Murder in the heart. Matthew 5:21-22 and 1 John 3:15 link hatred with killing.
  • Giving the devil a foothold. Ephesians 4:26-27 warns that nursed anger gives the devil an opportunity.
  • Unrighteousness. James 1:20: human anger does not produce God's righteousness.
  • Cost to leaders. Moses, for striking the rock in anger, is not allowed to enter the promised land (Numbers 20:10-12).

Buddhism

Anger harms the angry person first.

  • Hatred feeds on itself. Dhammapada 5: hatreds never cease through hatred.
  • Burnt by one's own fire. The Buddha's image of "anger has a poisonous root and a sweet tip" (SN 1.71, Sujato's translation) shows that anger can feel good in the moment and bring suffering after.
  • Ugly results. AN 7.64 (Kodhana Sutta) lists seven things that please an enemy about an angry person, including that they become ugly, sleep badly and lose wealth and friends.
  • Karma. Harsh speech and harmful action from anger lead to suffering for oneself and others.
  • Blocks meditation. Ill will is one of the five hindrances to concentration.

Sources

  • Dhammapada 1-20 (external link), verse 5
  • SN 1.71, Kodhavagga/Anger (external link)
  • AN 7.64, Kodhana Sutta (external link)

Philosophical

Philosophers describe anger as a kind of temporary madness.

  • Loss of reason. Seneca (On Anger 1.1) calls anger "a short madness" (Stewart translation; he attributes the saying to "some of the wisest of men"), and says it makes people deaf to counsel and blind to what is useful.
  • Harm without gain. Seneca (2.12) argues that anger is not useful even in war, because it gets in the way of calm judgment.
  • Cruelty. Plutarch (On the Control of Anger) describes how anger leads people into acts they regret.
  • Self-injury. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 11.18) notes that the anger and grief that follow are worse than the thing that caused them.
  • Disrespect for persons. Kant treats vengefulness as a violation of the respect owed to others.
