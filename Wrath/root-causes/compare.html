Deadly sin

Wrath

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Anger usually comes from how we read a situation, plus our state at the time.

  • Perceived wrong or threat. Anger tends to follow the thought "that was unfair" or "they disrespected me."
  • Blocked goals and stress. Tiredness, hunger, pain, noise, heat and overload lower the threshold.
  • Hurt underneath. Anger often covers fear, shame, grief or feeling powerless, which are harder to admit.
  • Rumination. Replaying a grievance keeps the feeling alive long after the event.
  • Learned patterns. Many people copy the way anger was handled around them as children.
  • Sense of entitlement and rigid "shoulds." Rules such as "people must treat me right" produce frequent anger.
  • Substances such as alcohol reduce self-control.

Sources

  • Averill (1983), American Psychologist 38(11), 1145-1160. doi:10.1037/0003-066X.38.11.1145 (external link)

Hinduism

Hindu thought traces anger to blocked desire and to the ego that feels wronged.

  • The chain. Gita 2.62-63: dwelling on objects leads to attachment, attachment to desire, and "from desire comes anger" (kamat krodho 'bhijayate). Anger leads to delusion, loss of memory, loss of reason and ruin.
  • Rajas. Gita 3.37 says that desire and anger are born of rajas.
  • Ego and "mine." Gita 16.18 lists ahamkara (ego), power, arrogance, desire and anger together, as the traits that lead people to hate the Divine in themselves and others.
  • Ignorance. Gita 16.4 ties anger to ajnana (ignorance).
  • Karma and fate. Gita 18.60 says that one is bound by action born of one's own nature, and so acts even against one's wish.

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the root in separateness and unruly senses and mind.

  • A second. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.2-3 says fear arose because there was a second. Where there is another, there can be conflict.
  • Unruly horses. Katha 1.3.3-6 describes the senses as horses, which run wild when the driver (the intellect) is untrained and the reins (the mind) slack.
  • Outward gaze. Katha 2.1.1 says the senses were turned outward, so people react to what happens outside and miss what is within.
  • Ignorance of unity. Isha 6-7: the one who sees all as the Self has no hatred, so the one who does not see this is open to it.

Christianity

Christian writers find anger's roots in pride, wounded self-love and unforgiveness.

  • From pride. Gregory the Great (Moralia 31.45) lists anger as a child of pride. If I must be honoured, any slight enrages me.
  • Desire for vengeance. Aquinas (ST II-II q.158) describes anger as a desire for retribution that goes beyond reason.
  • Inner life. James 4:1-2 asks, "Those conflicts and disputes among you, where do they come from? Do they not come from your cravings that are at war within you?"
  • Fall. Cain's anger follows the rejection of his offering (Genesis 4:5-7), and God warns him that "sin is lurking at the door."
  • Unforgiveness. The parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21-35) shows resentment as the refusal to give what one has received.

Buddhism

Buddhism traces anger to aversion, attachment to a self and wrong view.

  • Aversion. Dosa is the root: pushing away what is unpleasant. Craving and aversion are two sides of the same grip (SN 56.11).
  • Self-view. If there is a "me" to defend, every insult is an attack on it. SN 22.59 teaches that the aggregates are not self.
  • Dwelling on grievances. Dhammapada 3-4 describes thoughts of "He abused me, he struck me" (Buddharakkhita translation), which keep hatred alive.
  • Unwise attention. The suttas say that attending to the repulsive sign feeds ill will, as with the attractive sign for sensual desire (SN 46.51).
  • Conditions of the body and mind. Pain, tiredness and hunger make irritation easier (MN 2 notes that discomfort and hardship are to be endured).

Philosophical

Philosophers see anger as arising from judgment about a wrong, plus bodily and social conditions.

  • A judgment of slight. Aristotle (Rhetoric 2.2) says anger follows an apparent slight, belittlement or insult, and is mixed with the pleasure of hoped-for revenge.
  • False opinion. The Stoics held that anger rests on a judgment that one has been wronged and that revenge is appropriate. Epictetus (Enchiridion 20) says it is not the person who insults you that harms you, but your belief that you are harmed.
  • Weak character. Seneca (On Anger 1.20 and 2.19) says anger is more common in the soft, the sick and the over-indulged.
  • Spirited part unruled. Plato (Republic 4, 439e-441c) shows anger as the spirited part, which needs to be trained by reason.
  • Self-importance. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 11.18) lists reminders that others act from ignorance, and that we too offend.
