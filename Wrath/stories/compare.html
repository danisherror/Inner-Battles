Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how anger works and how people step out of it.

Imran's story

Imran, 42, ran a warehouse and was known as a man you didn't cross. A late truck, a mistake on a pick list, a driver cutting him off: each one set off a blast. At home it was quieter but worse. He would go silent for days if his wife disagreed with him, and he shouted at his 12-year-old son over homework.

One evening he slammed his fist on the table so hard that the glasses jumped. His son flinched and ran from the room. Imran saw the fear on the boy's face, and recognised it, because it was how he used to look at his own father.

The turning point was not the fear itself but his son's question later: "Are you always going to be angry?"

What helped:

  1. He learned his early signs: a hot face and clenched hands.
  2. He built a pause. He walked out of the room and breathed slowly for a few minutes before answering.
  3. He asked "what's under this?" and found a lot of fear of failing and of losing control.
  4. He apologised properly to his son, without excuses, and said what he'd do next time.
  5. He joined an anger-management group and cut back on drinking.
  6. He used exercise, a daily walk and later swimming, to burn off stress.

He still got angry. But the blasts became rarer and shorter, and he repaired them faster.

What it shows: Anger is often covering fear or hurt. A pause, honest naming and repair made more difference than trying to never feel it.

Hinduism

Vishvamitra's anger

In the Ramayana (Bala Kanda), Vishvamitra begins as a king. Out hunting with his army, he comes to the hermitage of the sage Vasishtha, who feeds the whole army with the help of his wish-granting cow, Shabala. Vishvamitra, dazzled, asks for the cow. Vasishtha refuses, and Vishvamitra tries to take her by force.

His army is destroyed by beings that Shabala brings forth, and his hundred sons are burnt by Vasishtha's power. Humbled, Vishvamitra concludes that a kshatriya's strength is nothing against spiritual power, gives the kingdom to his one surviving son and goes to do austerities.

Over a long time he gains great power, but he is also easily angered. In the story of Trishanku he curses, and at one point he curses Menaka's enticement of him. Each time he spends the merit he has gathered.

In the end, the tradition says, he overcomes his anger. He is recognised by Vasishtha as a Brahmarshi, the highest rank of sage.

What it shows: Power gained by austerity can be lost by anger. The story presents mastery of anger as the final step in Vishvamitra's journey, more than the power itself.

Sources

  • Valmiki Ramayana, Bala Kanda (external link), the Vishvamitra and Vasishtha episodes (overview)

Upanishads

The thunder's three syllables

Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 5.2 tells of Prajapati, the creator, and his three groups of children: gods (devas), humans and demons (asuras). Each lived for a time as a student of Prajapati. When they finished, the gods asked him, "Speak to us, sir." He answered with one syllable: "Da. Have you understood?"

The gods said, "We have understood: you said damyata, control yourselves." "Yes," said Prajapati, "you have understood."

Then the humans asked, and got the same Da. They said, "You said datta, give." "Yes."

Then the demons asked, and heard Da again. They said, "You said dayadhvam, be compassionate." "Yes, you have understood."

The text adds that the divine voice of thunder still repeats the same: Da, da, da. Therefore one should practise these three: self-control, giving and compassion.

What it shows: The tradition reads each group's lack in the answer: gods lack restraint, humans lack generosity and demons lack compassion. The last is the one aimed at cruelty and rage.

Sources

  • Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (external link) 5.2.1-3

Christianity

Moses at the rock

Numbers 20:1-13 tells of the people of Israel in the wilderness of Zin. There was no water, and the people quarrelled with Moses and Aaron, saying they had been brought out only to die. Moses and Aaron went to the tent of meeting and fell on their faces.

God told Moses to take his staff, gather the people, and speak to the rock before their eyes so that it would give water.

Moses took the staff and gathered the community. Then he said, "Listen, you rebels, shall we bring water for you out of this rock?" and struck the rock twice with his staff. Water came out in abundance, and the people and animals drank.

But God said to Moses and Aaron, "Because you did not trust in me, to show my holiness before the eyes of the Israelites, therefore you shall not bring this assembly into the land that I have given them."

Moses had led the people for forty years. He saw the promised land from a mountain but did not enter it (Deuteronomy 34:1-4).

What it shows: After decades of patience, one moment of angry impatience cost Moses what he had worked for. The story shows how even a great leader's anger can undo good work.

Buddhism

The gift that was not accepted

In the Akkosa Sutta (SN 7.2), the brahmin Bharadvaja "the Rude" hears that a brahmin of his clan has left home to follow the Buddha. Angry and displeased, he goes to the Buddha and abuses him with harsh and rude words.

The Buddha waits until he has finished, then asks calmly: "What do you think, brahmin? Do friends and colleagues, relatives and kin, and guests still come to visit you?" "Sometimes they do." "Do you then serve them with fresh and cooked foods and savories?" "Sometimes I do." "But if they don't accept it, brahmin, who does it belong to?" "In that case it still belongs to me."

"In the same way, brahmin," says the Buddha, "when you abuse, harass, and attack us who do not abuse, harass, and attack, we don't accept it. It still belongs to you, brahmin, it still belongs to you!"

He adds that someone who answers abuse with abuse is said to "eat the food and have a reaction to it," and that "when you don't get angry at an angry person you win a battle hard to win" (Sujato's translation).

Bharadvaja goes for refuge and asks for ordination. The sutta says that not long after, living alone and diligent, he became one of the perfected (an arahant).

What it shows: The Buddha does not answer anger with more anger, and does not suppress it either. He shows that an insult only lands if it is accepted.

Philosophical

Plato and the raised hand

Seneca, in On Anger (3.12), tells of Plato, who found that one of his slaves had done something wrong, and was angry. He raised his hand to strike, and then stopped, hand still in the air.

A friend came in and asked what he was doing. Plato answered: "I am making an angry man expiate his crime." Seneca says he kept the posture of one about to give way to passion, as if struck by how degrading it was for a philosopher.

On another occasion, being angry at a fault, Plato said, "Speusippus, will you please to correct that slave with stripes; for I am in a rage." He would not strike the slave himself: "I should beat him more than I ought," he said, and he would not let the slave fall "into the power of one who is not in his own power."

Seneca asks whether anyone should give the power of revenge to an angry man when Plato himself gave up that right. The story, in his hands, is about not acting while angry.

What it shows: The moment between anger and action is where freedom lies. Plato's pause made the difference.

Sources

  • Seneca, On Anger (external link) 3.12

Retold and shortened; the quoted words and the Speusippus detail were checked in the Wikisource translation (Stewart) of On Anger 3.12. Seneca reports the incident, and it may be an anecdote, not a verified event.
