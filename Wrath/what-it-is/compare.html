Deadly sin

Wrath

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Wrath is uncontrolled anger: anger that turns into rage, resentment or the wish to get even. Anger itself is a normal emotion and can be useful, because it signals that something is wrong or unfair. The sin is anger that is out of proportion, holds on for too long, or leads to cruelty.

Psychologists separate three things:

  • Anger, the feeling.
  • Hostility, a settled attitude of ill will and suspicion.
  • Aggression, behaviour meant to harm.

Wrath covers the second and third, and the first when it takes charge. Common signs are shouting or going cold and silent, replaying grievances, wanting someone to suffer, and saying things you later regret.

Hinduism

The Sanskrit word is krodha, anger, one of the six inner enemies (shadripu). It is closely tied to desire (kama): anger arises when desire is blocked.

In the Gita, 3.37 names desire and anger, born of rajas, as the all-devouring enemy. In 16.21 they are two of the three gates to ruin. In 16.4, anger is listed among the traits of the asuri (demonic) nature. In contrast, the divine qualities of 16.1-3 include akrodha (freedom from anger), kshama (forgiveness), shanti (peace) and ahimsa (non-harm).

The Yoga Sutras (2.35) add that where ahimsa is firmly established, hostility ceases in its presence.

Upanishads

The Upanishads rarely name anger directly. They describe the calm that is its opposite, and treat harshness as a sign of ignorance.

  • Brihadaranyaka 4.4.23 describes the knower as shanta (calm), danta (self-controlled), uparata (withdrawn from distraction), titikshu (patient with hardship) and samahita (collected).
  • Isha 6 says that the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, does not hate (na vijugupsate).
  • Brihadaranyaka 5.2.3 reports the thunder's advice to humans, gods and demons: da, da, da, which is read as self-control, giving and compassion.
  • Katha 1.1.10 shows anger inside a family: Nachiketa asks that his father, who is angry at him, may calm down and be free of anger (vita-manyu).

This is related to wrath and not identical to it: the texts describe calm and self-control and treat harshness as ignorance, rather than naming a sin of anger.

Christianity

Christian tradition calls it wrath or anger (Latin ira): anger that seeks vengeance or turns to hatred. The Catechism says anger is a desire for revenge, and that it is gravely wrong to wish to harm one who must be punished, and a sin to desire to see a neighbour suffer (CCC 2302).

The tradition distinguishes righteous anger at injustice from sinful anger. Ephesians 4:26 says, "Be angry but do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger." James 1:19-20 says to be "slow to anger, for your anger does not produce God's righteousness." In Matthew 5:21-22, Jesus links anger with murder at the level of the heart.

Aquinas (ST II-II q.158) argues that anger is sinful when it goes against reason, in measure or in aim, and not when it is a proportionate response to wrong.

Buddhism

The Pali word is dosa, hatred or aversion, one of the three unwholesome roots with greed (lobha) and delusion (moha). Its forms include anger (kodha), ill will (vyapada), resentment (upanaha) and cruelty.

The opening verses of the Dhammapada (3-5) describe people who keep thinking "He abused me, he struck me, he overpowered me, he robbed me" and so do not still their hatred. Verse 5 says: "Hatred is never appeased by hatred in this world. By non-hatred alone is hatred appeased. This is a law eternal" (Buddharakkhita translation).

Ill will is also one of the five hindrances to meditation. Anger is not treated as a wrong to be hidden. It is to be seen clearly and let go.

Sources

  • Dhammapada 1-20 (chapter 1) (external link), verses 3-5

Philosophical

Philosophers take anger seriously, and disagree about whether it can ever be good.

Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics (4.5) the virtue is mildness (praotes): being angry at the right things, with the right people, in the right way and for the right length of time. The vices are being too angry (irascibility) and never getting angry. In the Rhetoric (2.2) anger is a desire for revenge after an apparent slight.

Seneca. In On Anger (1.1) he writes of anger as the passion that is, "above all others, hideous and wild" (Stewart translation), and argues it should not be used even for good ends.

Plato. In the Republic (4) the spirited part (thumos) is the source of anger and should ally with reason.

Kant. In the Doctrine of Virtue (sections 36-37, 40), he treats vengefulness as contrary to the duty to others.
