Burnout and apathy

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here place the cause in work and its setting, as well as in the person.

  • Chronic strain. The WHO's description: "chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed."
  • The organisation. Maslach and colleagues say research "places the individual stress experience within a larger organizational context of people's relation to their work." Panagioti and colleagues conclude: "This finding provides support for the view that burnout is a problem of the whole health care organization, rather than individuals."
  • Long strain without recovery. Common description, not from these sources: overload, little control, little reward, unfair treatment, a team that has broken apart, and work that clashes with your values.
  • Other causes of flatness. Also common description: apathy can come from illness, grief or depression, and not only from work.

Burnout is not a character flaw. Burnout is not laziness, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If exhaustion or flatness lasts, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust; a doctor can also check whether something else, such as illness or depression, is involved (a standard statement, not drawn from the sources below). If you may harm yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Maslach, Schaufeli and Leiter (2001), "Job burnout," Annual Review of Psychology 52, 397-422. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.52.1.397 (external link)
  • Panagioti and colleagues (2017), "Controlled interventions to reduce burnout in physicians: a systematic review and meta-analysis," JAMA Internal Medicine 177(2), 195. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.7674 (external link)
  • World Health Organization (2019), "Burn-out an 'occupational phenomenon': International Classification of Diseases" (external link) (28 May 2019), read in full on 2026-11-15.
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

The two papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref; Panagioti and colleagues studied physicians, so the finding is about them. The last two points cite no source.

Hinduism

The Gita names attachment to results as a cause of strain. 2.47: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities".

2.48: "Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The verses are about the mind's relation to results, and burnout often comes from overload and an unfair setting, which are not mentioned. Reading them as a root of burnout is ours, and they should not be used to tell an overworked person that the fault is their attachment. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (external link), 2.48 (external link)

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the basis of effort in what feeds the body. Chandogya 7.9: "Food (anna) is better than power. Therefore if a man abstain from food for ten days, though he live, he would be unable to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand. But when he obtains food, he is able to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand."

Chandogya 7.8 puts power before the rest: "If a man is powerful, he becomes a rising man."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The passage is about food and power in a teaching, and it does not speak of work. Reading it as a root of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

  • Chandogya Upanishad, Seventh Prapathaka (external link), 7.8-7.9 (Max Müller's translation)

Christianity

Exodus shows how a good task wears a person out. Jethro asks Moses: "Why do you sit alone, while all the people stand around you from morning until evening?" He answers his own question: "the task is too heavy for you; you cannot do it alone."

Mark 6:31 describes the apostles' day: "they had no leisure even to eat."

Psalm 127:2 names the strain: "the bread of anxious toil".

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The causes shown are too much, alone, and without breaks. Reading these as roots of burnout is ours, and how commentators read them was not checked. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name tension and slackness in energy, and the way attention feeds them. AN 6.55: "when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness."

SN 46.51 says what fuels dullness: "There is discontent, sloth, yawning, sleepiness after eating, and mental sluggishness. Frequent irrational application of mind to that fuels the arising of dullness and drowsiness".

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The texts are about a monk's practice, and "irrational application of mind" is the translator's phrase for a way of attending. Reading them as roots of burnout is ours, and they should not be read as saying that burnout is a failure of attention. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • AN 6.55 (external link), SN 46.51 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-15. The SN 46.51 quotation joins three lines. The Pali words were not examined.

Philosophical

Seneca places the cause in unbroken labour. Of Peace of Mind: "Neither ought we always to keep the mind strained to the same pitch, but it ought sometimes to be relaxed by amusement."

And: "the liveliness of our minds will be destroyed by unceasing labour".

Aristotle gives the reason: "we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. Both are arguments for rest and not accounts of a syndrome, and neither speaks of unfair or overloaded work. Reading them as roots of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Seneca, Of Peace of Mind (external link), section XVII (Aubrey Stewart's translation); Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book X (external link), 10.6 (W. D. Ross's translation)
