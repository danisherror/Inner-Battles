Burnout and apathy

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Burnout is exhaustion from long strain that has left you drained, distant and doubting your own use. Apathy is the flatness that can come with it: little interest in what once mattered. Those are standard descriptions and not taken from the sources below, and apathy is not defined in them.

Maslach, Schaufeli and Leiter write that "Burnout is a prolonged response to chronic emotional and interpersonal stressors on the job, and is defined by the three dimensions of exhaustion, cynicism, and inefficacy." The World Health Organization's ICD-11 describes it as "a syndrome conceptualized as resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed", with three dimensions: "feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion; increased mental distance from one's job, or feelings of negativism or cynicism related to one's job; and reduced professional efficacy." The WHO says burn-out "is not classified as a medical condition", and "should not be applied to describe experiences in other areas of life."

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these sources: waking tired, dreading what you used to manage, snapping, and feeling nothing about things that used to matter. It is a cousin of sloth, and it is not laziness.

Burnout is not laziness, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If exhaustion or flatness lasts, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust; a doctor can also check whether something else, such as illness or depression, is involved (a standard statement, not drawn from the sources below). If you may harm yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for burnout, but it names both ends of a strained life. Gita 6.16: "those who eat too much or too little, sleep too much or too little, cannot attain success in Yog."

On the other side, 2.47: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, nor be attached to inaction."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. 6.16 is advice on the discipline of yoga and 2.47 is about working without clinging to results, and neither describes exhaustion as a state. Reading them as an account of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads show depletion in their ladder of "better than". Chandogya 7.9: "Food (anna) is better than power. Therefore if a man abstain from food for ten days, though he live, he would be unable to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand."

And 7.8: "Power (bala) is better than understanding. One powerful man shakes a hundred men of understanding. If a man is powerful, he becomes a rising man."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The passage is about food and power as steps in a teaching, and does not speak of work or exhaustion. Reading it as an account of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

The Bible names being worn out. Jethro says to Moses: "What you are doing is not good. You will surely wear yourself out, both you and these people with you, for the task is too heavy for you; you cannot do it alone" (Exodus 18:17-18).

Isaiah 40:30: "Even youths will faint and be weary, and the young will fall exhausted".

And Psalm 127:2: "It is in vain that you rise up early and go late to rest, eating the bread of anxious toil, for he gives sleep to his beloved."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. These passages describe the state and its cause without a name for it. The tradition's own word for a weariness of spirit, acedia, is not read here and is discussed in Sloth. Reading the passages as an account of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name dullness as one of the five hindrances. SN 46.51 says what fuels "dullness and drowsiness": "There is discontent, sloth, yawning, sleepiness after eating, and mental sluggishness."

AN 6.55 names the two ways energy goes wrong: "when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The texts are about practice and not about work, and "sloth" and "laziness" are the translator's words for states that are not the same as exhaustion from strain. Reading them as an account of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Aristotle says we cannot work without stopping. Nicomachean Ethics 10.6: "amusement is a sort of relaxation, and we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously."

Seneca names what unbroken work does: "continuous toil produces a sort of numbness and sluggishness."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. Aristotle is explaining why we amuse ourselves, and Seneca is writing about the mind's need for rest. Neither describes a syndrome. Reading them as an account of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book X (external link), 10.6 (W. D. Ross's translation); Seneca, Of Peace of Mind (external link), section XVII (Aubrey Stewart's translation)
