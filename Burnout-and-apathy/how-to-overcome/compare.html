Burnout and apathy

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here finds that remedies help a little, and that the setting matters. Panagioti and colleagues, reviewing trials in physicians, found "small significant reductions in burnout" overall (a standardised mean difference of -0.29), with organisation-directed interventions (-0.45) doing better than physician-directed ones (-0.18). Their conclusion: "recent intervention programs for burnout in physicians were associated with small benefits that may be boosted by adoption of organization-directed approaches." Maslach and colleagues point to "engagement, the positive antithesis of burnout". The steps below are common advice.

  1. Name it and check. Say "I am burnt out" (words of our own), and see a doctor, who can check for illness or depression. That is a standard statement, not drawn from the papers.
  2. Rest properly. Sleep, real breaks and days off. That is common advice.
  3. Change the load where you can. Talk to a manager, hand tasks on, say no. This follows the review's finding that changes to the setting did better than changes to the person, and the review is of physicians.
  4. Keep something that gives energy back. A walk, a friend, a hobby, with no target. That is common advice.
  5. Do not carry it alone. Tell someone. That is common advice.
  6. Get help. Burnout is not laziness, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If exhaustion or flatness lasts, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust; a doctor can also check whether something else, such as illness or depression, is involved (a standard statement, not drawn from the sources below). If you may harm yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Panagioti and colleagues (2017), "Controlled interventions to reduce burnout in physicians: a systematic review and meta-analysis," JAMA Internal Medicine 177(2), 195. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.7674 (external link)
  • Maslach, Schaufeli and Leiter (2001), "Job burnout," Annual Review of Psychology 52, 397-422. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.52.1.397 (external link)
  • World Health Organization (2019), "Burn-out an 'occupational phenomenon': International Classification of Diseases" (external link) (28 May 2019), read in full on 2026-11-15.
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

The figures are the abstract's standardised mean differences, rounded as given there. Steps 1, 2, 4 and 5 are common advice and step 3 is the site's reading of the review, not tested in trials of those steps.

Hinduism

The Gita counsels balance and a steady mind.

  1. Keep a moderate rhythm. 6.17: "those who are temperate in eating and recreation, balanced in work, and regulated in sleep, can mitigate all sorrows by practicing Yog."
  2. Avoid both extremes. 6.16: "those who eat too much or too little, sleep too much or too little, cannot attain success in Yog."
  3. Work steadily, and let go of the result. 2.48: "Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. These verses are about the discipline of yoga and do not speak of overload, and they should not be read as telling a worn-out person to work on with a calmer mind. Reading them as a way through burnout is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to what restores. Chandogya 7.9: "when he obtains food, he is able to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand. Meditate on food."

And 7.8: "If a man is powerful, he becomes a rising man. If he rises, he becomes a man who visits wise people."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The passage is a step in a teaching and does not speak of recovery from exhaustion. The site makes no claim about how to practise it, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

The Christian texts counsel rest, sharing the load, and waiting.

  1. Rest. Mark 6:31: "Come away to a deserted place all by yourselves and rest a while."
  2. Share the load. Exodus 18:22: "let them bring every important case to you but decide every minor case themselves. So it will be easier for you, and they will bear the burden with you."
  3. Receive rest. Matthew 11:28: "Come to me, all you who are weary and are carrying heavy burdens, and I will give you rest."
  4. Wait to be renewed. Isaiah 40:31: "those who wait for the Lord shall renew their strength".
  5. Sleep. Psalm 127:2: "he gives sleep to his beloved."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. These verses do not say that rest is a reward for the worthy, and Galatians 6:9 ("let us not grow weary in doing what is right") should not be read as telling anyone to push on. Reading them as a way through burnout is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts counsel tuning energy like the strings of a harp.

  1. Tune it. AN 6.55: "when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness. So, Soṇa, you should focus on energy and serenity, find a balance of the faculties, and learn the character of this situation."
  2. Find the middle. The same sutta: "when your harp's strings were tuned neither too tight nor too slack, but fixed at an even tension, was it resonant and playable?"
  3. Start small when dull. SN 46.51 names what starves dullness and drowsiness: "the elements of initiative, persistence, and exertion."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The texts are about a monk's practice, and step 3 should be read beside step 1, since the first point of the sutta is not to strain. Reading them as a way through burnout is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

The philosophers' counsel is to rest in order to work, and to alternate.

  1. Rest so that you can act. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 10.6: "to amuse oneself in order that one may exert oneself, as Anacharsis puts it, seems right; for amusement is a sort of relaxation, and we need relaxation because we cannot work continuously."
  2. Do not force the field. Seneca, Of Peace of Mind: "We must not force crops from rich fields, for an unbroken course of heavy crops will soon exhaust their fertility".
  3. Alternate. Same passage: "pass our lives alternately in solitude and among throngs of people".

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. Both are advice for a healthy mind, and neither describes recovery from exhaustion. Reading them as a way through burnout is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.
