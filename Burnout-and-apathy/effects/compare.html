Burnout and apathy

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes effects on health and work. Salvagioni and colleagues reviewed prospective studies and report that "Burnout was a significant predictor of the following physical consequences: hypercholesterolemia, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, hospitalization due to cardiovascular disorder, musculoskeletal pain, changes in pain experiences, prolonged fatigue, headaches, gastrointestinal issues, respiratory problems, severe injuries and mortality below the age of 45 years." The psychological effects were "insomnia, depressive symptoms, use of psychotropic and antidepressant medications, hospitalization for mental disorders and psychological ill-health symptoms." They also note: "Conflicting findings were observed."

Panagioti and colleagues write that "Burnout is prevalent in physicians and can have a negative influence on performance, career continuation, and patient care."

These are group findings from studies of workers, and they say nothing certain about one person. Common effects, as people describe them and not findings from these papers: dreading work, short temper, less patience at home, and flatness about what used to bring joy.

None of this is a reason for alarm, and it is a reason to rest and to ask for help. Burnout is not laziness, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If exhaustion or flatness lasts, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust; a doctor can also check whether something else, such as illness or depression, is involved (a standard statement, not drawn from the sources below). If you may harm yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Salvagioni and colleagues (2017), "Physical, psychological and occupational consequences of job burnout: a systematic review of prospective studies," PLOS ONE 12(10), e0185781. doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0185781 (external link)
  • Panagioti and colleagues (2017), "Controlled interventions to reduce burnout in physicians: a systematic review and meta-analysis," JAMA Internal Medicine 177(2), 195. doi:10.1001/jamainternmed.2016.7674 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

Both papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The reading that these are group findings and not certain for one person is ours. The list of common effects cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita names what an unbalanced life costs. 6.16: "those who eat too much or too little, sleep too much or too little, cannot attain success in Yog."

18.28 describes a worker in the mode of ignorance: "A performer in the mode of ignorance is one who is undisciplined, vulgar, stubborn, deceitful, slothful, despondent, and a procrastinator."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. 18.28 is a description of a kind of work in the Gita's system of modes, and its words ("vulgar", "deceitful") are the translator's. They should not be read as a description of people who are exhausted. Reading the verses as effects of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

  • Bhagavad Gita 6.16 (external link), 18.28 (external link)

Upanishads

The Upanishads name what depletion does. Chandogya 7.9: "if a man abstain from food for ten days, though he live, he would be unable to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand."

And what restores: "But when he obtains food, he is able to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The passage is about food, and the effects named are of going without. Reading it as the effects of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

The Bible names the weariness: "Come to me, all you who are weary and are carrying heavy burdens" (Matthew 11:28). Isaiah 40:30: "Even youths will faint and be weary, and the young will fall exhausted".

Exodus 18:18: "You will surely wear yourself out, both you and these people with you".

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. The effects shown are fainting, weariness and wearing out, and they reach the people around the one who is spent. Reading them as effects of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts show the pull to quit. In AN 6.55 the monk Soṇa, who is "one of the Buddha's most energetic disciples", thinks: "Why don't I resign the training and return to a lesser life, so I can enjoy my wealth and make merit?"

SN 46.51 names dullness and drowsiness as what grows when "discontent, sloth, yawning, sleepiness after eating, and mental sluggishness" are fed.

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. Soṇa's thought comes from his sense that his effort is not freeing him, and the text does not call it exhaustion. Reading the passages as effects of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

  • AN 6.55 (external link), SN 46.51 (external link)

Philosophical

Seneca names the effect of unbroken work: "continuous toil produces a sort of numbness and sluggishness." And: "the liveliness of our minds will be destroyed by unceasing labour, but they will recover their strength after a short period of rest and relief".

Aristotle names the cost of making amusement the aim: "it would, indeed, be strange if the end were amusement, and one were to take trouble and suffer hardship all one's life in order to amuse oneself."

This is related to burnout and not identical to it. Seneca is writing about the mind and Aristotle about the purpose of amusement, and neither describes a syndrome. Reading them as effects of burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Seneca, Of Peace of Mind (external link), section XVII (Aubrey Stewart's translation); Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book X (external link), 10.6 (W. D. Ross's translation)
