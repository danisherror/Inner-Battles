Burnout and apathy

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how burnout works and how people recover.

Nadia's story

Nadia led a small team. After two years of covering gaps, answering messages at night and skipping breaks, she began waking tired and dreading Mondays. Things she had enjoyed felt flat, and she snapped at a colleague over nothing.

What helped:

  1. She named it and checked. She told her doctor she thought she was burnt out. The doctor asked about her sleep and mood, and ruled out illness.
  2. She rested properly. She took the leave she had been saving, and slept.
  3. She changed the load. She told her manager what she was carrying, handed two tasks on, and stopped answering messages after seven.
  4. She kept something that gave energy back. A Saturday walk with a friend, with no target.
  5. She did not carry it alone. She told her sister, and then the team.
  6. She asked for more help. The flatness lingered for weeks, so she saw a counsellor.

It took months, and some weeks she slipped back into old habits.

What it shows: Nadia did not fix it by pushing harder. Rest, a lighter load and someone to tell mattered more than another resolution.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. Burnout is not laziness, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If exhaustion or flatness lasts, or comes with low mood, hopelessness or thoughts of harming yourself, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust; a doctor can also check whether something else, such as illness or depression, is involved (a standard statement, not drawn from the sources below). Hinduism

The regulated life

Two verses of the Gita draw the picture. 6.16: "those who eat too much or too little, sleep too much or too little, cannot attain success in Yog." 6.17: "those who are temperate in eating and recreation, balanced in work, and regulated in sleep, can mitigate all sorrows by practicing Yog."

What it shows: The life that works is neither overdone nor underdone, in food, sleep and work. This is a picture drawn from two verses and not a tale of a person, and linking it to burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

Narada on power and food

In Chandogya 7 Narada asks again and again: "Sir, is there something better than" the last thing? In 7.8 Sanatkumara says: "Power (bala) is better than understanding. One powerful man shakes a hundred men of understanding."

Then, in 7.9: "Food (anna) is better than power. Therefore if a man abstain from food for ten days, though he live, he would be unable to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand. But when he obtains food, he is able to see, hear, perceive, think, act, and understand."

What it shows: In the teaching, power depends on food, and when the body is emptied the mind goes with it. This is a moment in a dialogue and not a tale of burnout, and linking it to burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

Moses and Jethro

In Exodus 18 Moses sits as judge "from morning until evening". His father-in-law Jethro asks: "Why do you sit alone, while all the people stand around you from morning until evening?" Moses explains: "Because the people come to me to inquire of God."

Jethro says: "What you are doing is not good. You will surely wear yourself out, both you and these people with you, for the task is too heavy for you; you cannot do it alone." He advises that Moses teach the people, and "look for able men among all the people" and set them over groups: "let them bring every important case to you but decide every minor case themselves. So it will be easier for you, and they will bear the burden with you."

"So Moses listened to his father-in-law and did all that he had said."

What it shows: The remedy is to share the work. The story is about judging a people and not about burnout as such; the link is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

Soṇa and the harp

In AN 6.55 the monk Soṇa thinks: "I am one of the Buddha's most energetic disciples. Yet my mind is not freed from defilements by not grasping." He considers going back to a lesser life. The Buddha knows his thought, appears before him and asks: "When you were still a layman, weren't you a good player of the arched harp?"

"When your harp's strings were tuned too tight, was it resonant and playable?" "No, sir." The same for too slack. "But when your harp's strings were tuned neither too tight nor too slack, but fixed at an even tension, was it resonant and playable?"

"In the same way, Soṇa, when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness. So, Soṇa, you should focus on energy and serenity, find a balance of the faculties, and learn the character of this situation."

"After some time Soṇa focused on energy and serenity, found a balance of the faculties, and learned the pattern of this situation."

What it shows: The Buddha's answer to a man tempted to quit is to tune his effort. The sutta is about a monk's practice and not about burnout as such; the link is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

The rested field

Seneca gives a picture. "Men's minds ought to have relaxation: they rise up better and more vigorous after rest. We must not force crops from rich fields, for an unbroken course of heavy crops will soon exhaust their fertility, and so also the liveliness of our minds will be destroyed by unceasing labour, but they will recover their strength after a short period of rest and relief: for continuous toil produces a sort of numbness and sluggishness."

What it shows: The mind is pictured as a field that gives more when it is allowed to lie fallow. It is an argument in an essay and not a tale of a person, and linking it to burnout is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and exhaustion that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.
