Buddhist texts have no word for overthinking, but they describe its parts closely. **Restlessness and remorse** (*uddhacca-kukkucca*) is the fourth of the five hindrances to meditation (see [sloth in Buddhism](../../Sloth/what-it-is/buddhism.html) for the list). The mind itself is described as unsteady. Dhp 33 says: "The mind quivers and quakes, hard to guard, hard to curb." Dhp 35: "Hard to hold back, flighty, alighting where it will".

The Buddha also describes how thinking feeds on itself. In MN 19 he says: "Whatever a mendicant frequently thinks about and considers becomes their heart's inclination."

And in SN 36.6, the *Sallatha Sutta*, he describes the way thought adds suffering to pain: an unlearned person who feels a painful sensation sorrows and laments, and so "experiences two feelings: physical and mental. It's like a person who is struck with an arrow, only to be struck with a second arrow."

This is related to overthinking and not identical to it. The texts speak of restlessness, remorse and the habits of thought in the context of meditation and suffering, and do not name overthinking as a category.
