Overthinking

Going round the same thoughts about the past or the future, unable to settle or to act.

What it is

Philosophers describe overthinking as the mind disturbing itself, through judgments, anticipation and an inability to be still.

Epictetus begins the Enchiridion by sorting what is up to us: "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions." And in section 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."

Seneca writes to Lucilius about fear that runs ahead of events: "some things torment us more than they ought; some torment us before they ought; and some torment us when they ought not to torment us at all. We are in the habit of exaggerating, or imagining, or anticipating, sorrow" (Letter 13).

Pascal names the restlessness behind it: "all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber" (Pensées, in the section on diversion).

These are related to overthinking and not identical to it. The Stoics speak of judgments and fear, and Pascal of restlessness and distraction; none writes of rumination as a modern term.
