Overthinking

Modern

Overthinking is going over the same thoughts again and again without getting closer to a decision or to peace. Psychologists use more exact words for its forms:

  • Rumination: repeatedly dwelling on distress, its causes and its consequences, usually about the past or the present.
  • Worry: the same looping aimed at the future, rehearsing what might go wrong.
  • Restlessness: an agitated mind that cannot settle, jumping from one thing to another. In a smartphone study of people's ongoing thoughts, Killingsworth and Gilbert found that people are thinking about what is not happening almost as often as they are thinking about what is.

Repeated thinking is not harmful in itself. Watkins's review found that repetitive thought can be constructive, as in adaptive preparation, planning and recovery from upsetting events, or unconstructive, as in depression, anxiety and difficulties in physical health. The difference lies in how you think, not in how much.

Overthinking is a different thing from depression or an anxiety disorder, though it can come with them. If the loops are constant or your mood is low, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Watkins (2008), "Constructive and unconstructive repetitive thought," Psychological Bulletin 134(2), 163-206. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.163 (external link)
  • Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky (2008), "Rethinking rumination," Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(5), 400-424. doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x (external link)
  • Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010), "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind," Science 330, 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word for overthinking, but the Bhagavad Gita describes both the restless mind and a loop of dwelling that grows worse.

Arjuna says in 6.34: "The mind is very restless, turbulent, strong and obstinate, O Krishna. It appears to me that it is more difficult to control than the wind." The Sanskrit words glossed there are chanchala (restless) and pramathi (turbulent).

In 2.62-63, the Gita describes a chain that starts with dwelling: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger. Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

This is related to overthinking and not identical to it. The Gita is speaking of dwelling on the objects of the senses and desire, and it does not use a word for brooding on the past or rehearsing the future.

Upanishads

The Upanishads have no word for overthinking. They speak of the unsteady mind and of grief that does not let go, and they set against these a still mind and a witness.

In the Katha Upanishad, the self is compared to a rider in a chariot: the body is the chariot, the intellect the charioteer and the mind the reins (1.3.3-4). "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer" (1.3.5).

The Mundaka Upanishad gives an image of two birds on one tree. "On the same tree man sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence. But when he sees the other lord contented and knows his glory, then his grief passes away" (3.1.2).

This is related to overthinking and not identical to it: the texts speak of the restless mind and of grief, within a path toward knowledge of the Self, and not of everyday loops of thought.

Christianity

The Bible has no word for overthinking, but it names its relatives: worry, anxiety and a mind that is "disquieted within me".

Jesus says: "do not worry about your life" (Matthew 6:25). To Martha he says: "you are worried and distracted by many things" (Luke 10:41). Proverbs 12:25 says: "Anxiety weighs down the human heart, but a good word cheers it up." The psalmist asks himself: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you disquieted within me?" (Psalm 42:5).

The desert tradition calls tempting inner thoughts logismoi, "thoughts". Evagrius of Pontus writes: "Whether these thoughts are able to disturb the soul or not is not up to us; but whether they linger or not, and whether they arouse passions or not; that is up to us."

This is related to overthinking and not identical to it. Scripture speaks of worry about life and provision, and Evagrius of the thoughts that tempt a monk, and neither describes a general habit of looping over the past and future.

Buddhism

Buddhist texts have no word for overthinking, but they describe its parts closely. Restlessness and remorse (uddhacca-kukkucca) is the fourth of the five hindrances to meditation (see sloth in Buddhism for the list). The mind itself is described as unsteady. Dhp 33 says: "The mind quivers and quakes, hard to guard, hard to curb." Dhp 35: "Hard to hold back, flighty, alighting where it will".

The Buddha also describes how thinking feeds on itself. In MN 19 he says: "Whatever a mendicant frequently thinks about and considers becomes their heart's inclination."

And in SN 36.6, the Sallatha Sutta, he describes the way thought adds suffering to pain: an unlearned person who feels a painful sensation sorrows and laments, and so "experiences two feelings: physical and mental. It's like a person who is struck with an arrow, only to be struck with a second arrow."

This is related to overthinking and not identical to it. The texts speak of restlessness, remorse and the habits of thought in the context of meditation and suffering, and do not name overthinking as a category.

Philosophical

Philosophers describe overthinking as the mind disturbing itself, through judgments, anticipation and an inability to be still.

Epictetus begins the Enchiridion by sorting what is up to us: "Some things are in our control and others not. Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions." And in section 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."

Seneca writes to Lucilius about fear that runs ahead of events: "some things torment us more than they ought; some torment us before they ought; and some torment us when they ought not to torment us at all. We are in the habit of exaggerating, or imagining, or anticipating, sorrow" (Letter 13).

Pascal names the restlessness behind it: "all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber" (Pensées, in the section on diversion).

These are related to overthinking and not identical to it. The Stoics speak of judgments and fear, and Pascal of restlessness and distraction; none writes of rumination as a modern term.
