A story

The Buddha sorts his thoughts

In the Dvedhavitakka Sutta (MN 19), the Buddha tells his monks how he handled his own thoughts before his awakening. "Before my awakening—when I was still unawakened but intent on awakening—I thought: 'Why don't I meditate by continually dividing my thoughts into two classes?'"

He put sensual, malicious and cruel thoughts in one class, and thoughts of renunciation, good will and harmlessness in the other.

When a harmful thought arose, he did not fight it. He looked at where it led: "It leads to hurting myself, hurting others, and hurting both. It blocks wisdom, it's on the side of distress, and it doesn't lead to extinguishment." And then: "When I reflected that it leads to hurting myself, it went away."

He compares this to a cowherd in the last month of the rains, when the crops stand close together: the cowherd keeps tapping and poking them with his staff, on this side and that, to keep them in check, because if the cattle wander into the crops he could be punished.

Even with good thoughts, he adds, he did not go on without end: "thinking and considering for too long would tire my body. And when the body is tired, the mind is stressed." So he "stilled, settled, unified, and immersed" his mind.

What it shows: The Buddha does not describe emptying the mind. He describes watching which thoughts arise, seeing where they lead, and then, when thinking has done its work, letting the mind rest.
