Seneca's test for imagined troubles

In Letter 13, Seneca writes to his friend Lucilius about fear. We do not know what Lucilius was afraid of, but Seneca's advice is a method anyone can follow.

He observes how common the problem is: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." He tells Lucilius to ask himself directly: "Am I tormented without sufficient reason, am I morose, and do I convert what is not an evil into what is an evil?"

Then comes the test. Lucilius might object: "How am I to know whether my sufferings are real or imaginary?" Seneca answers: "We are tormented either by things present, or by things to come, or by both." For the present, he says, the decision is easy: if you are free, healthy and uninjured, "To-day there is nothing wrong with it."

For the future, he asks for proof: "consider whether your proofs of future trouble are sure." We are often troubled by our apprehensions, he says, and we agree too quickly with what we hear: "We do not put to the test those things which cause our fear; we do not examine into them."

What it shows: Seneca does not tell Lucilius to stop thinking. He asks him to examine each fear, as a judge would look at evidence, and to separate what is happening now from what only might.
