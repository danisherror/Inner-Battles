Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how overthinking works and how people step out of it.

Anil's story

In a team meeting, Anil disagreed with his manager's plan. His manager said, "Let's take that offline," and moved on.

By midnight, Anil was lying awake replaying it. Had he sounded rude? Had the tone changed? Why does this always happen to me? What if it counted against him in the next review? Each pass through produced no new fact. It only produced more dread.

The next day he was tired, short with a colleague, and could not focus on his own work.

What helped:

  1. He named it. "This is rumination. I'm going round, not solving."
  2. He asked a concrete question. Instead of "why does this always happen?", he asked "what is one thing I can do?" The answer was to send his manager a two-line note: "Thanks for hearing me out. Happy to talk through the plan whenever suits."
  3. He changed the way he talked to himself. He tried, "Anil, you made a reasonable point. What would you tell a friend?"
  4. He gave the worry a limit. Ten minutes in the evening to think it through on paper, then a walk.
  5. He noticed that it was not getting better. After two weeks of poor sleep, he booked a visit with his doctor.

His manager replied the next morning with a thumbs-up and a time for a chat. The loop did not vanish at once, but it got shorter each time.

What it shows: Going round a problem feels like work and rarely is. A concrete next step, a kinder voice and a time limit moved Anil from replaying to doing.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If loops are constant or you cannot sleep, please see a doctor.

Hinduism

Arjuna and the wind

In chapter 6 of the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna has been teaching Arjuna how to steady the mind in meditation. Arjuna interrupts with an honest objection. In 6.33 he says: "The system of Yog that you have described, O Madhusudan, appears impractical and unattainable to me, due to the restless mind."

He goes on in 6.34: "The mind is very restless, turbulent, strong and obstinate, O Krishna. It appears to me that it is more difficult to control than the wind."

Krishna does not tell him he is wrong. In 6.35 he answers: "what you say is correct; the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled."

And in 6.26 he adds what to do in practice: "Whenever and wherever the restless and unsteady mind wanders, one should bring it back".

What it shows: The restless mind is treated as ordinary, even for the best warrior in the story. The remedy offered is not a one-time victory but steady returning, each time it wanders.

Upanishads

The two birds on one tree

The Mundaka Upanishad tells a short parable. "Two birds, inseparable friends, cling to the same tree. One of them eats the sweet fruit, the other looks on without eating" (3.1.1).

The first bird stands for the person caught up in life, tasting each fruit and being moved by it. The Upanishad says of this one: "On the same tree man sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence" (3.1.2).

The second bird only watches. And the verse turns on what happens when the first bird looks at it: "But when he sees the other lord contented and knows his glory, then his grief passes away."

What it shows: The image says that the same person has two positions, one inside the thoughts and one watching them, and that grief eases when attention shifts from the first to the second. The teaching is that the watcher is already there.

Sources

  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) (Max Müller's translation) 3.1.1-2

Christianity

Martha and Mary

In Luke's Gospel, Jesus comes to a village where a woman named Martha welcomes him. Her sister Mary "sat at Jesus's feet and listened to what he was saying" (Luke 10:39).

"But Martha was distracted by her many tasks, so she came to him and asked, 'Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to do all the work by myself? Tell her, then, to help me.'"

Jesus answers: "Martha, Martha, you are worried and distracted by many things, but few things are needed—indeed only one. Mary has chosen the better part, which will not be taken away from her" (10:40-42).

Martha is not described as overthinking in so many words. The story shows a related state: a mind pulled in many directions, a rising resentment, and a sense that everything depends on her.

What it shows: Jesus does not scold her for caring. He names the state ("worried and distracted by many things") and points to the one thing that matters. The way out is shown as choosing, not as thinking harder.

Sources

  • Luke 10:38-42 (external link)

The quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway on 2026-11-14. Reading the passage as a story about a busy, worried mind is one common reading; commentators differ on what "the better part" means.

Buddhism

The Buddha sorts his thoughts

In the Dvedhavitakka Sutta (MN 19), the Buddha tells his monks how he handled his own thoughts before his awakening. "Before my awakening—when I was still unawakened but intent on awakening—I thought: 'Why don't I meditate by continually dividing my thoughts into two classes?'"

He put sensual, malicious and cruel thoughts in one class, and thoughts of renunciation, good will and harmlessness in the other.

When a harmful thought arose, he did not fight it. He looked at where it led: "It leads to hurting myself, hurting others, and hurting both. It blocks wisdom, it's on the side of distress, and it doesn't lead to extinguishment." And then: "When I reflected that it leads to hurting myself, it went away."

He compares this to a cowherd in the last month of the rains, when the crops stand close together: the cowherd keeps tapping and poking them with his staff, on this side and that, to keep them in check, because if the cattle wander into the crops he could be punished.

Even with good thoughts, he adds, he did not go on without end: "thinking and considering for too long would tire my body. And when the body is tired, the mind is stressed." So he "stilled, settled, unified, and immersed" his mind.

What it shows: The Buddha does not describe emptying the mind. He describes watching which thoughts arise, seeing where they lead, and then, when thinking has done its work, letting the mind rest.

Sources

  • MN 19, Dvedhavitakka Sutta (external link)

Philosophical

Seneca's test for imagined troubles

In Letter 13, Seneca writes to his friend Lucilius about fear. We do not know what Lucilius was afraid of, but Seneca's advice is a method anyone can follow.

He observes how common the problem is: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." He tells Lucilius to ask himself directly: "Am I tormented without sufficient reason, am I morose, and do I convert what is not an evil into what is an evil?"

Then comes the test. Lucilius might object: "How am I to know whether my sufferings are real or imaginary?" Seneca answers: "We are tormented either by things present, or by things to come, or by both." For the present, he says, the decision is easy: if you are free, healthy and uninjured, "To-day there is nothing wrong with it."

For the future, he asks for proof: "consider whether your proofs of future trouble are sure." We are often troubled by our apprehensions, he says, and we agree too quickly with what we hear: "We do not put to the test those things which cause our fear; we do not examine into them."

What it shows: Seneca does not tell Lucilius to stop thinking. He asks him to examine each fear, as a judge would look at evidence, and to separate what is happening now from what only might.

  • Seneca, Letters 13 (external link) (Gummere)
