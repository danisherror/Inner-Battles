Modern

Researchers describe overthinking as something that develops and is kept going by several factors working together.

  • A habit that grows out of an unresolved goal. In their model of rumination, Watkins and Roberts say it results from "dwelling on problematic goals developing into a learnt habit". In plain terms, a problem you have not solved or let go of keeps pulling your thoughts back.
  • Abstract thinking. The same model points to a tendency to process negative information in an abstract way (asking "why does this always happen to me?") and not concretely (asking "what exactly happened, and what is the next step?"). Watkins's earlier review identifies the level of construal, abstract against concrete, as one of the principles that explain whether repetitive thought is constructive or not.
  • Weak mental control and negative bias. The model adds poor executive control, the ability to steer and switch attention, and a tendency to pick up negative information.
  • Negative styles of thinking. Nolen-Hoeksema and colleagues report that rumination interacts with negative cognitive styles to predict how long depressive symptoms last.

Sources

  • Watkins and Roberts (2020), "Reflecting on rumination: consequences, causes, mechanisms and treatment of rumination," Behaviour Research and Therapy 127, 103573. doi:10.1016/j.brat.2020.103573 (external link)
  • Watkins (2008), Psychological Bulletin 134(2), 163-206. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.163 (external link)
  • Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky (2008), Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(5), 400-424. doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x (external link)

The quoted phrase and the factors listed were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in the full papers. The plain-language examples in brackets are ours.

Hinduism

The Gita traces the restless, circling mind to dwelling, desire and an undisciplined mind.

  • Dwelling on what attracts. Verse 2.62: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger." The loop begins with attention that keeps returning.
  • The nature of the mind. Arjuna in 6.34 calls it "restless, turbulent, strong and obstinate". The problem is a mind that moves, not a failing of character.
  • Not being steady. Verse 2.66 says an undisciplined person "who has not controlled the mind and senses, can neither have a resolute intellect nor steady contemplation".
  • Unmet desires pouring in. Verse 2.70 compares the sage to the ocean, unmoved "by the incessant flow of waters from rivers merging into it", and contrasts "the person who strives to satisfy desires".

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.62 (external link), 6.34 (external link), 2.66 (external link), 2.70 (external link)

Upanishads

The Upanishads locate the root in a mind that is not held, and in identifying with one's own helplessness.

  • Reins not held. Katha 1.3.5: "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable". The senses and thoughts run where they like.
  • Being immersed in one's own distress. Mundaka 3.1.2: "On the same tree man sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence." The man eats the fruit and is caught in it, while the other bird, in 3.1.1, "looks on without eating".
  • Unmindfulness. Katha 1.3.7: "He who has no understanding, who is unmindful and always impure, never reaches that place, but enters into the round of births."

Christianity

Scripture places the roots of a worried, circling mind in anxiety about life, in being pulled in too many directions, and in thoughts that are allowed to stay.

  • Anxiety about provision and the future. Jesus names food, drink and clothing, and says: "Therefore do not worry, saying, 'What will we eat?' or 'What will we drink?' or 'What will we wear?'" He calls his hearers "you of little faith" (Matthew 6:30-31).
  • Too many things at once. Luke 10:41: "you are worried and distracted by many things".
  • A disquieted soul. Psalm 42:5: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you disquieted within me?"
  • Thoughts allowed to linger. Evagrius: "whether they linger or not, and whether they arouse passions or not; that is up to us."

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts point to an unsettled mind, to resisting pain, and to the way habits of thought become inclinations.

  • An unsettled mind, attended to unwisely. SN 46.51 asks what fuels restlessness and remorse: "There is the unsettled mind. Frequent irrational application of mind to that fuels the arising of restlessness and remorse, or, when they have arisen, makes them increase and grow."
  • Resisting pain and reaching for relief. SN 36.6: when an unlearned person is touched by painful feeling "they resist it", and "look forward to enjoying sensual pleasures", because they "don't understand any escape from painful feeling apart from sensual pleasures."
  • Habit. MN 19: "Whatever a mendicant frequently thinks about and considers becomes their heart's inclination."
  • A mind that is hard to hold. Dhp 34 compares it to "a fish pulled from the sea and cast upon the shore", which "flounders about".

Philosophical

The philosophers find the roots in our judgments, in running ahead of events, and in not being able to be still.

  • Judgments, not things. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." Marcus Aurelius says the same: "If thou art pained by any external thing, it is not this thing that disturbs thee, but thy own judgement about it" (Meditations 8).
  • Exaggerating, imagining and anticipating. Seneca, Letter 13: "We are in the habit of exaggerating, or imagining, or anticipating, sorrow." He also says: "it is more often the case that we are troubled by our apprehensions".
  • Striving over what is not in our control. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1: "Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."
  • Being unable to be still. Pascal: unhappiness comes from the fact "that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber."
