Modern

Research links long, unproductive loops of thought with a range of costs.

  • Mood. Nolen-Hoeksema and colleagues review evidence that rumination exacerbates depression, enhances negative thinking, impairs problem solving, interferes with instrumental behaviour (getting things done) and erodes social support. It appears to predict the onset of depression more consistently than its duration.
  • A wider range of problems. The same review reports that rumination is associated with anxiety, binge eating, binge drinking and self-harm, as well as depression.
  • A shared weak point. Watkins and Roberts describe rumination as a "transdiagnostic" vulnerability: it magnifies and prolongs negative mood, interferes with therapy, and is linked with anxiety, depression, psychosis, insomnia and impulsive behaviours. They also report that it exacerbates and maintains physiological stress responses.
  • Unhappiness. Killingsworth and Gilbert found that thinking about what is not happening typically makes people unhappy.

Most of this evidence shows association, and some is correlational, so it cannot show that thinking alone causes each problem. If you are thinking about harming yourself, please contact your local emergency number or a crisis line.

Sources

  • Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky (2008), Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(5), 400-424. doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x (external link)
  • Watkins and Roberts (2020), Behaviour Research and Therapy 127, 103573. doi:10.1016/j.brat.2020.103573 (external link)
  • Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010), Science 330, 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439 (external link)

Findings were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in the full texts. The note about correlational evidence is our caution, not a quotation.

Hinduism

The Gita describes the effects of the unsteady, dwelling mind as a decline in judgment and loss of peace.

  • A downward chain. Verse 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."
  • No peace. Verse 2.66: "For one who never unites the mind with God there is no peace; and how can one who lacks peace be happy?"
  • A mind that overpowers. In 6.34 Arjuna says the mind is "turbulent", "strong and obstinate", harder to control than the wind.
  • Giving up the path. In 6.33 Arjuna says the practice taught to him "appears impractical and unattainable to me, due to the restless mind."

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.63 (external link), 2.66 (external link), 6.34 (external link), 6.33 (external link)

All quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The "no peace" verse names God as the object of union; a secular reading as "no steady object for the mind" is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe the effects as grief, lack of control and going round without arriving.

  • Grief. Mundaka 3.1.2: the man on the tree "sits grieving, immersed, bewildered".
  • Unmanageable senses. Katha 1.3.5: when the mind is never firmly held, the senses are "unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer".
  • The round without end. Katha 1.3.7: the unmindful one "never reaches that place, but enters into the round of births". Here the cycle is the text's own word for the effect, and we read it as describing the going-round of an unquiet mind.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 1.3.5, 1.3.7
  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 3.1.2

The quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. The last bullet is our reading, since the text means the cycle of rebirth.

Christianity

Scripture is plain that worry weighs on a person and gains nothing.

  • It weighs. Proverbs 12:25: "Anxiety weighs down the human heart".
  • It gains nothing. Matthew 6:27: "which of you by worrying can add a single hour to your span of life?"
  • It crowds out what matters. Luke 10:40-42: Martha is "distracted by her many tasks", and says that her sister has left her to do the work. Jesus answers: "you are worried and distracted by many things, but few things are needed—indeed only one."
  • It casts the soul down. Psalm 42:5: "Why are you cast down, O my soul?"
  • The day's own trouble is enough. Matthew 6:34: "So do not worry about tomorrow, for tomorrow will bring worries of its own. Today's trouble is enough for today."

Sources

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts describe the effects as added suffering, a growing habit and a tired mind.

  • A second arrow. SN 36.6: the unlearned person "sorrow[s] and wail[s] and lament[s], beating their breast and falling into confusion" and so feels both the physical pain and the mental pain, like being struck by a second arrow.
  • Restlessness grows with attention. SN 46.51: frequent irrational attention to an unsettled mind "makes them increase and grow".
  • The inclination deepens. MN 19: what a person frequently thinks about becomes the inclination of their mind.
  • Even good thoughts can tire. In MN 19 the Buddha says of wholesome thoughts: "thinking and considering for too long would tire my body. And when the body is tired, the mind is stressed. And when the mind is stressed, it's far from immersion."

Sources

  • SN 36.6 (external link)
  • SN 46.51 (external link)
  • MN 19 (external link)

Philosophical

The philosophers describe the cost as needless suffering, a distorted view of events, and a restless search for distraction.

  • Suffering before it is necessary. Seneca, Letter 98: "He suffers more than is necessary, who suffers before it is necessary."
  • Imagined troubles. Seneca, Letter 13: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality."
  • Dangers that never arrive. In the same letter he says it may be "that the dangers before which you paled as if they were threatening you, will never come upon you; they certainly have not yet come."
  • Terror from our own notion. Epictetus: "Death, for instance, is not terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates. But the terror consists in our notion of death that it is terrible."
  • Flight into distraction. Pascal observes the "quarrels, passions, bold and often bad ventures" people expose themselves to, and traces them to the inability to stay quietly at home.

Sources

  • Seneca, Letters 13 (external link), 98 (external link) (Gummere)
  • Epictetus, Enchiridion (external link) 5 (Higginson)
  • Pascal, Pensées (external link) (Trotter)

The quotations were read at Wikisource, MIT Classics and Project Gutenberg on 2026-11-14. Pascal's sentence about "quarrels, passions, bold and often bad ventures" is quoted from the opening of the "Diversion" passage; his point about risky ventures is about distraction in general, not overthinking.
