Overthinking

Going round the same thoughts about the past or the future, unable to settle or to act.

How to overcome it

Most of what helps works on how you think, not on trying to stop thinking.

  1. Notice and name the loop. "I'm ruminating" or "I'm worrying" puts a little distance between you and the thought.
  2. Move from "why" to "what next". Watkins's work links abstract processing with unconstructive repetitive thought, so shift to concrete questions: what exactly happened, and what is one small step I can take? Reviews of treatments for rumination report that therapies aimed at these mechanisms look promising in preliminary trials.
  3. Talk to yourself as you would to someone else. Kross and colleagues found that people who used their own name or "you", instead of "I", while thinking about a stressful event enhanced self-distancing, performed better on a speech and a first-impression task, showed less distress and engaged in less maladaptive thinking afterwards.
  4. Interrupt with a pleasant or absorbing activity. The review by Nolen-Hoeksema and colleagues says dozens of experimental studies show positive distractions relieve depressed mood, though correlational studies are inconsistent. Treat it as relief for the moment, not a cure.
  5. Give the thinking a place and a limit. Some people set a short daily "thinking time" and park the rest. This is common advice, not drawn from the studies above.
  6. Act on what can be acted on. Write down what you can decide or do, do the first piece, and note what is out of your hands.
  7. Get help for persistent loops. If thoughts are constant or tied to low mood, anxiety or poor sleep, a doctor or therapist can help.

All findings were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full papers. The Kross studies used lab tasks with social stress (first impressions and public speaking), so the result may not carry over to every kind of loop. The move from abstract to concrete questions is our reading of Watkins's findings, not a tested instruction in the abstract. Steps 1, 5 and 6 are common advice and not tied to one study.
