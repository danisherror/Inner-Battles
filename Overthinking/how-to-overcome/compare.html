Overthinking

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Most of what helps works on how you think, not on trying to stop thinking.

  1. Notice and name the loop. "I'm ruminating" or "I'm worrying" puts a little distance between you and the thought.
  2. Move from "why" to "what next". Watkins's work links abstract processing with unconstructive repetitive thought, so shift to concrete questions: what exactly happened, and what is one small step I can take? Reviews of treatments for rumination report that therapies aimed at these mechanisms look promising in preliminary trials.
  3. Talk to yourself as you would to someone else. Kross and colleagues found that people who used their own name or "you", instead of "I", while thinking about a stressful event enhanced self-distancing, performed better on a speech and a first-impression task, showed less distress and engaged in less maladaptive thinking afterwards.
  4. Interrupt with a pleasant or absorbing activity. The review by Nolen-Hoeksema and colleagues says dozens of experimental studies show positive distractions relieve depressed mood, though correlational studies are inconsistent. Treat it as relief for the moment, not a cure.
  5. Give the thinking a place and a limit. Some people set a short daily "thinking time" and park the rest. This is common advice, not drawn from the studies above.
  6. Act on what can be acted on. Write down what you can decide or do, do the first piece, and note what is out of your hands.
  7. Get help for persistent loops. If thoughts are constant or tied to low mood, anxiety or poor sleep, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Watkins (2008), Psychological Bulletin 134(2), 163-206. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.163 (external link)
  • Watkins and Roberts (2020), Behaviour Research and Therapy 127, 103573. doi:10.1016/j.brat.2020.103573 (external link)
  • Kross et al. (2014), "Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism: how you do it matters," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 106(2), 304-324. doi:10.1037/a0035173 (external link)
  • Nolen-Hoeksema, Wisco and Lyubomirsky (2008), Perspectives on Psychological Science 3(5), 400-424. doi:10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x (external link)

All findings were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full papers. The Kross studies used lab tasks with social stress (first impressions and public speaking), so the result may not carry over to every kind of loop. The move from abstract to concrete questions is our reading of Watkins's findings, not a tested instruction in the abstract. Steps 1, 5 and 6 are common advice and not tied to one study.

Hinduism

The Gita's answer is practice, detachment and returning again and again.

  • Practice and detachment. Krishna replies to Arjuna in 6.35: "the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled."
  • Bring it back. Verse 6.26: "Whenever and wherever the restless and unsteady mind wanders, one should bring it back and continually focus it on God." The instruction expects the mind to wander and says what to do when it does. In this translation the object is God; reading it as "your chosen object of attention" is our gloss.
  • Be like the ocean. Verse 2.70: the sage remains undisturbed, as the ocean is by the rivers that flow into it, and attains peace.
  • Lift yourself. Verse 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."

Upanishads

The Upanishadic answer is to still the mind, hold the reins, and become the witness.

  • Still the mind. Katha 2.3.10-11: "When the five instruments of knowledge stand still together with the mind, and when the intellect does not move, that is called the highest state. This, the firm holding back of the senses, is what is called Yoga. He must be free from thoughtlessness then, for Yoga comes and goes."
  • Hold the reins. Katha 1.3.6: "he who has understanding and whose mind is always firmly held, his senses are under control, like good horses of a charioteer."
  • Be the onlooker. In the image of the two birds, grief passes when the man on the tree sees "the other lord contented and knows his glory" (Mundaka 3.1.2). The second bird "looks on without eating" (3.1.1).
  • Wake up. Katha 1.3.14: "Rise, awake! having obtained your boons, understand them!"

The last words of Katha 2.3.11, that "Yoga comes and goes", are an honest note that stillness is not permanent.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 1.3.6, 1.3.14, 2.3.10-11
  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 3.1.1-2

The quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. Other translations differ in wording and sometimes in numbering. Taking these as advice for everyday overthinking is our reading.

Christianity

The Christian answer is to hand the worry over, to speak to the soul, and to guard the mind.

  • Hand it over. Philippians 4:6-7: "Do not be anxious about anything, but in everything by prayer and supplication with thanksgiving let your requests be made known to God. And the peace of God, which surpasses all understanding, will guard your hearts and your minds in Christ Jesus." 1 Peter 5:7: "Cast all your anxiety on him, because he cares for you."
  • Take one day at a time. Matthew 6:34: "Today's trouble is enough for today."
  • Talk to your own soul. Psalm 42:5 answers its own question: "Hope in God, for I shall again praise him".
  • Seek a good word. Proverbs 12:25: "a good word cheers it up". Other people can help.
  • Take thoughts captive. 2 Corinthians 10:5: "we take every thought captive to obey Christ."
  • Renew the mind. Romans 12:2: "be transformed by the renewing of the mind".
  • Don't feed what lingers. Evagrius: whether thoughts linger "is up to us."
  • Steadfast mind. Isaiah 26:3: "Those of steadfast mind you keep in peace".

Buddhism

The Buddhist remedies are to settle the mind, to sort thoughts, and to feel one arrow and not two.

  • Starve what fuels it. SN 46.51: "There is the settled mind. Frequent rational application of mind to that starves the arising of restlessness and remorse, or, when they have arisen, starves their increase and growth."
  • Sort your thoughts. In MN 19 the Buddha describes sorting thoughts into two classes: sensual, malicious and cruel thoughts in one, and thoughts of renunciation, good will and harmlessness in the other. When a harmful thought arose he reflected that it leads to hurting himself, others and both, and that it blocks wisdom, and "it went away".
  • Guard the mind. Dhp 33: "The discerning straighten it out, like a fletcher an arrow." Dhp 36: "the discerning protect the mind, a guarded mind leads to bliss."
  • Settle and still it. In MN 19 he says: "I stilled, settled, unified, and immersed my mind internally... So that my mind would not be stressed."
  • Feel one arrow. SN 36.6: the learned disciple, touched by painful feeling, does not sorrow or wail, and "experiences one feeling: physical, not mental."

Sources

  • SN 46.51 (external link), MN 19 (external link), Dhp 33-43 (external link), SN 36.6 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-14. The description of the two classes of thought in MN 19 is shortened. The method is monastic, and applying it to everyday overthinking is our reading. Persistent loops with low mood or anxiety are a matter for a doctor or therapist, which the suttas do not discuss.

Philosophical

The philosophers' remedies are to test the thought, change the judgment, and focus on what is in your control.

  • Change the judgment. Marcus Aurelius: "it is in thy power to wipe out this judgement now."
  • Sort what is yours. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1: opinion, pursuit, desire and aversion are in our control; body, property and reputation are not. Spend effort on the first.
  • Cross-examine the fear. Seneca, Letter 13: "Put the question voluntarily to yourself: 'Am I tormented without sufficient reason, am I morose, and do I convert what is not an evil into what is an evil?'"
  • Test the evidence. Seneca's rule is to ask whether the trouble is present or only to come: "As to things present, the decision is easy... To-day there is nothing wrong with it." And for the future: "consider whether your proofs of future trouble are sure."
  • Look for the thought behind the feeling. Epictetus, section 5: "When therefore we are hindered, or disturbed, or grieved, let us never attribute it to others, but to ourselves; that is, to our own principles."
