Gluttony

Modern

Chronic over-indulgence has costs for body, mind and life.

  • Physical health. Habitual overeating is linked to weight gain and to higher risk of type 2 diabetes, heart disease and some cancers. Alcohol excess adds liver and other harms.
  • Mood. Eating past comfort often brings guilt and shame, which can fuel the next episode.
  • Energy and focus. Heavy meals and poor sleep go together with tiredness.
  • Money and time. Food, drink and delivery costs add up, as do hours spent on it.
  • Relationships. Shared meals and social life can be strained by secrecy or by drinking.

Weight is shaped by genes, medicine, environment and income as well as by choices, so body size alone says nothing about someone's character.

Hinduism

The Hindu texts name the costs of indulging the senses.

  • Loss of balance. Gita 6.16: yoga is not for one who eats too much or too little, sleeps too much or too little.
  • Ruin of the mind. Gita 2.62-63 describes the downward chain from dwelling on objects to loss of reason.
  • Disease and heaviness. Gita 17.10 notes that stale, tasteless and impure food belongs to tamas. 17.9 says that over-spicy and harsh food causes pain, grief and disease.
  • Stirred senses. Gita 2.67: the mind drawn by one wandering sense carries off wisdom like wind a ship.
  • Benefit of moderation. Gita 6.17: for the one moderate in food and recreation, yoga destroys sorrow.

Upanishads

The Upanishads connect eating with the quality of mind and of life.

  • Mind follows food. Chandogya 6.5.4: the mind is made of food, and 7.26.2 adds, in Müller's rendering, that when the "aliment" is purified the whole nature is purified (commentators disagree on whether this means literal food). Misuse of food clouds the mind.
  • Wasted gift. Taittiriya 3.7-8 instructs never to abuse or shun food, which suggests that both overindulgence and waste are a failure of respect.
  • Wrong direction. Katha 1.2.2: the one who chooses the pleasant loses the aim.
  • Unruly senses. Katha 1.3.4-5: the senses of the unsteady person are "like the wicked horses of a charioteer."

Christianity

Scripture and tradition list the consequences.

  • Poverty and drowsiness. Proverbs 23:21: "the drunkard and the glutton will come to poverty, and drowsiness will clothe them with rags."
  • Dulled heart. Luke 21:34 warns that hearts can be "weighed down with dissipation and drunkenness."
  • Lost birthright. Esau's stew costs him his inheritance (Genesis 25:34).
  • Neglecting the poor. In Luke 16:19-31, the rich man "feasted sumptuously every day" while Lazarus lay hungry at his gate.
  • Body as temple. 1 Corinthians 6:19-20 says the body is a temple of the Holy Spirit.
  • Gregory the Great (Moralia 31.45) lists dullness of mind and excessive merriment among the effects of gluttony.

Buddhism

Excess in eating brings heaviness, sleepiness and distraction.

  • Sloth and torpor. Overeating feeds thina-middha (sluggishness and drowsiness), one of the five hindrances to meditation.
  • Health. In SN 3.13, King Pasenadi, who eats a large amount and is uncomfortable, is told that mindful moderation brings ease, and his health improves.
  • Craving grows. Indulgence feeds kama-tanha, which the first sermon names as a source of suffering (SN 56.11).
  • Distraction. The Buddha taught monks to eat only to maintain the body and support the holy life (MN 2), and not for fun or vanity.
  • Benefits of restraint. MN 70 lists lightness, strength, health and comfort from eating less.

Philosophical

Philosophers describe the costs as lost freedom and lost pleasure.

  • Slavery to appetite. Plato (Republic 9, 571-576) pictures the person ruled by appetite as inwardly enslaved.
  • Self-defeat. Aristotle (NE 3.12) says the intemperate person's appetite grows with indulgence, and that he cannot be rid of it by reasoning in the moment.
  • Reduced pleasure. Epicurus argues that luxury makes us dependent and anxious about losing it, while plain living gives security (Letter to Menoeceus, 130-131).
  • Loss of reason. The Stoics (Seneca, Letters 95 and 122) describe heavy eating and drinking as dulling the mind.
  • Harm to body. Seneca (Letters 95.15-18) writes that excess brings many illnesses, and that in earlier times "they enjoyed plain health. It took elaborate courses to produce elaborate diseases" (Gummere translation).
