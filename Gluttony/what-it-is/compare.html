Modern

Gluttony is over-indulgence: taking more than you need, or taking it in a way that is driven by craving instead of hunger. It is usually pictured as overeating, but it covers drink, and by extension any habit of consuming past the point of benefit, such as bingeing on screens or sweets.

Aquinas listed five forms: eating too soon, too expensively, too much, too eagerly and too daintily. Modern research on hedonic eating (eating for pleasure when not hungry) and emotional eating fits the same picture.

It is not the same as an eating disorder. Binge eating disorder and similar conditions are medical problems, and they call for care, not moral blame.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word equal to the Christian sin of gluttony. The closest ideas are lobha (greed), kama (craving) applied to taste, and the failure of sense-control (indriya-nigraha), with the tongue seen as the hardest sense to master.

The Gita speaks to it directly. In 6.16-17, yoga is "not for one who eats too much or fasts too much", and it belongs to the one who is moderate in eating, recreation, work and sleep. In 17.8-10, it describes the food that each guna prefers: sattvic food is nourishing and pleasant, while rajasic food (very bitter, sour, salty, hot or dry) and tamasic food (stale, tasteless, impure) belong to the other two.

This is related to gluttony and not identical to it: the focus is balance, not sin.

Upanishads

The Upanishads treat food as sacred and as something to be handled with care. The Taittiriya Upanishad describes food (anna) as the first sheath of the self (2.2) and, in the Bhrigu chapter, says food is Brahman (3.2). Its rules on food are: "Let him never abuse food, that is the rule" (3.7), "Let him never shun food" (3.8) and "Let him acquire much food" (3.9), in the Müller translation.

The Chandogya Upanishad (7.26.2) links what one takes in to purity of mind: "When the intellectual aliment has been purified, the whole nature becomes purified" (Müller translation; traditional commentators differ on whether ahara means literal food or all that the senses take in).

Gluttony in this view is the misuse of something holy: eating without awareness, or taking more than the body needs, or hoarding it from others. Taittiriya 3.10 also ties food to generosity, praising the one who gives food to others.

This is related to gluttony and not identical to it: the texts speak of respect for food, not of a sin of overeating.

Christianity

Christian tradition calls it gluttony (Latin gula): excessive eating or drinking, and the disordered love of food and drink behind it. The Catechism names temperance as the virtue that moderates the attraction of pleasures (CCC 1809) and warns against excess in food and drink (CCC 2290).

The Bible warns against it often. Proverbs 23:20-21 says, "Do not be among winebibbers or among gluttonous eaters of meat; for the drunkard and the glutton will come to poverty." Philippians 3:19 speaks of those "their god is the belly." Luke 21:34 warns against "dissipation and drunkenness."

Aquinas (ST II-II q.148) says the fault lies in the disordered desire, and that food itself is good. Gregory the Great (Moralia 30.60) described five ways of gluttony, which Aquinas took up.

Buddhism

Buddhism treats it as lack of moderation in eating (bhojane mattannuta, "knowing the right amount in eating"), a quality the Buddha taught monks and nuns, and a form of sensual craving (kama-tanha).

The Maha-Assapura Sutta (MN 39) lists moderation in eating as part of training. The Kitagiri Sutta (MN 70) describes the Buddha giving up the evening meal and finding the benefits in health and ease. The Buddha also gave a lay example: in SN 3.13 (Doṇapāka Sutta) he advised King Pasenadi, who ate a large amount, to take a little less at each meal, and the king's health improved.

For lay followers, the standard guidance is simply to avoid excess, and eight-precept days include not eating after noon. This is a training rule, not a sin in the Christian sense.

Philosophical

Greek philosophers treat gluttony as a failure of temperance (sophrosyne), the virtue about bodily pleasures.

Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics (3.10-11), temperance is the mean about the pleasures of touch and taste. The excess is licentiousness, and he says that gluttony is the person who takes pleasure in food and drink beyond what is right. He notes that "belly-gods" (gastrimargoi) fill themselves beyond what is natural.

Plato. In the Republic (4, 430e-431d), temperance is a harmony in which the better part rules the appetites. In the Gorgias (493-494) appetite is likened to a leaky jar that can never be filled.

Epicurus. In the Letter to Menoeceus, simple food gives as much pleasure as luxurious food, and the pleasure of eating is complete when hunger is gone.
