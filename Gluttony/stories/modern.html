A story

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how over-indulgence works and how people step out of it.

Rohan's story

Rohan, 31, worked long hours as an analyst and ate his dinner at 10 p.m. in front of the laptop. It began as a quick takeaway. It became a second takeaway, then dessert, then a bag of snacks while he watched something. He never felt hungry exactly. He felt wound up, then flat, and the food took the edge off both.

He gained weight, felt sluggish, and hated the feeling of being stuffed. He would promise to "be good" on Monday, eat almost nothing all day, and by night eat twice as much.

The turning point was a check-up. His doctor did not lecture. She asked him to describe a normal evening. Saying it aloud, he heard it.

What helped:

  1. He kept a two-week log of what he ate and how he felt. The pattern was stress and the 10 p.m. hour, not hunger.
  2. He ate a real meal earlier, away from the screen.
  3. He added a 15-minute walk after work as his wind-down.
  4. He stopped the starve-and-binge cycle, with regular meals instead.
  5. He saw a dietitian and, later, a counsellor for the stress.
  6. He treated slips as data, not as proof he'd failed.

It took months. He lost some weight, but the bigger change was that food stopped being his only way to unwind.

What it shows: Over-eating is often about feelings, not food. Naming the trigger and meeting the need did more than willpower.
