A story

Socrates and the shared dinner

Xenophon's Memorabilia (3.14) records how Socrates dealt with a small problem at shared dinners. At such meals, some guests brought a small supply of food and others a large one. Socrates would tell the servants either to throw the small supplies into the common stock, or to give each guest an equal share all round.

The result, Xenophon says, was that the guests with large supplies were ashamed not to share in the common stock, and also ashamed not to put their own in. So their supplies went in with the rest. Now being no better off than the small contributors, they soon stopped buying expensive delicacies.

In the same chapter, at another supper, Socrates noticed a guest who had put aside the plain bread and was eating only the dainties (opson). He asked the company what makes a man a "dainty fellow" (opsophagos). They agreed that eating dainties with bread is not enough to earn the name, but that eating them with little or no plain food, only for pleasure, does.

Elsewhere (Memorabilia 1.3.5-6) Xenophon says Socrates ate with an appetite that was itself the sauce, so he needed no special seasoning to enjoy his food.

What it shows: Socrates used a quiet, practical move instead of a sermon. Moderation here is partly about fairness at the table, and partly about not needing luxury to enjoy food.
