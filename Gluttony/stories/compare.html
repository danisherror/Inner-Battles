Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how over-indulgence works and how people step out of it.

Rohan's story

Rohan, 31, worked long hours as an analyst and ate his dinner at 10 p.m. in front of the laptop. It began as a quick takeaway. It became a second takeaway, then dessert, then a bag of snacks while he watched something. He never felt hungry exactly. He felt wound up, then flat, and the food took the edge off both.

He gained weight, felt sluggish, and hated the feeling of being stuffed. He would promise to "be good" on Monday, eat almost nothing all day, and by night eat twice as much.

The turning point was a check-up. His doctor did not lecture. She asked him to describe a normal evening. Saying it aloud, he heard it.

What helped:

  1. He kept a two-week log of what he ate and how he felt. The pattern was stress and the 10 p.m. hour, not hunger.
  2. He ate a real meal earlier, away from the screen.
  3. He added a 15-minute walk after work as his wind-down.
  4. He stopped the starve-and-binge cycle, with regular meals instead.
  5. He saw a dietitian and, later, a counsellor for the stress.
  6. He treated slips as data, not as proof he'd failed.

It took months. He lost some weight, but the bigger change was that food stopped being his only way to unwind.

What it shows: Over-eating is often about feelings, not food. Naming the trigger and meeting the need did more than willpower.

Hinduism

Kumbhakarna

In the Ramayana, Kumbhakarna is the giant brother of the demon king Ravana. The tradition says that when he did severe penance, Brahma was about to grant him a boon, and the gods, fearing what he might ask, had the goddess Saraswati twist his tongue. Instead of nirdevatvam (a seat among the gods) he asked for nidravatvam (sleep). He was granted long sleep.

He slept for months, and in the story, when he was woken, he ate vast amounts: buffaloes, deer and drink, in huge quantities, before he could think.

When Ravana's war went badly, Kumbhakarna was woken and fed. He told Ravana plainly that he had been wrong to abduct Sita and should return her. But he said that as his brother he would fight. He did, and he died in battle against Rama.

What it shows: The story is a vivid picture of appetite and sleep ruling a person's life. It is related to gluttony and sloth together, not identical to either, and the texts do not frame it as a sermon on eating.

Sources

  • Valmiki Ramayana, Yuddha Kanda (external link), Kumbhakarna's awakening and death (overview)

Retold from memory. The Saraswati detail is in the traditional telling and in some versions of the epic; versions differ and chapter numbers were not checked.

Upanishads

Shvetaketu's fast

In Chandogya Upanishad 6.7, the teacher Uddalaka Aruni wants to show his son Shvetaketu a point about food and the mind.

He tells the boy to stay without eating for fifteen days, and to drink only water, since water is life-breath and will not be lost to a thirsty person. Shvetaketu does so, and then comes back to his father.

Uddalaka asks him to recite the Vedas he has learned. The boy says nothing comes: "I do not remember, sir."

Uddalaka explains with a fire. If a large fire is left with only a single ember the size of a firefly, it cannot burn much, but if you add straw it blazes up again. In the same way, of sixteen parts of the boy, only one is left, and with it he cannot recall the Vedas. "Eat," says his father. Shvetaketu eats, then recalls everything.

The teacher concludes: "Mind is made of food, breath of water, speech of fire."

What it shows: What and how we eat shape the mind. Starving is as unwise as overeating, and the aim is nourishment in right measure.

Sources

  • Chandogya Upanishad (external link) 6.5-6.7

Christianity

Esau's stew

Genesis 25:19-34 tells of twin brothers, Esau and Jacob. Esau, born first, was entitled to the birthright: the family leadership and a double share of the inheritance. He was a hunter, and Jacob was a quiet man who stayed among the tents.

One day Esau came in from the field, worn out. Jacob was cooking a red lentil stew. Esau said, "Let me eat some of that red stuff, for I am famished." Jacob answered, "First sell me your birthright."

Esau said, "I am about to die; what use is a birthright to me?" Jacob made him swear, and Esau sold his birthright for bread and lentil stew. The text says, "he ate and drank, and rose and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright."

Hebrews 12:16 later calls Esau "profane" for having sold his birthright "for a single meal."

What it shows: Hunger is not the sin. The fault is in letting the demand of the moment outweigh what matters most. Esau's "I am about to die" shows how appetite can make a loss feel small.

Sources

  • Hebrews 12:16 (external link)

Buddhism

King Pasenadi's meals

The Donapaka Sutta (SN 3.13) tells of King Pasenadi of Kosala, a patron of the Buddha. The king used to eat boiled rice by the bucketful. After one such meal he went up to the Buddha, huffing and puffing.

The Buddha, seeing this, spoke a verse: "When a man is always mindful, knowing moderation in eating, his discomfort fades, and he ages slowly, taking care of his life" (Sujato's translation).

The king did not follow it on his own. He asked the student Sudassana, who was standing behind him, to memorise the verse and recite it whenever a meal was served, and offered him a daily allowance for doing so.

The king gradually got used to having at most a cup of rice, and after some time his body slimmed right down. He then said: "In both ways the Buddha has sympathy for me: in the good of this life and the good of lives to come."

What it shows: The Buddha did not shame him. A short verse, someone to remind him and small steps worked better than a rule or a vow.

Philosophical

Socrates and the shared dinner

Xenophon's Memorabilia (3.14) records how Socrates dealt with a small problem at shared dinners. At such meals, some guests brought a small supply of food and others a large one. Socrates would tell the servants either to throw the small supplies into the common stock, or to give each guest an equal share all round.

The result, Xenophon says, was that the guests with large supplies were ashamed not to share in the common stock, and also ashamed not to put their own in. So their supplies went in with the rest. Now being no better off than the small contributors, they soon stopped buying expensive delicacies.

In the same chapter, at another supper, Socrates noticed a guest who had put aside the plain bread and was eating only the dainties (opson). He asked the company what makes a man a "dainty fellow" (opsophagos). They agreed that eating dainties with bread is not enough to earn the name, but that eating them with little or no plain food, only for pleasure, does.

Elsewhere (Memorabilia 1.3.5-6) Xenophon says Socrates ate with an appetite that was itself the sauce, so he needed no special seasoning to enjoy his food.

What it shows: Socrates used a quiet, practical move instead of a sermon. Moderation here is partly about fairness at the table, and partly about not needing luxury to enjoy food.

Sources

  • Xenophon, Memorabilia 3.14 and 1.3.5-6, in the Project Gutenberg translation (external link). Text read in this session.

Retold and shortened. The translation numbers the sections slightly differently from some editions, and the "appetite as sauce" line is a paraphrase of the passage in that translation.
