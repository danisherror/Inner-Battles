Modern

Over-indulgence has several well-studied roots.

  • Emotional regulation. People eat to soothe stress, boredom, loneliness or sadness. The food works for a few minutes, which trains the habit.
  • Food environment. Cheap, highly palatable, calorie-dense food is everywhere, and large portions shift what feels normal.
  • Reward and habit. Highly palatable foods engage reward circuits, and cues (a screen, a time of day) become triggers.
  • Restriction. Strict dieting can make bingeing more likely in some people.
  • Mindless eating. Distraction (TV, phone) reduces awareness of fullness.
  • Sleep and stress. Poor sleep and high stress change hunger hormones.

  • Wansink (2006), Mindless Eating (book). Some of Wansink's published studies were retracted for data problems, so rely on other research for the details.

Hinduism

Hindu thought traces over-indulgence of the senses to desire for pleasure and loss of mastery over the senses.

  • The senses are strong. Gita 2.60 says the turbulent senses can carry off the mind of even a wise person who is striving. 2.67 compares the mind that follows the roving senses to a wind carrying away a boat.
  • Dwelling on objects. Gita 2.62-63 gives the chain: thinking of sense objects leads to attachment, desire, anger, delusion, loss of memory and then ruin.
  • The guna of rajas and tamas. Gita 17.9-10 ties craving for pungent or heavy foods to rajas and the taste for stale or leftover food to tamas.
  • Craving the tongue's pleasure. Gita 2.59 says objects turn away from one who abstains, but the taste lingers, and falls away only on seeing the Highest.

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the source in outward-turned senses and the choice of the pleasant over the good.

  • Senses turned outward. Katha 2.1.1 says the Self-existent pierced the senses outward, so people look to what is outside and do not see the inner Self.
  • The chariot. Katha 1.3.3-6 compares the body to a chariot, the intellect to the driver and the mind to the reins. The senses are the horses. When the driver is untrained and the mind unsteady, the senses are like unruly horses.
  • Pleasant versus good. Katha 1.2.1-2: the unwise choose preyas, the pleasant.
  • Food shapes mind. Chandogya 6.5.4 says the mind is made of food, so what and how we eat shapes the mind.

Christianity

Christian writers find the root in disordered appetite and the longing to fill a hunger that food cannot meet.

  • Appetite over reason. Aquinas (ST II-II q.148) says the fault is in desire that goes against reason. Food is good, and the sin is in excess or in unruly desire.
  • The belly as god. Philippians 3:19 describes those whose "god is the belly."
  • Giving up what matters for pleasure. Esau trades his birthright for a bowl of stew (Genesis 25:29-34).
  • The first temptation. Genesis 3:6 tells how the fruit looked "good for food." The tempting of Jesus in the desert begins with "turn these stones into bread" (Matthew 4:3).
  • Deeper hunger. Matthew 4:4 answers, "One does not live by bread alone." Augustine's "our hearts are restless until they rest in You" (Confessions 1.1, Pilkington translation) names what food is sometimes asked to do.

Buddhism

Buddhism traces over-indulgence to craving, pleasure-seeking and inattention.

  • Craving for sense pleasure. The first sermon (SN 56.11) names kama-tanha as one of three cravings that drive suffering.
  • Lack of restraint of the senses. The standard training of "guarding the sense doors" (indriya-samvara, for example in MN 27) is taught because uncontrolled contact leads to desire.
  • Not knowing "enough." Bhojane mattannuta is listed as a quality to cultivate, and its absence leads to heaviness and sloth.
  • Eating to feel good. AN 3.39 (Sukhumala) shows how intoxication with comfort and health weakens restraint.
  • Inattention. The Buddha describes mindful reflection on food (MN 2 and MN 39) so that eating is not driven by taste alone.

Philosophical

Philosophers locate it in appetite untrained by reason, and habit.

  • Unruly appetite. Plato (Republic 4, 439d-441a) describes the appetitive part of the soul, which wants food, drink and sex, and which needs to be ruled by reason. In Phaedrus (253c-254e), appetite is the unruly horse of the soul.
  • Habit. Aristotle (NE 2.1-3) says that we become temperate or intemperate through the pleasures we repeatedly choose.
  • Pleasure as a trap. Aristotle (NE 3.10) says that people become intemperate because the pleasures of taste and touch are shared with animals and are easy to overdo.
  • Empty desires. Epicurus separates natural and necessary desires, such as plain food, from empty ones that have no limit.
