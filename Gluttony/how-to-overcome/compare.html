Gluttony

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is a calmer, more aware relationship with food and drink, not harsh dieting.

  1. Notice hunger and fullness. Rate hunger from 1 to 10 before you eat, and stop around comfortable.
  2. Eat without screens, slowly, and put the fork down between bites.
  3. Find your triggers. Track when and why you overeat for two weeks (stress, boredom, time of day).
  4. Meet the need. If it is stress, try a walk or a call. If it is tiredness, sleep.
  5. Shape the environment. Smaller plates, no stock of trigger foods at home, regular meals.
  6. Avoid all-or-nothing. Strict rules tend to backfire. After a slip, return to your next meal as planned.
  7. Get help when needed. A doctor, dietitian or therapist, especially if bingeing, purging or drinking is out of control.

Sources

  • Kristeller & Wolever (2011), "Mindfulness-based eating awareness training for treating binge eating disorder," Eating Disorders 19(1), 49-61. doi:10.1080/10640266.2011.533605 (external link)

Hinduism

Hindu practice answers it with moderation, sattvic food and fasting with purpose.

  • Yukta-ahara. Gita 6.17: moderate in eating and recreation, in action and in rest.
  • Sattvic food. Gita 17.8: foods that increase life, strength, health and cheer, and are tasty and nourishing.
  • Offer the food. Gita 3.13 says those who eat what is left after offering are freed from sin, and that those who cook only for themselves eat sin. Giving thanks and sharing before eating is a standard practice (prasada).
  • Fasting. Upavasa on set days (such as Ekadashi) trains self-control. It is traditionally done with a purpose, and the Gita (17.5-6) warns against severe, harmful austerity.
  • Sense control. Gita 2.58 compares the sage to a tortoise drawing in its limbs.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic response is to treat food with respect and to train the "reins" of the mind.

  • Honour food. Taittiriya 3.7-8: "Let him never abuse food... never shun food" (Müller translation). Eat with awareness and gratitude.
  • Share it. Taittiriya 3.10 praises giving food: "If he gives food amply, food is given to him amply."
  • Pure food, pure mind. Chandogya 7.26.2: "When the intellectual aliment has been purified, the whole nature becomes purified" (Müller translation). Traditional readings differ on whether ahara means literal food or all that the senses take in.
  • Drive the chariot. Katha 1.3.3-9: the one who has a skilled driver (a steady intellect) and holds the reins (the mind) reaches the end of the road.
  • Choose shreyas. Katha 1.2.1-2: choose the good over the merely pleasant.

Christianity

Christian practice answers gluttony with temperance, thanksgiving and fasting.

  • Temperance. Titus 2:12 and Galatians 5:23 name self-control as a fruit of the Spirit.
  • Give thanks. 1 Timothy 4:4-5 says everything created is good if received with thanksgiving. Grace before meals is a habit of gratitude.
  • Eat and drink for God's glory. 1 Corinthians 10:31.
  • Fasting. Jesus assumes fasting as a normal practice (Matthew 6:16-18). The Church keeps seasons such as Lent. Fasting is meant to teach freedom, not to punish.
  • Share the table. Luke 14:12-14 asks that feasts include the poor.
  • Know your limit. Proverbs 25:16: "If you have found honey, eat only enough for you."

Buddhism

Buddhism offers specific training in eating.

  • Reflect before you eat. MN 2 gives the monk's reflection: "Not for fun, indulgence, adornment, or decoration, but only to sustain this body, to avoid harm, and to support spiritual practice" (Sujato's translation).
  • Know the right amount. Bhojane mattannuta: stop while still able to eat a little more. SN 3.13 shows the king counselled to take a little less each day.
  • Mindfulness of eating. Notice taste, texture, hunger and satisfaction. Many teachers add eating slowly and in silence.
  • Guard the sense doors. Indriya-samvara, as in MN 27.
  • Eight precepts. On observance days, lay people refrain from food after noon, a temporary training and not a rule for everyone.
  • Generosity. Offering food (dana) puts the focus on giving.

Philosophical

Philosophy offers training in measure.

  • Hold to the mean. Aristotle (NE 3.11) says the temperate person desires what is right, as much as is right, and when it is right, and builds this by habit.
  • Start small. Epicurus (Letter to Menoeceus, 130-131): plain fare gives full pleasure once hunger is gone, and brings health.
  • Practise a little hardship. Seneca (Letters 18) suggests days of simple food, to know that one can do with little.
  • Eat as a guest at a banquet. Epictetus (Enchiridion 15) says to take the dish as it comes, and to wait and not grab, as at a polite dinner.
  • Reason rules appetite. Plato's Republic 4 describes temperance as harmony, with reason leading the appetites.
