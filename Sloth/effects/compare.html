Deadly sin

Sloth

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Chronic avoidance costs more than the task you put off.

  • Stress and guilt. Procrastinators report higher stress and worse well-being on average, as the work and the worry build up.
  • Health. Studies link procrastination with poorer sleep and delay in seeking medical care.
  • Work and money. Missed deadlines, lower performance and late bills.
  • Relationships. Others carry what you leave undone, which breeds resentment.
  • Lost growth. Skills and goals stall, and regret about unlived years is common.
  • Spiral. Shame about avoiding makes the next task feel worse, which feeds more avoidance.

Sources

  • Sirois (2014), "Procrastination and stress: exploring the role of self-compassion," Self and Identity 13(2), 128-145. doi:10.1080/15298868.2013.763404 (external link)

Hinduism

The Gita describes where tamas leads.

  • Darkness and delusion. Gita 14.13: when tamas prevails, there is darkness, inertia, heedlessness and delusion.
  • Downward path. Gita 14.18: those in tamas go downward. 14.16-17 say that the fruit of tamas is ignorance, and that ignorance and delusion arise from it.
  • Hollow happiness. Gita 18.39: the happiness of tamas deludes from beginning to end, arising from sleep, sloth and heedlessness.
  • Duty undone. Gita 3.8 and 3.20 stress that action is better than inaction and that even keeping the body going depends on action. 3.16 says one who does not turn the wheel of action "lives in vain."
  • Obstacle to yoga. Yoga Sutras 1.30-31: laziness is an obstacle that brings distress.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe the cost of heedlessness as missing the goal.

  • Missing the Self. Mundaka 3.2.4: the Self cannot be gained by the heedless or the weak.
  • Staying asleep. Katha 1.3.14: "Arise, awake!" The cost of not waking is staying on the wrong path.
  • Wandering. Katha 1.2.5-6: the ignorant go round and round, and the one who thinks "this world is all there is" falls under death's sway again and again.
  • A wasted chance. Kena 2.5 says that if one knows here, then there is truth; if one does not know here, there is great loss. The human birth is the chance to know.
  • Neglected duties. Taittiriya 1.11: do not be heedless about study, teaching, truth and dharma.

Christianity

Scripture and tradition list sloth's costs.

  • Poverty. Proverbs 6:9-11: "A little sleep, a little slumber... and poverty will come upon you like a robber." Proverbs 10:4: "A slack hand causes poverty, but the hand of the diligent makes rich."
  • Unfinished work. Proverbs 12:24 says diligent hands will rule, while the lazy will be put to forced labour.
  • A buried talent. In Matthew 25:24-30, the servant who hid his master's money out of fear loses what he had.
  • Burden on others. 2 Thessalonians 3:10-12 asks those who live in idleness to earn their own bread.
  • Despair. The desert fathers saw acedia as leading to despair and abandoning the call of one's life.
  • Neglected love. Matthew 25:41-45 shows judgment on those who did nothing for the hungry, strangers and sick.

Buddhism

Sloth and laziness cost progress and bring regret.

  • No path. Dhammapada 280: the idle one, young and strong but not rising, does not find the way by wisdom.
  • Blocks meditation. Sloth and torpor cloud the mind and make concentration impossible.
  • Lost opportunity. The Buddha's final advice was to strive on with diligence (DN 16). Life is short and the chance is rare.
  • Slipping back. Dhammapada 168-169 urges one to rise up and not be heedless, and says the heedless suffer.
  • Heedlessness is a path to death. Dhammapada 21: heedfulness (appamada) is the way to the deathless, and heedlessness the way to death.

Philosophical

Philosophers describe the cost as a life unlived.

  • Wasted life. Seneca (On the Shortness of Life 1-3): life is long enough if you use it well, but it is wasted by those who put off and neglect it.
  • Lost happiness. Aristotle (NE 1.7-8) argues that happiness lies in virtuous activity. A life of idle ease achieves none of it.
  • Weakened will. Aristotle (NE 2.1): idleness repeated becomes character.
  • Unrealised talents. Kant (Groundwork 4:423) says a rational being cannot will that talents be left to rust.
  • Lost duty to others. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 5.1) reminds himself that he is made for work, as bees are, and for the common good.
