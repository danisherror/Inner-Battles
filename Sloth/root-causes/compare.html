Modern

Sloth rarely comes from simple "laziness". Common roots are:

  • Avoiding discomfort. Tasks that cause anxiety, boredom or fear of failure get postponed to feel better now.
  • Present bias. Humans tend to prefer immediate reward over later benefit, so future costs feel small.
  • Perfectionism and fear of failure. If you might not do it well, not starting protects your self-image.
  • Unclear or overwhelming goals. A big, vague task is hard to begin.
  • Low energy. Poor sleep, inactivity, diet, and mental-health conditions such as depression reduce drive.
  • Distraction. Phones and feeds offer instant, easy rewards.
  • Learned helplessness. After repeated failure or lack of control, people stop trying.

Hinduism

Hindu thought traces sloth to tamas, the guna of inertia and ignorance.

  • Born of ignorance. Gita 14.8 says that tamas arises from ignorance and deludes all embodied beings through heedlessness, laziness and sleep.
  • Rising and falling gunas. Gita 14.10: sometimes sattva prevails over the other two, sometimes rajas, sometimes tamas. Habit and food, company and activity shift the balance.
  • Food and environment. Gita 17.10 describes stale, tasteless, rotten and impure food as the food of tamas.
  • Delusion about duty. Gita 18.32 says tamasic understanding sees wrong as right, covered in darkness. Gita 3.4-8 argues no one can avoid action, so refusing to act is itself self-deception.
  • Fear and attachment. In Gita 1-2, Arjuna's despondency before the battle shows sloth as a failure of nerve.

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the cause in heedlessness (pramada) and ignorance.

  • Heedlessness. Mundaka 3.2.4 names pramada as one of the ways the Self is missed.
  • Ignorance as sleep. Katha 1.3.14 says "Arise, awake," which implies that ordinary life is a kind of sleep.
  • Choosing the pleasant. Katha 1.2.1-2: the unwise choose the pleasant (preyas), which includes ease and comfort, over the good (shreyas).
  • Not seeing the aim. Katha 1.2.5: living in ignorance, people go round like the blind led by the blind. Without a goal, there is nothing to rouse effort.
  • Lack of strength. Mundaka 3.2.4 says the Self is not gained by one lacking strength (balahina), which links effort to inner vigour.

Sources

  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) 3.2.4
  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.2.1-2, 1.2.5, 1.3.14

Christianity

Christian writers find sloth's roots in weariness of spirit, loss of hope and misplaced love.

  • Noonday demon. Evagrius describes acedia as a restlessness at midday that makes the day seem endless, the place unbearable and the work pointless. It arises from boredom and from the temptation to want to be elsewhere.
  • Sorrow about the good. Aquinas (ST II-II q.35) says acedia is sorrow at spiritual good, because it demands effort.
  • Discouragement. Galatians 6:9 urges, "Let us not grow weary in doing what is right," and Hebrews 12:3 says to consider Christ "so that you may not grow weary in your souls or lose heart."
  • Love grown cold. Matthew 24:12 speaks of love growing cold. Revelation 3:15-16 criticises being "neither cold nor hot."
  • Fear. In the parable of the talents (Matthew 25:24-25), the third servant says, "I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground."

Buddhism

Buddhism traces sloth and torpor to unwholesome conditions that can be changed.

  • Heavy body and mind. The suttas list causes of the hindrance: discontent, sloth, yawning, sleepiness after eating and mental sluggishness, which "fuel the arising of dullness and drowsiness" (SN 46.51, Sujato's translation).
  • Lack of energy and interest. Kosajja arises where there is no vision of the goal. The Buddha speaks of "unwise attention" feeding the hindrances.
  • Over-comfort. AN 3.39 (Sukhumala) describes the intoxication of health and ease making people careless.
  • Wrong view. Fatalist views that deny the point of effort were rejected by the Buddha (AN 3.61, the Sectarian Doctrines).
  • Aversion to effort. Avoiding the discomfort of practice.

Philosophical

Philosophers find the cause of sloth in weak will, the pull of immediate pleasure and lack of purpose.

  • Akrasia. Aristotle (NE 7.3) describes the person who knows the better course but is overcome by appetite or feeling.
  • Habit. Aristotle (NE 2.1) says we become what we repeatedly do, so idleness grows by repetition.
  • Pleasure of ease. Seneca (Letters 82) says that leisure without philosophy is a kind of death, and that comfort makes the mind soft.
  • Wasted time. Seneca (On the Shortness of Life 9) sees people postponing the start of living: "postponement is the greatest waste of life."
  • Fear of failure. Kant (Metaphysics of Morals, Doctrine of Virtue) treats neglect of one's natural powers as a failing in duty to oneself.
