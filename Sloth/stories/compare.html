Deadly sin

Sloth

A story, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how sloth works and how people step out of it.

Nisha's story

Nisha was in the last year of a master's degree with a thesis due in four months. She told herself she would start properly on Monday. Monday became next Monday. She tidied her desk, reorganised her notes, watched "one" video that turned into five, and told herself she worked better under pressure.

The weeks passed. The thesis felt bigger each day, and the bigger it felt, the more she avoided it. She stopped replying to her supervisor. At night she lay awake feeling guilty, and the next day the guilt made her want to hide more.

The turning point was an email from her supervisor, short and kind: "Send me anything, even a page, and we'll go from there."

What helped:

  1. She checked her health. She was sleeping badly, and a doctor's visit confirmed she was low on energy and mood. She started treatment for it.
  2. She cut the task down. The goal for the day was one paragraph, written before opening any messages.
  3. She sent the supervisor a rough page, which was bad, and got useful comments.
  4. She set a fixed hour (9 to 10 a.m.) at the library, with the phone in her bag.
  5. She dropped the self-attack, and wrote down what she had done each day, not what she hadn't.

She did not finish early, but she finished on time and the work was fine.

What it shows: Sloth often hides fear and exhaustion. A small first step and some kindness did more than a strict resolve.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If low mood lasts, please see a doctor.

Hinduism

Arjuna's despondency

The Bhagavad Gita begins on the battlefield of Kurukshetra, with two armies ready. Arjuna, the Pandava warrior, asks his charioteer Krishna to drive between the armies so he can see who he must fight.

He sees teachers, uncles, cousins and friends on the other side. His limbs fail, his mouth goes dry, his body trembles and his bow slips from his hand (Gita 1.28-30). He says that he sees no good in killing his kin, and that he does not want victory or a kingdom. He sits down in the chariot, throws down his bow and, as the text says, "his mind overwhelmed with grief" (1.47).

In chapter 2, Krishna's first words rebuke him: "Whence has this weakness (klaibya) come upon you in a time of crisis? Do not yield to it. It does not suit you. Give up this petty weakness of heart and arise" (2.2-3).

The rest of the Gita is Krishna's teaching on duty, action without attachment and the Self.

What it shows: Arjuna's refusal to act is not laziness in the usual sense, but it is the same inertia of tamas: freezing in front of what must be done. Krishna's answer is a call to arise and act with the right understanding.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 1.28-30, 1.47 (external link)
  • Bhagavad Gita 2.2-3 (external link)

Retold from memory; the quoted lines are paraphrases. Reading Arjuna's despondency as an illustration of tamas is an interpretation, and many commentators see it differently, for example as a moral crisis.

Upanishads

Satyakama and the cattle

In Chandogya Upanishad 4.4-9, a boy named Satyakama asks his mother Jabala who his father was, as he wishes to become a student. She tells him she does not know, as she was a servant who moved from house to house in her youth. "Tell the teacher your name is Satyakama Jabala," she says.

He goes to the sage Gautama and says truthfully that he doesn't know his father. Gautama is struck by his honesty: "Only a true Brahmin could say that," and accepts him.

Gautama gives him four hundred lean, weak cows and tells him to take them away and not come back until they are a thousand. Satyakama says, "I will not return until they number a thousand," and lives with them in the forest for years.

When the herd reaches a thousand, a bull, then fire, a swan and a diving bird each speak to him in turn, teaching him a part of Brahman. When he returns, his teacher sees his face shine and says, "You shine like one who knows Brahman." The teacher then teaches him the rest.

What it shows: This is the opposite of sloth: long, patient, unrewarded work done for a goal and a promise. Learning arrived through the effort.

Sources

  • Chandogya Upanishad (external link) 4.4-4.9

Christianity

Jonah runs away

The Book of Jonah begins with God telling the prophet Jonah to go to Nineveh and preach against its wickedness. Nineveh was the capital of Assyria, a feared enemy. Jonah got up, but to run: he went to Joppa and boarded a ship for Tarshish, "away from the presence of the Lord" (Jonah 1:3).

A great storm came, and the sailors were terrified and threw cargo overboard. And where was Jonah? "Jonah had gone down into the hold of the ship and had lain down and was fast asleep" (1:5). The captain woke him and told him to call on his God.

Jonah told the sailors the storm was his fault and asked to be thrown overboard. They did so reluctantly, and the sea calmed. A large fish swallowed him, and in its belly, after three days, he prayed and was released.

God told him again to go to Nineveh. This time he went and preached, and the city repented. Even so, Jonah was angry that God showed mercy (Jonah 4).

What it shows: The first move of avoidance is to flee from what one is called to do, and sleep through the storm. The story shows that the call does not go away, and that the second try is possible.

Sources

  • Jonah 1-4 (external link)

Buddhism

Moggallana nods off

The Pacalayamana Sutta (AN 7.61) tells how the Buddha's chief disciple in psychic powers, Maha Moggallana, was sitting in meditation near the village of Kallavalamutta in Magadha, and kept nodding with drowsiness.

The Buddha saw this with his divine eye, appeared before him, and asked: "Are you nodding off, Moggallana? Are you nodding off?" "Yes, sir," said Moggallana.

The Buddha then gave him a series of remedies, in order. If drowsiness comes while you are attending to a particular perception, don't give attention to it. If it persists, think over and examine the Dhamma you have learned. If that fails, recite it aloud. If that fails, pull your earlobes and rub your limbs. If that fails, get up and wash your eyes with water and look at the sky and stars. If that fails, attend to the perception of light, by day and by night. If that fails, walk back and forth, with senses turned inward. And finally, if that fails, lie down on the right side, mindful, focused on the time of getting up.

On waking he should get up quickly, thinking that he will not live indulging in the pleasures of sleeping, lying down and drowsing.

What it shows: Even a great disciple gets sleepy. The Buddha's answer is practical, a ladder of remedies, with no shame attached.

Sources

  • AN 7.61, Pacalayamana Sutta (external link)

The sutta number, the exchange and the order of the remedies were checked against Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API (suttacentral.net (external link)) on 2026-11-14; the remedies are paraphrased and shortened.

Philosophical

Heracles at the crossroads

Xenophon's Memorabilia (2.1.21-34) records a story that Socrates says he heard from the sophist Prodicus. When the young Heracles was about to enter manhood, he went to a quiet place and sat wondering which road in life to take. Two tall women came toward him.

One was Vice, plump and made-up, who called herself Happiness. She offered him an easy road to happiness without toil: "I will guide you by a short and easy road to happiness" (Dakyns translation).

The other was Virtue, simply dressed, with a quiet face. She told him that "not one" of the lovely things has been given to mortals "apart from toil and pains": to win the favour of the gods he must serve them, to be loved by friends he must benefit them, and to have the earth yield fruit he must tend it (Dakyns translation). She said that Vice's pleasures were not satisfying, since those who take them before they are hungry or tired are never really content.

Xenophon, as Socrates reports it, breaks off with Virtue's speech and does not record which road Heracles took.

What it shows: Sloth is the first woman's offer: comfort now. Virtue's reply is that anything worth having is bought with effort.

Sources

  • Xenophon, Memorabilia 2.1.21-34. English translations are on Project Gutenberg (external link) (translation by Dakyns).

Retold and shortened from memory; Xenophon puts the story in Prodicus's mouth, and wording is paraphrased. The passage was checked in the Gutenberg text, where the speech is attributed to Prodicus as Socrates reports it.
