Modern

Sloth is neglecting what matters, out of laziness, apathy or avoidance. It is not the same as resting. Rest restores you. Sloth is putting off or skipping the things you know you should do for yourself and others, until the neglect becomes a habit.

Modern terms for the pieces are:

  • Procrastination, delaying a task despite expecting to be worse off for it.
  • Avoidance, steering away from things that cause discomfort.
  • Apathy, a loss of care or motivation.
  • Learned helplessness, giving up because past effort seemed pointless.

Sloth also differs from depression, which is a medical condition that can look like laziness (low energy, no motivation) but is not a moral failing.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word equal to sloth, but the closest ideas are alasya (laziness) and tamas, the quality of inertia, dullness and heaviness, one of the three gunas.

The Gita describes tamas in several places. In 14.8, it is born of ignorance and binds through heedlessness (pramada), laziness (alasya) and sleep (nidra). In 18.28, a tamasic doer is "unsteady, vulgar, stubborn, deceitful, malicious, lazy (alasa), despondent and procrastinating (dirgha-sutri)." And 18.39 describes a tamasic happiness that comes from sleep, laziness and heedlessness.

Yoga Sutras 1.30 names alasya (laziness, heaviness of body and mind) among the nine obstacles to yoga.

This is related to sloth and not identical to it: Hinduism sees it as a guna to be balanced, not as a sin.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not list laziness as a sin. They treat it as the opposite of the effort the path demands, and as a form of the heedlessness (pramada) that keeps the seeker asleep.

  • Wake up. Katha 1.3.14 says: "Arise, awake! Having obtained your boons, understand them. The path is sharp as a razor's edge, hard to cross, say the wise."
  • Strength needed. Mundaka 3.2.4 says "This Self cannot be gained by one without strength, nor through heedlessness (pramada), nor by austerity without the right aim."
  • Neglect of duty to learn and teach. Taittiriya 1.11 tells the graduate not to neglect study and teaching (svadhyaya-pravacanabhyam na pramaditavyam), nor truth, dharma, welfare, prosperity or duties to gods and ancestors.

The theme is related to sloth and not identical to it: the texts speak of heedlessness, not of a sin of laziness.

Christianity

Christian tradition calls it sloth (Latin acedia, from Greek akedia, "lack of care"). It began as a spiritual condition described by the desert monk Evagrius Ponticus as the "noonday demon": restlessness, boredom and distaste for one's work and prayer. Later it broadened to include laziness and neglect of duty.

The Catechism (CCC 2733) describes acedia as a temptation to which presumption opens the gate: "a form of depression due to lax ascetical practice, decreasing vigilance, carelessness of heart." Aquinas (ST II-II q.35) defines it as sorrow about spiritual good: a sadness or weariness about doing what one ought.

The Bible has plenty on idleness. Proverbs 6:6-11 tells the sluggard to learn from the ant. Proverbs 26:14 says "As a door turns on its hinges, so does a lazy person in bed." Romans 12:11 says, "Do not lag in zeal." 2 Thessalonians 3:10 says, "Anyone unwilling to work should not eat."

Buddhism

The Pali words are thina-middha, sloth and torpor, the third of the five hindrances to meditation, and kosajja, laziness, whose opposite is viriya, energy or effort.

Thina is sluggishness of mind and middha is heaviness or drowsiness. They are described as a mental state, which can have causes such as heavy eating, and can be dealt with.

The Buddha's last words, in the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (DN 16), were: "Conditions fall apart. Persist with diligence" (appamadena sampadetha, Sujato's translation, DN 16 at 6.7.3).

Dhammapada 280 says that one who does not rise when it is time to rise, and who is young and strong but given to idleness, with a mind full of sluggish thoughts, will not find the way by wisdom.

Philosophical

Philosophers discuss sloth as a failure to act on what one knows is good, and as waste of the life one has.

Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics (7.1-10) he describes akrasia, weakness of will: knowing the better and doing otherwise. In the Politics and the Ethics he argues that happiness is an activity of the soul in line with virtue (NE 1.7), not a state of rest.

Seneca. In On the Shortness of Life (1) he writes: "It is not that we have a short space of time, but that we waste much of it."

Marcus Aurelius. In the Meditations (5.1) he scolds himself for lying in bed in the morning: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being" (Long translation).

Kant. In the Groundwork (4:423), he argues that a rational being cannot will universal neglect of talents, so cultivating them is a duty to oneself.
