Deadly sin

Sloth

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is to make starting easy, and to be kind to yourself while you do.

  1. Rule out health causes. Check sleep, food, movement and mood. If low energy lasts for weeks, see a doctor, since depression is not laziness.
  2. Shrink the first step. Commit to five minutes. Starting is the hard part.
  3. Make the task clear. Break it into steps and write the very next action.
  4. Set a time and place ("after coffee, at the desk"), so you decide once.
  5. Remove distractions. Phone in another room, blockers on feeds.
  6. Reward progress with something small after you finish.
  7. Use self-compassion, not self-attack. Studies find that forgiving yourself for past procrastination makes it less likely next time.
  8. Add accountability. Tell a friend, or work alongside someone.

Sources

  • Wohl, Pychyl & Bennett (2010), "I forgive myself, now I can study," Personality and Individual Differences 48(7), 803-808. doi:10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029 (external link)
  • Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis," Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 69-119. doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 (external link)

Steps are general advice, not a tested programme. Citations were from memory and not re-checked. This is not medical advice.

Hinduism

Hindu practice answers tamas by raising rajas and then sattva, and by acting without clinging to results.

  • Act. Gita 3.8: "Perform your allotted duty, for action is better than inaction."
  • Karma yoga. Gita 2.47-48: do your work without attachment to the result, and stay steady in success and failure. This frees you from the fear that stops many people starting.
  • Lift yourself. Gita 6.5: "Let a person lift themselves by their own self; let them not degrade themselves."
  • Moderation. Gita 6.16-17: not too much sleep or too little, not too much food or too little, with balanced effort.
  • Sattvic food and routine. Gita 17.8 and the tradition of an early waking time (brahma-muhurta).
  • Practice and detachment. Yoga Sutras 1.12-14: steady, long and devoted practice.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic call is to wake up and make effort.

  • Arise, awake. Katha 1.3.14 is the classic call: get up, find a teacher and understand.
  • Gather strength. Mundaka 3.2.4: the Self is not for the weak or the heedless. Cultivate vigour through discipline.
  • Keep to study and duty. Taittiriya 1.11: do not neglect study and teaching, and do your duties.
  • Choose the good. Katha 1.2.1-2: choose shreyas over preyas, which means doing the harder, worthwhile thing.
  • Hold the reins. Katha 1.3.3-9: let the driver (intellect) hold the reins and the senses will follow.
  • Seek the teacher. Mundaka 1.2.12 says to go to a teacher with fuel in hand, ready for service and study.

Christianity

Christian practice answers sloth with work as service, perseverance and hope.

  • Learn from the ant. Proverbs 6:6-8.
  • Work as service. Colossians 3:23: "Whatever task you must do, work as if your soul depends on it, as for the Lord and not for humans."
  • Do not grow weary. Galatians 6:9 and Hebrews 12:1-3: run with perseverance.
  • Stay in your cell. The desert fathers advised, against the noonday demon, to stay and keep working and praying (Evagrius, Praktikos 12, and a saying attributed to Abba Moses that the cell itself will teach the monk everything; this saying was not checked against a specific edition of the Sayings of the Desert Fathers).
  • Rhythm. Benedict's Rule gives a daily order of prayer and work (ora et labora).
  • Rest too. Sabbath (Exodus 20:8-11) is built in, so diligence is not overwork.
  • Ask for help. Philippians 4:13 and prayer.

Buddhism

Buddhism treats sloth as a hindrance with practical remedies.

  • Cultivate energy (viriya). It is one of the five spiritual faculties and one of the seven factors of awakening, and the Buddha urged diligence (appamada) in his last words (DN 16).
  • Specific remedies. The suttas and commentaries suggest: change posture, walk, splash water on the face, look at light, reflect on the Dhamma, and avoid overeating (as in AN 7.61, Pacalayamana Sutta, on the monk nodding).
  • Right effort. The four efforts: prevent unwholesome states, abandon those that have arisen, develop wholesome states, maintain them (AN 4.13 and the eightfold path).
  • Reflect on urgency. Death is certain and the time is unknown (AN 6.19-20, mindfulness of death).
  • Friends. Good company (kalyana-mitta) is "the whole of the spiritual life" (SN 45.2, Sujato's translation).

Philosophical

Philosophy offers habits that make action easier.

  • Remember why you are here. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 5.1): when you struggle to get up in the morning, tell yourself, "I am rising to the work of a human being."
  • Use your time. Seneca (On the Shortness of Life 3, 9): don't postpone, and live as if you will not have tomorrow.
  • Build habits. Aristotle (NE 2.1-3): we become diligent by doing diligent things. Start with small repeated acts.
  • Train will. Aristotle (NE 7.7-8) says the weak-willed can improve by foreseeing temptation and planning around it.
  • Make it a duty. Kant (Groundwork 4:423) treats developing your talents as a duty to yourself.
  • Daily review. Seneca (On Anger 3.36) and Epictetus (Discourses 3.10) recommend reviewing the day.
