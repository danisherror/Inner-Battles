Impulsivity

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how impulsivity works and how people loosen its grip.

Reza's story

Reza had been saving for a deposit on a flat. Late one night, tired and a little low after a hard day, he saw a flash sale on a camera and bought it before he had finished reading the page. The next morning the receipt was in his inbox and the feeling was already gone.

It was not the first time. Most of his impulse buys were late at night and with a saved card.

What helped:

  1. He noticed the pattern. Looking back, nearly every purchase came after 10 p.m., on a phone, with a card already saved.
  2. He changed the situation. He deleted the saved card and logged out of the shops.
  3. He added a delay. Anything he wanted went on a list, and he looked at the list again after a day.
  4. He made an if-then plan. "If I feel the urge to buy at night, then I put it on the list and go to bed."
  5. He made the later reward real. He set up an automatic transfer to the deposit account on payday and checked the balance monthly.
  6. He noticed it was not lifting. After a few months the late-night buying had eased, but a run of rash decisions elsewhere made him speak to his doctor, who referred him to a counsellor.

The urges did not vanish. They got easier to wait out, and the deposit grew.

What it shows: Reza changed what was in front of him and gave the later reward a plan, instead of relying on feeling strong at midnight.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If impulsive behaviour is harming your money, safety or relationships, please see a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Arjuna admits the mind is hard to hold

In Chapter 6 of the Gita, Arjuna tells Krishna honestly what he finds: "The mind is very restless, turbulent, strong and obstinate, O Krishna. It appears to me that it is more difficult to control than the wind" (6.34).

Krishna does not argue: "what you say is correct; the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled" (6.35). He adds: "Yog is difficult to attain for one whose mind is unbridled. However, those who have learnt to control the mind, and who strive earnestly by proper means, can attain perfection in Yog" (6.36).

What it shows: Arjuna names the difficulty first, and Krishna agrees with him before giving the way. The passage is about yoga and the mind and not about impulsivity as such; the link is ours: the answer to an unruly mind is neither shame nor force, but practice.

Upanishads

Indra and Virochana

In the Chandogya Upanishad, the god Indra and the demon Virochana both go to Prajapati to learn about the Self. "They dwelt there as pupils for thirty-two years." Prajapati tells them: "The person that is seen in the eye, that is the Self." They look into a pan of water and see their own reflections, and Prajapati says: "That is the Self, this is the immortal, the fearless, this is Brahman." "Then both went away satisfied in their hearts."

Prajapati, watching them go, says that they leave "without having perceived and without having known the Self". Virochana, "satisfied in his heart", goes to the demons and teaches that the body is the self.

But Indra "saw this difficulty" before he reached the gods: if the self is the reflection of the body, it would be blind if the body were blind, and would perish when the body perished. "Therefore I see no good in this." He goes back to Prajapati, who tells him to stay another thirty-two years. Twice more he finds a difficulty and returns, and the last stay is five years. "This made in all one hundred and one years."

What it shows: Both pupils were handed an answer that satisfied them at once. One took it and left, and the other went back for more, and waited over a hundred years. The passage is about knowledge of the Self and not about impulsivity; the link to delayed gratification is ours.

Sources

  • Chandogya Upanishad (external link) 8.7-8.12 (Max Müller's translation)

Christianity

Esau and the stew

In Genesis 25 Esau comes in from the field, "famished". His brother Jacob is cooking. Esau says: "Let me eat some of that red stuff, for I am famished!"

Jacob answers: "First sell me your birthright." Esau says: "I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?" Jacob makes him swear, and "he swore to him and sold his birthright to Jacob."

"Then Jacob gave Esau bread and lentil stew, and he ate and drank and rose and went his way. Thus Esau despised his birthright" (25:29-34).

What it shows: Esau trades something lasting for something quick, and the verse ends with a verdict and no speech about it. The story is about a family and a birthright and not about impulsivity as such; the link is ours: the need of the moment crowded out the later good. Jacob's part in the exchange is not examined here.

Buddhism

The reckless monkey

In SN 47.7 the Buddha tells of monkeys in the Himalayas and hunters who "set snares of tar on the monkey trails". The wary monkeys "see the tar and avoid it from afar. But a foolish and reckless monkey goes up to the tar and grabs it with a hand. He gets stuck there."

"Thinking to free his hand, he grabs it with his other hand. He gets stuck there." He does the same with a foot, the other foot and his snout. "And so the monkey, trapped at five points, just lies there screeching. He'd meet with tragedy and disaster, and the hunter can do what he wants with him."

The Buddha ends: "So, mendicants, don't roam out of your own territory into the domain of others."

What it shows: The monkey does not stop after the first mistake; each attempt to free himself traps another limb. The sutta is a parable about sense pleasures and mindfulness and not about impulsivity as such; the link is ours: acting without pausing can turn one slip into five.

Philosophical

Leontius and the bodies

In Plato's Republic (Book 4), Socrates tells a story he has heard. "Leontius, the son of Aglaion, coming up one day from the Piraeus, under the north wall on the outside, observed some dead bodies lying on the ground at the place of execution. He felt a desire to see them, and also a dread and abhorrence of them; for a time he struggled and covered his eyes, but at length the desire got the better of him; and forcing them open, he ran up to the dead bodies, saying, Look, ye wretches, take your fill of the fair sight."

Socrates draws the moral: "anger at times goes to war with desire, as though they were two distinct things". In such a struggle a person "reviles himself, and is angry at the violence within him".

What it shows: Leontius wants something and also does not want it, struggles for a while, and gives way, and then turns on himself. The story is about the parts of the soul and not about impulsivity as such; the link is ours: the struggle is real, and the moment of giving way is followed by anger at oneself.
