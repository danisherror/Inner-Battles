The research read here favours changing the situation and planning ahead over relying on willpower in the moment.

1. **Change the situation, not just yourself.** Duckworth, Gendler and Gross describe "proactively choosing or changing situations in ways that weaken undesirable impulses or potentiate desirable ones", such as "the student who goes to the library without a cell phone". They argue that "impulses tend to grow stronger over time", so situational strategies "can nip a tempting impulse in the bud".
2. **Make an if-then plan.** Wang and colleagues' meta-analysis of 21 studies found that mental contrasting with implementation intentions (picturing the goal, the obstacle, and a plan for when it appears) "is effective for goal attainment with a small to medium effect size (g = 0.336)", though "because of some publication bias, the actual effect sizes may be smaller".
3. **Add a delay.** Waiting a few minutes before acting is common advice. Hofmann and colleagues found urges "tend to elicit resistance, with uneven success", so a pause gives that resistance time to work. That second point is our reading.
4. **Notice who is around.** The same study found that "the presence of others who already had enacted the desire in question" mattered. Choosing company is a situational strategy.
5. **Keep your promises to yourself.** Kidd and colleagues found children waited longer when waiting was shown to pay off. Making later rewards reliable is our reading.
6. **Get help if it is harming you.** A doctor or therapist can help when impulsive spending, drinking, gambling or outbursts are costing you money, safety or relationships.
