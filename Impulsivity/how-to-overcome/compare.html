Impulsivity

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here favours changing the situation and planning ahead over relying on willpower in the moment.

  1. Change the situation, not just yourself. Duckworth, Gendler and Gross describe "proactively choosing or changing situations in ways that weaken undesirable impulses or potentiate desirable ones", such as "the student who goes to the library without a cell phone". They argue that "impulses tend to grow stronger over time", so situational strategies "can nip a tempting impulse in the bud".
  2. Make an if-then plan. Wang and colleagues' meta-analysis of 21 studies found that mental contrasting with implementation intentions (picturing the goal, the obstacle, and a plan for when it appears) "is effective for goal attainment with a small to medium effect size (g = 0.336)", though "because of some publication bias, the actual effect sizes may be smaller".
  3. Add a delay. Waiting a few minutes before acting is common advice. Hofmann and colleagues found urges "tend to elicit resistance, with uneven success", so a pause gives that resistance time to work. That second point is our reading.
  4. Notice who is around. The same study found that "the presence of others who already had enacted the desire in question" mattered. Choosing company is a situational strategy.
  5. Keep your promises to yourself. Kidd and colleagues found children waited longer when waiting was shown to pay off. Making later rewards reliable is our reading.
  6. Get help if it is harming you. A doctor or therapist can help when impulsive spending, drinking, gambling or outbursts are costing you money, safety or relationships.

Sources

  • Duckworth, Gendler and Gross (2016), "Situational strategies for self-control," Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(1), 35-55. doi:10.1177/1745691615623247 (external link)
  • Wang, Wang and Gai (2021), "A meta-analysis of the effects of mental contrasting with implementation intentions on goal attainment," Frontiers in Psychology 12, 565202. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202 (external link)
  • Hofmann et al. (2012), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 102(6), 1318-1335. doi:10.1037/a0026545 (external link)
  • Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin (2013), Cognition 126(1), 109-114. doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2012.08.004 (external link)

All were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The abstract of Wang et al. describes mental contrasting only by its name; the parenthesis is a standard summary. Steps 3 and 5 are our reading, and step 6 is common advice.

Hinduism

The Gita points to taking control at the start, to practice, and to the happiness that is bitter first and sweet in the end.

  1. Act at the beginning. Gita 3.41: "Therefore, O best of the Bharatas, in the very beginning bring the senses under control and slay this enemy called desire, which is the embodiment of sin and destroys knowledge and realization."
  2. Practise and let go. Gita 6.35: "the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled."
  3. Prefer the long sweetness. Gita 18.37: "That which seems like poison at first, but tastes like nectar in the end, is said to be happiness in the mode of goodness."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses are about restraining the senses and the mind in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for impulsive behaviour that is causing harm. Reading them as advice for impulsivity is ours.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad points to understanding, to holding the reins, and to holding back the senses.

  1. Drive with understanding. Katha 1.3.6: "But he who has understanding and whose mind is always firmly held, his senses are under control, like good horses of a charioteer."
  2. Hold the senses back. Katha 2.3.10-11: "When the five instruments of knowledge stand still together with the mind, and when the intellect does not move, that is called the highest state. This, the firm holding back of the senses, is what is called Yoga."
  3. Stay awake to it. The same verse adds: "He must be free from thoughtlessness then, for Yoga comes and goes."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses are about yoga and self-mastery, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for impulsive behaviour that is causing harm. Reading them as advice for impulsivity is ours.

Christianity

The Bible's counsel is to slow down, to train, and to receive self-control as a gift.

  1. Slow down. James 1:19: "let everyone be quick to listen, slow to speak, slow to anger".
  2. Seek understanding. Proverbs 14:29: "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding".
  3. Train. 1 Corinthians 9:25: "Athletes exercise self-control in all things; they do it to receive a perishable wreath, but we an imperishable one."
  4. Let it grow. Galatians 5:22-23: "the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. These verses are about patience and self-control in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for impulsive behaviour that is causing harm.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts teach staying in your own ground, taming the mind, and conquering yourself.

  1. Stay in your own territory. SN 47.6: "You should roam inside your own territory, the domain of your fathers. If you roam inside your own territory, the domain of your fathers, Māra won't find a vulnerability or get an opportunity." The sutta says that territory is "the four kinds of mindfulness meditation".
  2. Tame the mind. Dhp 35: "it's good to tame the mind; a tamed mind leads to bliss."
  3. Be your own conqueror. Dhp 103: "The supreme conqueror is not he who conquers a million men in battle, but he who conquers a single man: himself."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The texts are about mindfulness and self-mastery in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for impulsive behaviour that is causing harm. Reading "roam inside your own territory" as a picture of changing your situation is ours.

Philosophical

The philosophers' advice is to wait, to look at both moments, and to think before the feeling arrives.

  1. Delay. Epictetus, Enchiridion 34: "If you are struck by the appearance of any promised pleasure, guard yourself against being hurried away by it; but let the affair wait your leisure, and procure yourself some delay."
  2. Look at both times. The same passage: "bring to your mind both points of time: that in which you will enjoy the pleasure, and that in which you will repent and reproach yourself after you have enjoyed it; and set before you, in opposition to these, how you will be glad and applaud yourself if you abstain."
  3. Think first. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.7: some people "if they have first perceived and seen what is coming and have first roused themselves and their calculative faculty, are not defeated by their emotion, whether it be pleasant or painful."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. These are practices for pleasure and emotion in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for impulsive behaviour that is causing harm.
