Impulsivity

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Impulsivity is acting on the urge of the moment without weighing what it will cost later. Delayed gratification is the other side of it: waiting, or passing something up now, for a bigger or better thing later. The conflict is between the smaller, sooner reward and the larger, later one.

Urges themselves are normal. Hofmann and colleagues had 205 adults wear beepers for a week and collect 7,827 reports of desire episodes. They found that "desires are frequent, variable in intensity, and largely unproblematic", and that "those urges that do conflict with other goals tend to elicit resistance, with uneven success".

The famous "marshmallow" tests study this in children. Kidd and colleagues open their paper by noting that "Children are notoriously bad at delaying gratification to achieve later, greater rewards". Mischel and colleagues' 1972 paper, "Cognitive and attentional mechanisms in delay of gratification", is an early study of what helps people wait.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: buying on a whim, sending the message before thinking, eating or scrolling past the point you meant to stop, and saying yes before checking the diary. It overlaps with procrastination, where the present mood also wins over the later self.

Impulsivity is not a diagnosis. If it is costing you money, safety or relationships, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Hofmann et al. (2012), "Everyday temptations: an experience sampling study of desire, conflict, and self-control," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 102(6), 1318-1335. doi:10.1037/a0026545 (external link)
  • Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin (2013), "Rational snacking: young children's decision-making on the marshmallow task is moderated by beliefs about environmental reliability," Cognition 126(1), 109-114. doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2012.08.004 (external link)
  • Mischel, Ebbesen and Zeiss (1972), "Cognitive and attentional mechanisms in delay of gratification," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 21(2), 204-218. doi:10.1037/h0032198 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for impulsivity, but the Gita describes senses that rush ahead of the will. Gita 2.60: "The senses are so strong and turbulent, O son of Kunti, that they can forcibly carry away the mind even of a person endowed with discrimination who practices self-control."

And 2.61: "They are established in perfect knowledge, who subdue their senses and keep their minds ever absorbed in Me."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The Gita is describing the pull of the senses and the discipline needed to steady them, and does not describe acting on urges without thought as a pattern of behaviour. Reading these verses as speaking to impulsivity is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name impulsivity, but the Katha Upanishad sets the pleasant against the good, which is the choice at its centre. Death says to Nachiketas (1.2.1): "The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man. It is well with him who clings to the good; he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."

And (1.2.2): "The good and the pleasant approach man: the wise goes round about them and distinguishes them. Yea, the wise prefers the good to the pleasant, but the fool chooses the pleasant through greed and avarice."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The passage is about choosing between what is good and what is pleasant, and does not describe acting without thought. Reading it as speaking to impulsivity is ours.

Christianity

The Bible speaks of self-control and its lack in short, blunt sayings. Proverbs 25:28: "Like a city breached, without walls, is one who lacks self-control."

Proverbs 14:29: "Whoever is slow to anger has great understanding, but one who has a hasty temper exalts folly." And Proverbs 29:20: "Do you see someone who is hasty in speech? There is more hope for a fool than for anyone like that."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The proverbs speak of haste in temper and speech, and of lacking self-control, and do not describe impulsivity as a pattern. Reading them as speaking to it is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts speak of the mind that will not stay put. Dhp 33: "The mind quivers and quakes, hard to guard, hard to curb. The discerning straighten it out, like a fletcher an arrow."

And Dhp 35: "Hard to hold back, flighty, alighting where it will; it's good to tame the mind; a tamed mind leads to bliss."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses are about the restless mind and its training, and do not describe acting on urges without thought as a pattern. Reading them as speaking to impulsivity is ours.

Philosophical

Aristotle separates two kinds of acting against your own judgement, and one is the impulsive kind. Nicomachean Ethics 7.7: "Of incontinence one kind is impetuosity, another weakness. For some men after deliberating fail, owing to their emotion, to stand by the conclusions of their deliberation, others because they have not deliberated are led by their emotion".

This fits the philosophical lens closely, since Aristotle's impetuous person acts on emotion before deliberating. He is describing a failure of self-control as a fault of character and reasoning, and not a medical condition.
