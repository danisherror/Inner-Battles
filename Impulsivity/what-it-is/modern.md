Impulsivity is acting on the urge of the moment without weighing what it will cost later. Delayed gratification is the other side of it: waiting, or passing something up now, for a bigger or better thing later. The conflict is between the smaller, sooner reward and the larger, later one.

Urges themselves are normal. Hofmann and colleagues had 205 adults wear beepers for a week and collect 7,827 reports of desire episodes. They found that "desires are frequent, variable in intensity, and largely unproblematic", and that "those urges that do conflict with other goals tend to elicit resistance, with uneven success".

The famous "marshmallow" tests study this in children. Kidd and colleagues open their paper by noting that "Children are notoriously bad at delaying gratification to achieve later, greater rewards". Mischel and colleagues' 1972 paper, "Cognitive and attentional mechanisms in delay of gratification", is an early study of what helps people wait.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: buying on a whim, sending the message before thinking, eating or scrolling past the point you meant to stop, and saying yes before checking the diary. It overlaps with [procrastination](../../Procrastination/what-it-is/modern.html), where the present mood also wins over the later self.

Impulsivity is not a diagnosis. If it is costing you money, safety or relationships, a doctor or therapist can help.
