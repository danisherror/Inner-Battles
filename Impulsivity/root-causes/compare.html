Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here points to three things, all with limits.

  • Beliefs about whether waiting pays off. Kidd and colleagues tested 28 children on the marshmallow task, in a setting made either reliable or unreliable. "Children in the reliable condition waited significantly longer than those in the unreliable condition", "suggesting that children's wait-times reflected reasoned beliefs about whether waiting would ultimately pay off". The authors conclude that wait-times "may not only reflect differences in self-control abilities, but also beliefs about the stability of the world". This is a small study.
  • Personality early, situation late. In Hofmann and colleagues' week-long study, "personality generally had a stronger impact on the dimensions of desire that emerged early in its course (desire strength and conflict)", while "situational factors showed relatively more influence on components later in the process (resistance and behavior enactment)". The situations included "alcohol consumption, the mere presence of others, and the presence of others who already had enacted the desire in question".
  • What it does not show. These are a small experiment and a self-report study. Neither shows what makes a particular person impulsive.

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: the reward in front of you feels vivid and the later one feels abstract, and tiredness and stress make the vivid one harder to refuse.

Sources

  • Kidd, Palmeri and Aslin (2013), Cognition 126(1), 109-114. doi:10.1016/j.cognition.2012.08.004 (external link)
  • Hofmann et al. (2012), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 102(6), 1318-1335. doi:10.1037/a0026545 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita places the root in how the senses meet their objects, and in the sweetness of the first taste.

Gita 3.34: "The senses naturally experience attachment and aversion to the sense objects, but do not be controlled by them, for they are way-layers and foes."

Gita 18.38 describes the pleasure: "Happiness is said to be in the mode of passion when it is derived from the contact of the senses with their objects. Such happiness is like nectar at first but poison at the end."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses are about attachment and the pleasure of the senses in general. Reading the sweetness of the first moment as what drives acting on impulse is ours.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 3.34 (external link), 18.38 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. "The mode of passion" is the translation's rendering of the term used in the verse, and its meaning was not examined.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad pictures the senses as horses and the mind as the reins. Katha 1.3.4: "The senses they call the horses, the objects of the senses their roads."

And 1.3.5: "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses are about the discipline of the mind in general. Reading unheld reins as the root of acting on impulse is ours.

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.3.4-5 (Max Müller's translation)

Christianity

The Bible does not give a theory of impulse, but Paul describes the split. Romans 7:15: "I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate."

Esau's reply in Genesis 25 shows the pull of the present need: "I am about to die; of what use is a birthright to me?" (25:32).

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. Romans 7 is about sin and the divided will, and Genesis 25 is about hunger. Reading them as roots of acting on impulse is ours.

Sources

  • Romans 7:15 (external link), Genesis 25:29-34 (external link)

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts trace desire back to pleasure and pain. In the Sutta Nipata (4.11) the question is: "So where do things held dear in the world spring from?" The answer: "What we hold dear in the world spring from desire".

Asked where desire comes from, it says: "What they call pleasure and pain in the world—based on that, desire comes about."

SN 47.6 names the pull: "the five kinds of sensual stimulation", "Sights known by the eye, which are likable, desirable, agreeable, pleasant, sensual, and arousing", and the same for sounds, smells, tastes and touches.

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The suttas speak of desire and sense pleasure in general, and not of acting on impulse. Reading them as roots of it is ours.

Sources

  • Snp 4.11 (external link)
  • SN 47.6 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-14. In Snp 4.11 the translation reads "what we hold dear in the world spring from desire", with the verb as printed. The SN 47.6 quotation is the sutta's list for sights, and the lists for the other senses are abbreviated in the sutta itself.

Philosophical

Aristotle says who is most prone to the impetuous kind of failure: "It is keen and excitable people that suffer especially from the impetuous form of incontinence; for the former by reason of their quickness and the latter by reason of the violence of their passions do not await the argument, because they are apt to follow their imagination."

Plato gives a picture of the divided soul in the Phaedrus: "the human charioteer drives his in a pair; and one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed; and the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. Aristotle is describing temperament and Plato a divided soul, and neither is a modern account of impulse. Reading them together as roots is ours.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.7 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
  • Plato, Phaedrus (external link) (Benjamin Jowett's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The translators named are those credited on the pages, and the Plato quotation is from the chariot image in the Phaedrus.
