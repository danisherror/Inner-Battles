Effects on life

The best-known claim is that children who waited longer in the marshmallow test did better later. A replication read here found a weaker link than the original.

Watts, Duncan and Quan studied children whose mothers had not completed college. They found that "an additional minute waited at age 4 predicted a gain of approximately one tenth of a standard deviation in achievement at age 15". But "this bivariate correlation was only half the size of those reported in the original studies and was reduced by two thirds in the presence of controls for family background, early cognitive ability, and the home environment". And "Associations between delay time and measures of behavioral outcomes at age 15 were much smaller and rarely statistically significant."

So the abstract does not support a claim that waiting as a four-year-old decides how life turns out. The replication has itself been discussed in print (Falk, Kosse and Pinger's 2020 paper compares the two studies), and that paper was not opened here.

Commonly described effects of impulsive choices in adults, not findings from these papers: debt, regret, broken plans and damaged trust. If they are piling up, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Watts, Duncan and Quan (2018), "Revisiting the marshmallow test: a conceptual replication investigating links between early delay of gratification and later outcomes," Psychological Science 29(7), 1159-1177. doi:10.1177/0956797618761661 (external link)
  • Falk, Kosse and Pinger (2020), "Re-revisiting the marshmallow test: a direct comparison of studies by Shoda, Mischel, and Peake (1990) and Watts, Duncan, and Quan (2018)," Psychological Science 31(1), 100-104. doi:10.1177/0956797619861720 (external link)
