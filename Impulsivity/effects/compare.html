Lenses to show

Modern

The best-known claim is that children who waited longer in the marshmallow test did better later. A replication read here found a weaker link than the original.

Watts, Duncan and Quan studied children whose mothers had not completed college. They found that "an additional minute waited at age 4 predicted a gain of approximately one tenth of a standard deviation in achievement at age 15". But "this bivariate correlation was only half the size of those reported in the original studies and was reduced by two thirds in the presence of controls for family background, early cognitive ability, and the home environment". And "Associations between delay time and measures of behavioral outcomes at age 15 were much smaller and rarely statistically significant."

So the abstract does not support a claim that waiting as a four-year-old decides how life turns out. The replication has itself been discussed in print (Falk, Kosse and Pinger's 2020 paper compares the two studies), and that paper was not opened here.

Commonly described effects of impulsive choices in adults, not findings from these papers: debt, regret, broken plans and damaged trust. If they are piling up, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Watts, Duncan and Quan (2018), "Revisiting the marshmallow test: a conceptual replication investigating links between early delay of gratification and later outcomes," Psychological Science 29(7), 1159-1177. doi:10.1177/0956797618761661 (external link)
  • Falk, Kosse and Pinger (2020), "Re-revisiting the marshmallow test: a direct comparison of studies by Shoda, Mischel, and Peake (1990) and Watts, Duncan, and Quan (2018)," Psychological Science 31(1), 100-104. doi:10.1177/0956797619861720 (external link)

Watts et al. was read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), and its details were checked in Crossref. Falk et al. was checked for title and publication details only and not opened, so the article says nothing about what it found. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita traces where a mind led by its senses ends. Gita 2.62: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger." And 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

On Gita 18.38 ("nectar at first but poison at the end") the translator's commentary says the joy "is as short-lived as the contact itself, and leaves in its wake greed, anxiety, guilt, and a thickening of the material illusion".

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses describe attachment and its results in general. Reading them as the toll of acting on impulse is ours.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.62 (external link), 2.63 (external link), 18.38 (external link)

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad names what comes of an unheld mind. Katha 1.3.7: "He who has no understanding, who is unmindful and always impure, never reaches that place, but enters into the round of births."

And 1.2.1: "he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The verses are about the end of human life, and are not about everyday consequences. Reading them as the effect of acting on impulse is ours.

Christianity

The Bible shows what haste costs.

  • Want. Proverbs 21:5: "The plans of the diligent lead surely to abundance, but everyone who is hasty comes only to want."
  • Hopelessness of the rash speaker. Proverbs 29:20: "Do you see someone who is hasty in speech? There is more hope for a fool than for anyone like that."
  • A birthright lost. Hebrews 12:16 says of Esau: "who sold his birthright for a single meal".

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The proverbs set haste against diligence, and Hebrews 12:16 uses Esau as a warning. Reading them as the toll of acting on impulse is ours, and these verses should not be used to shame anyone.

Buddhism

SN 47.6 tells what befalls the one who strays. A hawk seizes a quail, and the quail wails: "I'm so unlucky, of so little merit, to have roamed out of my territory into the domain of others."

The Buddha applies it: "If you roam out of your own territory into the domain of others, Māra will find a vulnerability and get an opportunity."

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. The sutta is about straying from mindfulness into sense pleasures, and the hawk and quail are a parable. Reading it as the effect of acting on impulse is ours.

Philosophical

The philosophers name regret as the usual aftermath. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.8: "incontinent man is likely to repent."

Epictetus, Enchiridion 34, tells the reader to "bring to your mind both points of time: that in which you will enjoy the pleasure, and that in which you will repent and reproach yourself after you have enjoyed it".

This is related to impulsivity and not identical to it. Aristotle is speaking of the incontinent person in general, and Epictetus of a promised pleasure. Neither is a study of what acting on impulse costs, and the regret they describe is not the only effect.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.8 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 34 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. In the Aristotle page the sentence reads "But incontinent man is likely to repent." The Epictetus page names Elizabeth Carter, but the exact edition was not established.
