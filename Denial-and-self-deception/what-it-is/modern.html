Denial and self-deception

Not letting yourself see the truth, and explaining away the discomfort that follows.

What it is

Denial is refusing to accept a fact that is plain to others, and self-deception is believing what you would prefer to be true. Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort of holding two things that do not fit, such as "I am an honest person" and "I just did something dishonest", and the urge to explain it away. These are standard definitions and not taken from the papers below.

Researchers have studied the last step. Egan, Santos and Bloom describe "decision rationalization": after a choice between two equally liked options, children and monkeys later treated the option they had not chosen as less valuable. Chance and colleagues ran four experiments on people who "go beyond merely explaining away their misconduct to actively deceiving themselves", and found that people who exploited chances to cheat on tests inferred "that their elevated performance is a sign of intelligence."

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: "it's not that bad", changing the subject when a worry comes up, finding a reason after the fact for something you already did, and being sure of a version of events that others do not recognise. It overlaps with overthinking, where a mind can rehearse reasons for hours.

Denial is not a diagnosis, and it is not always a fault: people sometimes need time before they can face bad news. If it keeps you from facing a health problem, a harmful habit, or a danger to you or someone else, a doctor or therapist can help.
