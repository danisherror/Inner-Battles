Inner battle

Denial and self-deception

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Denial is refusing to accept a fact that is plain to others, and self-deception is believing what you would prefer to be true. Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort of holding two things that do not fit, such as "I am an honest person" and "I just did something dishonest", and the urge to explain it away. These are standard definitions and not taken from the papers below.

Researchers have studied the last step. Egan, Santos and Bloom describe "decision rationalization": after a choice between two equally liked options, children and monkeys later treated the option they had not chosen as less valuable. Chance and colleagues ran four experiments on people who "go beyond merely explaining away their misconduct to actively deceiving themselves", and found that people who exploited chances to cheat on tests inferred "that their elevated performance is a sign of intelligence."

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: "it's not that bad", changing the subject when a worry comes up, finding a reason after the fact for something you already did, and being sure of a version of events that others do not recognise. It overlaps with overthinking, where a mind can rehearse reasons for hours.

Denial is not a diagnosis, and it is not always a fault: people sometimes need time before they can face bad news. If it keeps you from facing a health problem, a harmful habit, or a danger to you or someone else, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Egan, Santos and Bloom (2007), "The origins of cognitive dissonance: evidence from children and monkeys," Psychological Science 18(11), 978-983. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.02012.x (external link)
  • Chance, Norton, Gino and Ariely (2011), "Temporal view of the costs and benefits of self-deception," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 108 (Supplement 3), 15655-15659. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The definitions, the list of everyday signs and the remark that denial is sometimes a way of gaining time are a standard summary and not drawn from a source.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for denial, but it describes the self-deceiver. Gita 3.6: "Those who restrain the external organs of action, while continuing to dwell on sense objects in the mind, certainly delude themselves and are to be called hypocrites."

And 18.32 describes an intellect "shrouded in darkness, imagining irreligion to be religion, and perceiving untruth to be the truth".

This is related to denial and not identical to it. 3.6 is about restraint of the senses and 18.32 about a darkened intellect, and neither speaks of a refusal to face facts. Reading them as portraits of self-deception is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe the one who does not look past the visible. Katha 1.2.6: "The Hereafter never rises before the eyes of the careless child, deluded by the delusion of wealth. 'This is the world,' he thinks, 'there is no other;' — thus he falls again and again under my sway."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. The verse is about the Hereafter and a child deluded by wealth, and does not describe the refusal of an uncomfortable fact. Reading it as a picture of closing the eyes is ours.

Christianity

The Bible says plainly that we can deceive ourselves. 1 John 1:8: "If we say that we have no sin, we deceive ourselves, and the truth is not in us."

Jeremiah 17:9: "The heart is devious above all else; it is perverse— who can understand it?"

This is related to denial and not identical to it. 1 John 1:8 is about sin and Jeremiah about the heart, and neither describes a refusal to face every kind of fact. The wording of Jeremiah 17:9 differs in other translations, and the NRSVUE page was the only one read.

Buddhism

In Buddhist texts the closest idea is delusion and the perversions of view. Dhp 63: "The fool who thinks they're a fool is wise at least to that extent. But the true fool is said to be one who imagines that they are wise."

AN 4.49 names four perversions of perception, thought and view: "Taking impermanence as permanence. Taking suffering as happiness. Taking not-self as self. Taking ugliness as beauty."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. Dhp 63 is about thinking oneself wise, and AN 4.49 is about four mistaken views. Reading both as self-deception is ours.

Philosophical

Socrates describes meeting someone who is wrong about himself. Plato's Apology: "he was not really wise, although he was thought wise by many, and wiser still by himself". And: "for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. Socrates is speaking of false wisdom, and not of refusing to face facts. Reading it as a picture of self-deception is ours.
