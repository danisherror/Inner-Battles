Effects on life

The research read here suggests that self-deception can feel good now and cost later, and its limits matter.

  • A cost over time. Chance and colleagues found that people who cheated on tests inferred "that their elevated performance is a sign of intelligence". When predicting future performance, "participants expect to perform equally well-a lack of awareness that persists even when these inflated expectations prove costly."
  • Not foreseen. The authors write that "although people expect to cheat, they do not foresee self-deception".
  • What this does not show. These were laboratory experiments on cheating at tests. They do not show what denial does about illness, grief or a relationship.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: problems that grow because they are left alone, repeated mistakes, strained trust with people who see what you do not, and a nagging unease under the calm. If denial is keeping you from facing a health problem, a harmful habit or a danger, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Chance, Norton, Gino and Ariely (2011), "Temporal view of the costs and benefits of self-deception," PNAS 108 (Supplement 3), 15655-15659. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (external link)

Read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and the details were checked in Crossref. The "-" in "equally well-a lack" is as the abstract prints it. The last paragraph cites no study.
