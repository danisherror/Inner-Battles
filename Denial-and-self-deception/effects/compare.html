Inner battle

Denial and self-deception

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here suggests that self-deception can feel good now and cost later, and its limits matter.

  • A cost over time. Chance and colleagues found that people who cheated on tests inferred "that their elevated performance is a sign of intelligence". When predicting future performance, "participants expect to perform equally well-a lack of awareness that persists even when these inflated expectations prove costly."
  • Not foreseen. The authors write that "although people expect to cheat, they do not foresee self-deception".
  • What this does not show. These were laboratory experiments on cheating at tests. They do not show what denial does about illness, grief or a relationship.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: problems that grow because they are left alone, repeated mistakes, strained trust with people who see what you do not, and a nagging unease under the calm. If denial is keeping you from facing a health problem, a harmful habit or a danger, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Chance, Norton, Gino and Ariely (2011), "Temporal view of the costs and benefits of self-deception," PNAS 108 (Supplement 3), 15655-15659. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (external link)

Read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and the details were checked in Crossref. The "-" in "equally well-a lack" is as the abstract prints it. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita says what a clouded mind leads to. Gita 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

And 18.32 describes the intellect that perceives "untruth to be the truth" as being "of the nature of ignorance".

This is related to denial and not identical to it. 2.63 is about the chain that starts with anger, and 18.32 about a darkened intellect. Reading them as the cost of self-deception is ours. The same verse is used in Resentment.

Upanishads

The Upanishads say what the deluded face. Katha 1.2.6: "'This is the world,' he thinks, 'there is no other;' — thus he falls again and again under my sway."

Mundaka 1.2.8 describes the going round: "go round and round staggering to and fro, like blind men led by the blind."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. The verses are about the Hereafter and about conceit, and do not mention a refusal to face a fact. Reading them as the cost of self-deception is ours.

Christianity

The Bible says what self-deception costs. Proverbs 14:12: "There is a way that seems right to a person, but its end is the way to death."

Proverbs 28:13: "No one who conceals transgressions will prosper, but one who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy."

James 1:23-24 gives a picture: hearers who do not act "are like those who look at themselves in a mirror; for they look at themselves and, on going away, immediately forget what they were like."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These verses are about right and wrong ways, hidden wrongdoing and hearing without doing. Reading them as the cost of self-deception is ours, and "death" in Proverbs 14:12 is not explained in the page read.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts say what looking only at others' faults does. Dhp 253: "When you look for the flaws of others, always finding fault, your defilements only grow, you're far from ending defilements."

MN 61 says what happens once lying is easy: "when someone is not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie, there is no bad deed they would not do, I say."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. Dhp 253 is about fault-finding, and MN 61 is about lying to others. Reading them as the cost of self-deception is ours.

Philosophical

Seneca says what comes of not knowing one's fault. Letters 28: "he who does not know that he has sinned does not desire correction; you must discover yourself in the wrong before you can reform yourself."

Socrates names the stakes. Plato, Apology: "the life which is unexamined is not worth living".

This is related to denial and not identical to it. Seneca is writing about knowing one's faults and Socrates about examining one's life. Reading them as the cost of self-deception is ours.
