Denial and self-deception

Not letting yourself see the truth, and explaining away the discomfort that follows.

Modern lens

Root causes

The sources read here offer accounts of why we do it, and they are still debated.

  • Reducing discomfort. Egan and colleagues found "decision rationalization" in preschoolers and capuchin monkeys, and suggest that "the mechanisms underlying cognitive-dissonance reduction in human adults may have originated both developmentally and evolutionarily earlier than previously thought." The task was a choice between two stickers or two sweets.
  • An evolutionary account. von Hippel and Trivers "argue that self-deception evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception by allowing people to avoid the cues to conscious deception that might reveal deceptive intent." They add that it "eliminates the costly cognitive load that is typically associated with deceiving". This is an argument, and the abstract is of a target article followed by commentary, so it does not show the account is settled.
  • A benefit in the moment. Chance and colleagues found that "this short-term psychological benefit of self-deception" can come with longer-term costs.

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: protecting a picture of oneself as good, avoiding a painful truth, fear of the consequences of admitting it, and the pull of what we hope is true.

Sources

  • Egan, Santos and Bloom (2007), Psychological Science 18(11), 978-983. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.02012.x (external link)
  • von Hippel and Trivers (2011), "The evolution and psychology of self-deception," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 34(1), 1-16. doi:10.1017/s0140525x10001354 (external link)
  • Chance, Norton, Gino and Ariely (2011), PNAS 108 (Supplement 3), 15655-15659. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The last paragraph cites no study.
