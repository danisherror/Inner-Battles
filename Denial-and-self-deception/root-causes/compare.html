Denial and self-deception

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here offer accounts of why we do it, and they are still debated.

  • Reducing discomfort. Egan and colleagues found "decision rationalization" in preschoolers and capuchin monkeys, and suggest that "the mechanisms underlying cognitive-dissonance reduction in human adults may have originated both developmentally and evolutionarily earlier than previously thought." The task was a choice between two stickers or two sweets.
  • An evolutionary account. von Hippel and Trivers "argue that self-deception evolved to facilitate interpersonal deception by allowing people to avoid the cues to conscious deception that might reveal deceptive intent." They add that it "eliminates the costly cognitive load that is typically associated with deceiving". This is an argument, and the abstract is of a target article followed by commentary, so it does not show the account is settled.
  • A benefit in the moment. Chance and colleagues found that "this short-term psychological benefit of self-deception" can come with longer-term costs.

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: protecting a picture of oneself as good, avoiding a painful truth, fear of the consequences of admitting it, and the pull of what we hope is true.

Sources

  • Egan, Santos and Bloom (2007), Psychological Science 18(11), 978-983. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.02012.x (external link)
  • von Hippel and Trivers (2011), "The evolution and psychology of self-deception," Behavioral and Brain Sciences 34(1), 1-16. doi:10.1017/s0140525x10001354 (external link)
  • Chance, Norton, Gino and Ariely (2011), PNAS 108 (Supplement 3), 15655-15659. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita's root is ignorance that covers knowledge. Gita 5.15: "The living entities are deluded because their inner knowledge is covered by ignorance."

And 7.13: "Deluded by the three modes of Maya, people in this world are unable to know Me, the imperishable and eternal."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These verses are about ignorance and illusion in general, and do not mention looking away from a fact. Reading them as roots of self-deception is ours.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 5.15 (external link), 7.13 (external link)

Upanishads

The Upanishads name conceit and the lure of wealth. Mundaka 1.2.8: "Fools dwelling in darkness, wise in their own conceit, and puffed up with vain knowledge, go round and round staggering to and fro, like blind men led by the blind."

Katha 1.2.6 gives the cause of the child's blindness: "deluded by the delusion of wealth."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. The verses are about conceit and wealth, and do not speak of avoiding an uncomfortable fact. Taking them as roots of self-deception is ours. Mundaka 1.2.8 repeats the words of Katha 1.2.5, which are used in Ego and validation.

Christianity

The Bible's root is that we judge ourselves by our own eyes. Proverbs 16:2: "All one's ways may be pure in one's own eyes, but the Lord weighs the spirit."

Galatians 6:3: "For if those who are nothing think they are something, they deceive themselves."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These verses are about self-assessment and about thinking too well of oneself, and do not speak of refusing to face a fact. Taking them as roots of self-deception is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist root is that we see others' faults more easily than our own. Dhp 252: "It's easy to see the faults of others, hard to see one's own. For the faults of others are tossed high like hay, while one's own are hidden, as a cheat hides a bad hand."

AN 4.49 puts the root in a deeper error: "Yoked by Māra's yoke, these people find no sanctuary from the yoke."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. Dhp 252 is about noticing faults in oneself and others, and AN 4.49 is about wrong view. Reading them as roots of self-deception is ours.

Sources

  • Dhp 235-255 (external link), AN 4.49 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-15. The quotations join lines. The Pali words and the meaning of "Māra" in this verse were not examined.

Philosophical

Seneca says that some people turn their faults into virtues. Letters 28: "Some boast of their faults. Do you think that the man has any thought of mending his ways who counts over his vices as if they were virtues?"

Socrates describes how the politician was "wiser still by himself" than others thought him (Plato, Apology).

This is related to denial and not identical to it. Seneca is writing about boasting of faults and Plato about false wisdom. Reading them as roots of self-deception is ours.

  • Seneca, Moral letters to Lucilius, Letter 28 (external link) (Richard Mott Gummere's translation)
  • Plato, Apology (external link) (Benjamin Jowett's translation)
