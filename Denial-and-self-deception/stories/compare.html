Denial and self-deception

A story, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how denial works and how people loosen its grip.

Anil's story

Anil opened a small shop with his savings. For two years it lost money. Each month he said it was a slow season, then that the street was being dug up, then that the next festival would fix it. When his sister asked how it was going he said "fine" and changed the subject.

He knew, when he looked at the accounts, and he often did not look. What helped:

  1. He noticed the discomfort. The tightness in his chest when his sister asked was the sign that something was being pushed away.
  2. He wrote down the facts. Rent, sales and what he had put in, for each month, with no commentary.
  3. He asked someone he trusted. His sister said what she had seen, without blame, and he listened before answering.
  4. He looked ahead. He asked what the story would sound like in a year if nothing changed.
  5. He went at his own pace. He took a month to decide, and spoke to an adviser about the debts.
  6. He kept his dignity. He closed the shop and kept the lessons, and told people honestly why.

The disappointment was real. The two years of "next month" had cost more than the truth did.

What it shows: Anil did not have to be harsh with himself. Looking at the accounts, and letting someone else look with him, did what a good story had not.

Hinduism

Arjuna admits it

The Gita opens with a man who says plainly what is wrong with him. In 2.7 Arjuna tells Krishna: "I am confused about my duty, and am besieged with anxiety and faintheartedness. I am Your disciple, and am surrendered to You. Please instruct me for certain what is best for me."

In 2.8 he adds: "I can find no means of driving away this anguish that is drying up my senses. Even if I win a prosperous and unrivalled kingdom on the earth, or gain sovereignty like the celestial gods, I will be unable to dispel this grief."

What it shows: The teaching begins when Arjuna stops pretending he is fine and says that he is confused and grieving. The Gita has no tale of denial, so this is a moment in the dialogue and not a story of self-deception; the link is ours, and Arjuna's words are about his duty in the war.

Upanishads

The careless child and the call to wake

The Upanishads have no tale of self-deception, so this is a picture from the Katha. Death describes "the careless child, deluded by the delusion of wealth", who thinks "'This is the world... there is no other;'" and "thus he falls again and again under my sway" (1.2.6).

Later the text calls: "Rise, awake! having obtained your boons, understand them! The sharp edge of a razor is difficult to pass over; thus the wise say the path (to the Self) is hard" (1.3.14).

What it shows: The text sets the sleeper beside the call to wake, and says the path is hard. The passage is about the Self and the Hereafter and not about denial; the link is ours.

Christianity

Nathan and David

In 2 Samuel 12 the prophet Nathan comes to King David with a story. A rich man with "very many flocks and herds" takes a poor man's one little ewe lamb to feed a guest. "David's anger was greatly kindled against the man. He said to Nathan, 'As the Lord lives, the man who has done this deserves to die; he shall restore the lamb fourfold because he did this thing and because he had no pity.'"

Nathan says: "You are the man!" He names what David has done: "You have struck down Uriah the Hittite with the sword and have taken his wife to be your wife and have killed him with the sword of the Ammonites" (12:7-9). David answers: "I have sinned against the Lord" (12:13).

What it shows: David can see the wrong clearly in someone else's story, and not in his own. The account of what David did to Uriah is in the chapters before this one, and it involves adultery and a killing. The story is about justice and a king's wrongdoing and not about self-deception as such; the link is ours.

Buddhism

Rāhula and the water pot

In MN 61 the Buddha visits his son Rāhula, who sets out water for his feet. The Buddha leaves "a little water in the pot" and asks: "Rāhula, do you see this little bit of water left in the pot?" "Yes, sir." "That's how little of the ascetic's nature is left in those who are not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie."

He throws the water away: "That's how the ascetic's nature is discarded in those who are not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie." He turns the pot upside down, then upright and empty: "That's how vacant and hollow the ascetic's nature is in those who are not ashamed to tell a deliberate lie."

Then he asks: "What is the purpose of a mirror?" "It's for checking your reflection, sir." "In the same way, deeds of body, speech, and mind should be done only after repeated checking."

What it shows: The Buddha moves from a pot to a mirror, from the cost of lying to the habit of looking at one's own acts. The sutta is about lying and the checking of deeds and not about self-deception as such; the link is ours.

Philosophical

Socrates and the man who thought himself wise

In Plato's Apology Socrates tells the jury why he has enemies. An oracle called him the wisest of men, and he went to test it. He went "to one who had the reputation of wisdom", a politician: "I could not help thinking that he was not really wise, although he was thought wise by many, and wiser still by himself; and I went and tried to explain to him that he thought himself wise, but was not really wise; and the consequence was that he hated me".

He left reflecting: "I am better off than he is - for he knows nothing, and thinks that he knows. I neither know nor think that I know."

What it shows: The man was not told what he did not want to hear, and he took it badly. The passage is about wisdom and the examination of others and not about denial as such; the link is ours, and it is Socrates' own account of events, told at his trial.
