Denial and self-deception

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story
Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes self-deception and its costs and tests no remedy. The rest is common advice.

  1. Notice the discomfort. A sudden urge to explain, change the subject or defend is a common sign that something is being pushed away. That is common advice.
  2. Write down the facts. Put on paper what happened, what you did and what the evidence is, apart from how you feel about it. That is common advice.
  3. Ask someone you trust. Choose a person who will tell you the truth kindly, and listen before you answer. That is common advice.
  4. Look ahead. Chance and colleagues found that people "expect to cheat" but "do not foresee self-deception", and that inflated expectations "prove costly". A fair question to put to yourself is how the story you are telling will look in a year. That use of the finding is ours, and the study did not test it.
  5. Go slowly if it hurts. People sometimes need time before they can face bad news, and a counsellor can go at your pace.
  6. Get help for what is at stake. If denial is keeping you from facing a health problem, a harmful habit or a danger to you or someone else, a doctor or therapist can help. That is common advice.

Sources

  • Chance, Norton, Gino and Ariely (2011), PNAS 108 (Supplement 3), 15655-15659. doi:10.1073/pnas.1010658108 (external link)

Read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. Steps 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are common advice, not tied to a study read here; step 4 uses a finding in a way the paper did not test.

Hinduism

The Gita's counsel is to seek knowledge, and to use the mind as a friend and not an enemy.

  1. Ask a teacher. Gita 4.34: "Learn the Truth by approaching a spiritual master. Inquire from him with reverence and render service unto him."
  2. Value knowledge. Gita 4.38: "In this world, there is nothing as purifying as divine knowledge."
  3. Make the mind a friend. Gita 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."
  4. Check the match between act and thought. Gita 3.6 warns of those who restrain action "while continuing to dwell on sense objects in the mind", and "certainly delude themselves".

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These verses are about knowledge, a teacher and the mind, and the Gita sets them within its own path. Reading them as a way through self-deception is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Upanishads

The Upanishads call for waking up and for asking.

  1. Wake. Katha 1.3.14: "Rise, awake! having obtained your boons, understand them! The sharp edge of a razor is difficult to pass over; thus the wise say the path (to the Self) is hard."
  2. Ask the question you have not asked. In Chandogya 6.1.3 a father asks his conceited son: "have you ever asked for that instruction by which we hear what cannot be heard, by which we perceive what cannot be perceived, by which we know what cannot be known?"
  3. Admit what you did not know. The son answers in 6.1.7: "Surely those venerable men (my teachers) did not know that. For if they had known it, why should they not have told it me?"

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These verses are about the Self and about a student's learning, and they do not describe facing an uncomfortable fact. Reading them as a way through self-deception is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Christianity

The Bible's counsel is to ask to be searched, to confess and to look at your own log first.

  1. Ask to be searched. Psalm 139:23-24: "Search me, O God, and know my heart; test me and know my thoughts. See if there is any wicked way in me, and lead me in the way everlasting."
  2. Confess. 1 John 1:9: "If we confess our sins, he who is faithful and just will forgive us our sins and cleanse us from all unrighteousness." And Proverbs 28:13: "one who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy."
  3. Look at your own first. Matthew 7:5: "You hypocrite, first take the log out of your own eye, and then you will see clearly to take the speck out of your neighbor's eye."
  4. Test your own work. Galatians 6:4: "All must test their own work".

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These verses are about sin, confession and judging others, and they sit within the Christian life. Reading them as a way through self-deception is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts counsel checking, as one checks a mirror, and telling a trusted friend.

  1. Use a mirror. In MN 61 the Buddha asks Rāhula: "What is the purpose of a mirror?" "It's for checking your reflection, sir." "In the same way, deeds of body, speech, and mind should be done only after repeated checking."
  2. Check before, during and after. He teaches Rāhula to ask: "Does this act with the body that I want to do lead to hurting myself, hurting others, or hurting both?"
  3. Confess to a friend. "Then, Rāhula, you should confess, reveal, and clarify such a deed to the Teacher or a sensible spiritual companion."
  4. Take the correction. AN 4.49: "When a wise person hears them, they get their mind back."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. MN 61 is advice to a young monastic on honesty and action, and AN 4.49 is about wrong view. Reading them as a way through self-deception is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.

Philosophical

The philosophers' counsel is to examine yourself, and to be your own accuser first.

  1. Examine your life. Plato's Apology: "the life which is unexamined is not worth living".
  2. Be your own accuser. Seneca, Letters 28: "prove yourself guilty, hunt up charges against yourself; play the part, first of accuser, then of judge, last of intercessor. At times be harsh with yourself."
  3. Start with what you do not know. Socrates: "I neither know nor think that I know."

This is related to denial and not identical to it. These are one school's disciplines, and Seneca's advice to be harsh with yourself should be read beside Self-criticism, which describes how a harsh inner voice can do harm. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.
