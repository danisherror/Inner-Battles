Deadly sin

Pride

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story
Lenses to show

Modern

Hubristic pride costs more than it seems to give.

  • Relationships. Arrogant people are liked less over time. They interrupt, dismiss and rarely apologise, so friends and partners stop being honest with them.
  • Work. If you can't admit errors or take feedback, you repeat them. Teams stop sharing bad news with a leader who punishes it.
  • Learning. Believing you already know blocks growth. Research on intellectual humility links it with better learning and more open-minded thinking.
  • Decisions. Overconfidence leads to risks taken without checking, as in the leaders described in "hubris syndrome."
  • Inner life. Pride feels strong but is brittle: criticism hurts more, and the need to keep up appearances is tiring.

Sources

  • Leary et al. (2017), "Cognitive and interpersonal features of intellectual humility," Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 43(6), 793-813. doi:10.1177/0146167217697695 (external link)
  • Owen & Davidson (2009), Brain 132(5), 1396-1406. doi:10.1093/brain/awp008 (external link)

Hinduism

The Gita describes where pride leads.

  • Rejecting good advice. Gita 18.58 says that if, out of ahamkara, you do not listen, you will perish. Pride closes the ear to the teacher.
  • Bondage to the doer idea. In 3.27 the person who thinks "I am the doer" stays tied to the results of action, so success inflates and failure crushes.
  • Descent. Gita 16.16-20 follows the arrogant and wealth-driven mind to anxiety, anger and, finally, rebirth in lower states. The chapter calls them "the lowest of men."
  • Loss of peace. Gita 2.71 says peace comes to one who moves free from longing, without the sense of "mine" (nirmama) and "I" (nirahankara). Pride is the reverse.

The epics tell the same story in action: Ravana and Duryodhana both lose everything after refusing the counsel of those who loved them.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe the cost of pride as staying lost in ignorance.

  • Going round in circles. Katha 1.2.5 and Mundaka 1.2.8 say those who think themselves wise while ignorant "wander around, deluded, like blind men led by the blind." Learning has become a trap.
  • Sorrow despite knowledge. Chandogya 7.1 has Narada admit that although he knows much, he is in sorrow because he does not know the Self. Pride in learning does not remove grief.
  • Separation. Isha 6-7 says the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, feels no hatred or delusion. By contrast, the proud person who insists on difference keeps both.
  • Missing the teaching. Kena 2.3 holds that the one who is sure he knows, does not know. Certainty ends the search before it has begun.

Christianity

Scripture and tradition describe pride's cost as a fall, and as broken relationships.

  • A fall. Proverbs 16:18 ("Pride goes before destruction") and Proverbs 11:2 ("When pride comes, then comes disgrace; but wisdom is with the humble") state the pattern simply.
  • Opposition. James 4:6 says God "opposes the proud." The proud person refuses grace because it feels like an admission of need.
  • Contempt for others. Luke 18:9 says Jesus told the parable of the Pharisee and tax collector "to some who trusted in themselves that they were righteous and regarded others with contempt."
  • Blindness to your own faults. Matthew 7:3-5 asks why you notice the speck in a neighbour's eye but not the log in your own.
  • Dividing the community. Philippians 2:3 urges "in humility regard others as better than yourselves," and Paul sets this against "selfish ambition or conceit."

Buddhism

Conceit keeps a person bound to suffering, and spoils how they treat others.

  • It blocks release. In the traditional list of ten fetters, conceit (mana) is one of the last to fall, so a practitioner can be far along and still held by it. SN 22.89 describes this.
  • Heedlessness. AN 3.39 says intoxication with youth, health and life leads people to wrong conduct and, afterwards, regret.
  • Unwelcome results. MN 135 says arrogance and disrespect toward the worthy lead to lower rebirth, a teaching about the results of action, not about any group of people.
  • Loneliness. The Dhammapada (verse 74) shows the person who wants every deed credited to them: the wish to be seen as the doer breeds craving and conceit.

Buddhism treats the effect as a loop: the more you defend the "I", the more it suffers when it is challenged.

Philosophical

Philosophers describe pride's costs as lost friendship, lost wisdom and lost freedom.

  • Ridicule and isolation. Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3) says vain people make themselves look foolish by dressing up and boasting, and so are laughed at.
  • Poor judgment. Socrates in the Apology argues that thinking you know what you don't is the worst ignorance because it prevents inquiry (29a-b).
  • Slavery to opinion. The Stoics held that someone who needs praise gives control of their peace to others. Epictetus (Enchiridion 13, 33) advises not telling everyone about your achievements and being content to be thought foolish.
  • Disrespect for persons. For Kant, arrogance treats others as of little worth, which violates the respect owed to them as ends in themselves.
