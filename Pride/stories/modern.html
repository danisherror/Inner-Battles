A story

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how pride works and how people step out of it.

Marcus's story

Marcus was 34, the best engineer on his team, and he knew it. He rewrote other people's code without asking, finished colleagues' sentences, and called a junior's suggestion "not worth discussing." When a release failed because of a design he had insisted on, he blamed the testers.

Quietly, people stopped telling him things. A teammate who saw the problem early said nothing, because speaking up never went well. Marcus read the silence as agreement.

The turning point came in a performance review. His manager listed specific examples, and one line stuck: "People are working around you, not with you." He was angry for a week. Then he asked two colleagues for honest feedback and got the same picture.

What helped:

  1. He asked and listened. He said "thank you" and wrote things down before replying.
  2. He admitted a mistake in public, in a team meeting, and nothing terrible happened.
  3. He shared credit by name in his updates.
  4. He asked the junior for a review of his own code.
  5. He kept a "what I got wrong" note each Friday.

It was uncomfortable and slow. But within months people were bringing him problems early, and the work got better.

What it shows: Pride isolates you from the information you need. Admitting a mistake felt like losing standing but gained trust.
