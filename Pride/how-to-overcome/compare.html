Deadly sin

Pride

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is not to think badly of yourself. It is to see yourself accurately and keep your worth from depending on being right or better.

  1. Separate worth from performance. You can value yourself and still be wrong. Say it out loud: "I was wrong about that."
  2. Ask for honest feedback, from someone who is not afraid of you, and respond with "thank you" before you defend.
  3. Practise intellectual humility. Add "I might be wrong" or "what would change my mind?" to your disagreements.
  4. Share credit, own blame. In a team, name who else contributed. After a failure, say what you would do differently.
  5. Prefer authentic pride. Take pride in effort and growth ("I improved") instead of rank ("I'm better than them").
  6. Be around people better than you at something, and learn from them.
  7. Practise gratitude. Listing what luck, others and timing gave you weakens the story that you did it all.

Sources

  • Leary et al. (2017), Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin 43(6). doi:10.1177/0146167217697695 (external link)
  • Tracy & Robins (2007), JPSP 92(3). doi:10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.506 (external link)

The steps are general advice informed by that research, not a tested programme. Citations were given from memory. If pride is tied up with a mental-health condition, talk to a professional.

Hinduism

Hindu practice answers pride with humility, service and surrender of the fruits of action.

  • Cultivate amanitvam. Gita 13.8 starts the list of knowledge with humility and absence of pretence, and goes on to respect for the teacher.
  • Act without claiming the result. Gita 2.47 says you have a right to action but not to its fruits. Gita 3.30 asks you to offer actions to the Divine, free from "mine" and from fever of ego.
  • Learn by serving the teacher. Gita 4.34 advises seeking knowledge by humble inquiry and service.
  • Bhakti. Bhakti traditions teach seeing yourself as a servant. In Gita 12.13-14, the devotee dear to Krishna is free of "mine" and "I", and is forgiving and content.
  • Practise seva and charity, which put others' needs first.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic path is to question the "I" and to learn from a teacher with an open mind.

  • Approach a teacher humbly. Mundaka 1.2.12 says to seek a guru who is learned in the scriptures and established in Brahman, coming "with fuel in hand", a sign of readiness to serve.
  • Drop the idea that you already know. Kena 2.3 gives the posture: knowing that you do not know it is the beginning of knowing it.
  • Ask "who am I?" The Self is not the body, mind or intellect. Katha 1.2.23 says it is gained by the one the Self chooses, which is why grasping does not work.
  • See the same Self in all. Isha 6 says the one who sees all beings in the Self does not shrink from anyone. Seeing equality weakens the habit of ranking.
  • Study, reflect, meditate. This is shravana, manana, nididhyasana, the sequence taught in later Vedanta, built on passages such as Brihadaranyaka 2.4.5.

Christianity

Christian teaching on humility is concrete.

  • Follow Christ's example. Philippians 2:5-8 describes him as emptying himself and taking the form of a servant. John 13:1-17 shows him washing his disciples' feet.
  • Pray honestly. The tax collector's "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" (Luke 18:13) is the model. Confession and repentance remove the pretence.
  • Count others higher. Philippians 2:3 and Romans 12:10 ask you to outdo one another in showing honour.
  • Receive correction. Proverbs 12:1 says whoever loves discipline loves knowledge.
  • Give thanks. 1 Corinthians 4:7 asks, "What do you have that you did not receive?"
  • Serve in hidden ways. Matthew 6:1-4 says to give without announcing it.

The tradition's name for the virtue is humilitas, from humus (earth), meaning truthful grounded-ness, not self-contempt.

Buddhism

Buddhism treats conceit as something to be seen through, not fought with force.

  • Contemplate not-self. Looking closely at body, feeling, perception, habits and awareness (SN 22.59) shows there is nothing fixed to be proud of or ashamed of.
  • Notice comparing. When you catch the mind ranking itself as better, equal or worse, label it "measuring" and let it go without acting on it.
  • Remember impermanence. AN 3.39 teaches reflecting that youth, health and life do not last, which takes the air out of intoxication with them.
  • Practise respect and gratitude. Showing reverence to teachers and elders, and to those who have helped you, is a standard practice. The Mangala Sutta (Sn 2.4) lists respect, contentment and gratitude among the highest blessings.
  • Share merit. Dedicating good deeds to others weakens the claim "I did this."
  • Cultivate metta (loving-kindness), which makes it harder to look down on anyone.

Philosophical

Philosophy offers training in accuracy about yourself.

  • Examine yourself. Socrates (Apology 38a) said the unexamined life is not worth living for a human being. Ask honestly what you know and what you only assume.
  • Aim for the mean. Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3) wants you to claim what you actually deserve, no more and no less, and builds that habit through practice.
  • Stoic exercises. Epictetus (Enchiridion 13) says to accept being thought foolish. Marcus Aurelius opens the Meditations (Book 1) by thanking each person he learned from, a daily practice of credit-sharing. Seneca recommends an evening review of the day's faults, a habit he credits to Sextius.
  • Look from above. Marcus (Meditations 9.30, 12.24) pictures the earth as a point and fame as short-lived, which shrinks the importance of rank.
  • Respect persons. Kant's duty of respect asks you to treat every person as having dignity equal to yours.
