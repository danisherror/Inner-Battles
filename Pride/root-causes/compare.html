Deadly sin

Pride

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Pride is rarely confidence gone too far. More often it is insecurity wearing armour. Common roots:

  • Fragile self-worth. If your value feels like it depends on being impressive, every mistake is a threat, so you deny it or blame someone else.
  • Self-serving bias. The mind credits you for wins and blames circumstances for losses, which quietly inflates your self-image.
  • Comparison. Status is relative, so feeling "above" others becomes a cheap way to feel fine.
  • Praise and power. Success, flattery and a lack of honest feedback make people more certain they are right. Researchers describe a "hubris syndrome" in some leaders after long spells in power.
  • Upbringing. Being praised only for results, or for being "better" than others, teaches that worth must be earned by winning.

Sources

  • Tracy & Robins (2007), JPSP 92(3), 506-525. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.92.3.506 (external link)
  • Owen & Davidson (2009), "Hubris syndrome," Brain 132(5), 1396-1406. doi:10.1093/brain/awp008 (external link). It proposes a pattern seen in some leaders, not an official diagnosis.

The upbringing and comparison points are common psychological summaries rather than one study. Citations were given from memory and not re-checked here.

Hinduism

Hindu teaching traces pride to ahamkara, the sense of "I" and "mine", and to the way it grows when the mind is ruled by rajas and tamas instead of sattva.

  • Misidentifying the doer. Gita 3.27 says the deluded person, with the mind confused by ego, thinks "I am the doer," when actions are carried out by the gunas.
  • Wealth, power and craving. Gita 16.13-16 shows how desire and the thought "I have gained this, I will gain that" lead to arrogance and to being "bewildered by many thoughts."
  • Inner enemies feed each other. Pride (mada) sits among the six shadripu beside desire, anger, greed and delusion. Success in one tends to feed the others.
  • Forgetting the source. Gita 10.8 and 18.61 describe the Lord as the source of everything and as dwelling in the heart, so claiming achievements as purely your own is a kind of forgetting.

Upanishads

For the Upanishads, pride grows from avidya, ignorance of what you really are.

  • Confusing the self with the body-mind. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.1 describes the first sense of "I am" arising, and 1.4.2 says the Self then feared, because there was a second. The separate "I" is where both pride and fear start.
  • Knowing things instead of knowing the Self. In Chandogya 7.1, Narada tells Sanatkumara he has learned all the Vedas and sciences yet is still a "knower of words only," not of the Self, and so is in sorrow.
  • Intellect cannot settle it. Katha 1.2.23 says the Self is not gained by teaching, by intellect or by much learning. The learned who think this is enough are the ones Katha 1.2.5 calls blind.
  • Taking the knower for the known. Kena 1.3-4 says the Self cannot be grasped by the eye, speech or mind, so the mind that claims to have mastered it has missed it.

Christianity

Christian writers see pride's roots in a distorted love of self and a forgetting of dependence on God.

  • The first fall. Isaiah 14:12-15 (the boast "I will make myself like the Most High") has long been read as the pattern of pride, and the Genesis temptation "you will be like God" (Genesis 3:5) repeats it for humans.
  • Self-love turned inward. Augustine, in City of God XIV.13, says pride is a perverse desire for exaltation: the soul abandons the one it should be rooted in and makes itself its own centre.
  • Success and comfort. Deuteronomy 8:11-17 warns that once people are fed and prosperous their heart will be lifted up and they will say "My power and the might of my own hand have gotten me this wealth."
  • Comparing and being praised. Luke 18:11 shows the Pharisee building his self-image by comparison with the tax collector. Gregory the Great also lists vainglory, a hunger for praise, as pride's child.

Buddhism

Buddhism explains conceit through craving, ignorance and the idea of a fixed self.

  • Belief in a self. Because we cling to a sense of "I", we compare it with others. The Anattalakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59) teaches that the five aggregates are not self, which removes the thing conceit is built on.
  • Intoxication with advantages. AN 3.39 names the pride of youth, of health and of life, and says it makes people act badly in body, speech and mind.
  • Karmic habit. MN 135 (Culakammavibhanga Sutta) says that being arrogant and haughty, and not honouring those worthy of honour, leads to a low birth. The sutta frames this as the result of actions, not a verdict on any group of people today.
  • Residue even in the advanced. SN 22.89 shows the "I am" conceit survives until the final stage, so pride is not only for the crude.

Philosophical

Philosophers locate pride in ignorance of oneself, love of honour, and mistaking luck for merit.

  • Not knowing what you don't know. In the Apology (21c-22e), Socrates finds that the politicians, poets and craftsmen he questions believe they are wise about everything because they are skilled in one thing.
  • Love of honour. Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3) says the vain person overrates himself and is easily taken in by the outward goods of wealth and power, which he takes for the real worth that deserves honour.
  • Opinion over reality. Epictetus (Enchiridion 5) says people are troubled not by things but by their judgments of things, and our judgment of ourselves is no exception. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 12.4) notes how much we value others' opinions despite loving ourselves best.
  • Self-conceit. Kant, in the Critique of Practical Reason, calls the claim to be the standard of worth "self-conceit," and says the moral law "strikes down" that claim and so produces humility.
