Deadly sin

Pride

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Pride is an inflated view of yourself that you protect at the cost of truth and of other people. Psychologists split it in two. Authentic pride follows real effort ("I worked hard and it paid off") and tends to go with self-respect and kindness. Hubristic pride is about being better than others ("I am superior") and tends to go with arrogance, aggression and fragile self-esteem.

The sin is the second kind. Its everyday signs are:

  • Finding it hard to say "I was wrong" or "I don't know".
  • Taking credit and passing on blame.
  • Treating advice as an insult.
  • Needing to be the smartest or most important person in the room.

Related ideas: narcissism, the self-serving bias (crediting yourself for success and others or luck for failure), and the Dunning-Kruger effect, where people with low skill in an area overestimate their ability in it.

Hinduism

Hinduism has several words for pride, each with a different shade. Ahamkara is the "I-maker", the sense of being a separate self that owns and does things. Mada is intoxication with one's own status or power, and it is one of the six inner enemies (shadripu). Darpa and abhimana mean arrogance and conceit.

The Gita lists them plainly. In 16.4, Krishna names hypocrisy, arrogance (darpa), conceit (abhimana), anger, harshness and ignorance as the marks of those drawn toward the asuri (demonic) nature. In 16.13-15 he gives the voice of such a person: "This much I have gained today... I am rich and well-born. Who is equal to me?"

At the root is the error in 3.27: all action is done by the gunas (the qualities of nature), yet the person deluded by ahamkara thinks, "I am the doer."

Humility is the opposite. In 13.8, amanitvam (absence of self-regard) heads the list of marks of true knowledge.

Upanishads

The Upanishads rarely use a word for "pride". They go to the root: the false belief that you already know, and the sense of a separate "I" that holds the belief.

Learned conceit. Katha 1.2.5 and Mundaka 1.2.8 describe people who live in ignorance yet think themselves wise and learned. They wander about "like blind men led by the blind." Kena 2.3 turns it around: "He who thinks he knows it, knows it not; he who thinks he does not know it, knows it."

The "I". Brihadaranyaka 1.4.1 says that in the beginning the Self looked around and saw nothing but itself, and its first words were "This is I" (Müller translation). The text treats this "I am" as the start of separateness, and so of fear and desire.

A student's pride. In Chandogya 6.1, Shvetaketu returns after twelve years of study, proud of his learning. His father Uddalaka asks whether he has asked for that teaching by which the unheard becomes heard, and Shvetaketu has to admit he has not.

This is related to pride and not identical to it: the texts speak of conceit about knowledge and of the separate "I", not of a sin of pride.

Christianity

In Christianity, pride (Latin superbia) is traditionally the first and gravest of the seven capital sins, the one from which others grow. It means putting yourself in God's place, or above your neighbour.

The Bible is blunt about it:

  • "Pride goes before destruction, and a haughty spirit before a fall" (Proverbs 16:18).
  • "God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble" (James 4:6; 1 Peter 5:5).
  • Jesus tells of a Pharisee who prays "God, I thank you that I am not like other people" and a tax collector who says only "God, be merciful to me, a sinner" (Luke 18:9-14), and concludes that "all who exalt themselves will be humbled" (Luke 14:11).

Church writers carried this further. Augustine called pride the beginning of every sin, citing Sirach 10:13, and Aquinas treats it in the Summa as a turning away from God that can sit under other sins.

Humility, in this tradition, is not self-hatred. It is seeing yourself truthfully as a creature who has received everything.

Buddhism

The Buddhist word is mana, usually translated "conceit". It is the habit of measuring yourself against others, whether as better, equal or worse, and it is one of the traditional mental fetters that bind a person to suffering. It is subtler than boasting. Feeling inferior is also mana, because it still takes the comparison seriously.

The deepest layer is asmi-mana, the "I am" conceit. In the Khemaka Sutta (SN 22.89), monks who have already let go of the five lower fetters say they still have a residual sense of "I am". Khemaka compares it to the smell left in a cloth that has been washed but not yet scented: it fades only with deeper insight into the five aggregates.

The suttas also show crude pride. In the Sukhumala Sutta (AN 3.39), the Buddha describes how he once lived in luxury, and how the intoxication with youth, health and life drops away when you see they are not secure. The Dhammapada (verse 74) describes the fool who wants everyone to think "this was done by me."

Buddhism does not rank itself above other traditions here. It simply asks you to see that the "I" being defended is a changing process, not a fixed possession.

Philosophical

Greek and later European philosophers treat pride as a failure of self-knowledge and of proportion.

Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics (Book 4, chapters 3 and 7) the mean is magnanimity, claiming honour that you actually deserve. One extreme is the vain person (chaunos), who claims honour beyond their worth. The other is the small-souled person, who claims less than they deserve. Pride as vice is therefore a mismatch between self-image and reality.

Socrates. In Plato's Apology (21a-23b), Socrates tests people who think they are wise and finds that he is wiser only in knowing that he does not know.

The Stoics. Epictetus (Enchiridion 13) advises: "If you want to improve, be content to be thought foolish." Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 12.4) notes the oddity that we love ourselves more than others yet care more about their opinion than our own.

Kant. In the Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Virtue), arrogance (Hochmut) is the demand that others think little of themselves in comparison with us. It breaks the duty of respect owed to every person.
