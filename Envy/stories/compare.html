Envy

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how envy works and how people step out of it.

Sara's story

Sara and Meera joined the same design studio on the same day and became friends. Three years later Meera was made team lead. Sara said congratulations, went home and couldn't sleep.

Over the next months she noticed herself checking Meera's posts, tallying her wins and finding flaws in her work. In meetings she held back ideas Meera might use. Once she let a small mistake in Meera's file go by without pointing it out. Meera noticed the distance and stopped asking Sara to lunch.

The shift came when Sara caught herself enjoying a client's complaint about Meera. It felt ugly. She talked to a mentor, who asked: "What exactly do you want that she has?" After a long pause Sara said: "To be trusted with something big."

What helped:

  1. She named it as envy, and what it pointed to (wanting responsibility).
  2. She asked for a stretch project and got one.
  3. She unfollowed Meera's work account on her phone for a month.
  4. She told Meera, "I've been jealous and it wasn't fair to you." Meera admitted she had been scared of leading her friend.
  5. She practised small, real congratulations and noticed the sting fade.

The friendship came back, more honest than before.

What it shows: Envy is often a map of what you want. Used as a map it can be a spur. Left alone it turns into spite.

Hinduism

Manthara and Kaikeyi

In the Ramayana (Ayodhya Kanda), King Dasharatha decides to crown his eldest son Rama. All of Ayodhya celebrates, including Queen Kaikeyi, who loves Rama as her own son and rewards the servant who brings the news.

That servant, Manthara, a hunchbacked maid who came with Kaikeyi from her father's house, cannot bear it. She sees only that Kausalya, Rama's mother, will rise, and that Kaikeyi and her son Bharata will be pushed aside. She hurries to Kaikeyi and works on her fears, step by step: Rama's mother will be queen mother, you will be her servant, and Bharata will be at Rama's mercy.

At first Kaikeyi rejects her. Manthara persists, and reminds her of two boons the king once promised. Kaikeyi finally asks Dasharatha for them: Bharata to be crowned and Rama exiled for fourteen years.

Dasharatha dies of grief, Rama goes to the forest, and Bharata, when he returns, rejects the throne and rebukes his mother.

What it shows: One person's envy of another's good fortune can be passed on, like a spark. Kaikeyi's love was real, and envy still turned it.

Sources

  • Valmiki Ramayana, Ayodhya Kanda (external link), chapters on Manthara's counsel and Kaikeyi's boons

Upanishads

A parable: the two birds

The Mundaka Upanishad (3.1.1-2) and the Shvetashvatara Upanishad (4.6-7) use an image from the Rig Veda: two birds, close companions, sit on the same tree.

One of them eats the sweet fruit of the tree. It hops from branch to branch, tastes, wants more, and sometimes finds bitter fruit. It feels lost and helpless and grieves.

The other bird does not eat. It simply looks on, calm and bright.

The text says that when the first bird, which has been caught up in the fruit, turns and sees the other, the Lord, and sees his greatness, its sorrow passes.

What it shows: Envy lives in the bird that is busy comparing fruit. The teaching is not to find better fruit but to notice the witness that is already there, and is the same as you.

Sources

  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) 3.1.1-2
  • Shvetashvatara Upanishad (external link) 4.6-7

Retold and shortened. Verse numbers and key quotations were checked against Max Müller's translation (Sacred Books of the East 1 and 15) on sacred-texts.com on 2026-11-14; other translations differ in wording and sometimes in numbering. Reading it as a remedy for envy is an interpretation.

Christianity

Joseph and his brothers

Genesis 37 introduces Joseph, his father Jacob's favourite son. Jacob gives him a long robe, and the brothers "hated him, and could not speak peaceably to him." Joseph also tells them dreams in which they bow down to him, which makes it worse. Acts 7:9 says the patriarchs, "jealous of Joseph, sold him into Egypt."

The brothers first plan to kill him, then sell him to traders for twenty pieces of silver, and tell their father an animal killed him.

In Egypt, Joseph goes through slavery and prison and rises to rule under Pharaoh. Years later, famine brings the brothers to buy grain. They do not recognise him. After testing them, he tells them who he is (Genesis 45). They are afraid. He says, "Do not be distressed or angry with yourselves because you sold me here, for God sent me before you to preserve life."

After their father dies, the brothers fear revenge. Joseph weeps and tells them: "Even though you intended to do harm to me, God intended it for good" (Genesis 50:20).

What it shows: Envy led to a crime, but the story ends in forgiveness.

  • Acts 7:9 (external link)

Buddhism

Devadatta

Devadatta was the Buddha's cousin, and he joined the Sangha. Tradition says he developed psychic powers, but also a hunger for status and a growing envy of the Buddha's standing. The account is in the Vinaya (Cullavagga, chapter 7).

At one point he asked the Buddha to hand over leadership of the Sangha, saying the Buddha was old. The Buddha refused, saying he would not even hand it to Sariputta and Moggallana, and certainly not to someone who has "swallowed spittle."

Devadatta held a grudge. He is said to have won over Prince Ajatasattu, and then to have tried to have the Buddha killed, by hired men, by a rolling rock, and by a maddened elephant. He also tried to split the Sangha by pressing five strict rules, and some young monks followed him until Sariputta and Moggallana brought them back.

Traditional accounts say Devadatta's end was grim, though they also hold that he can eventually be released.

What it shows: Envy of status, even among those who have renounced the world, can grow into harm. It is why the tradition teaches mudita.

Sources

  • Vinaya, Cullavagga 7 (Devadatta) (external link)

Retold from memory and shortened. Accounts of Devadatta come from later monastic texts and are traditional narrative, not independent history. Details such as the phrase about spittle and the final fate vary by source and were not checked.

Philosophical

Aristides the Just

Plutarch, in his Life of Aristides (chapter 7), tells of the Athenian statesman Aristides, known as "the Just." In the 480s BC, Athenians could vote to ostracise a citizen, sending him into exile for ten years, by writing his name on a piece of pottery.

Aristides was a political rival of Themistocles. As the vote was held, says Plutarch, an illiterate farmer who did not know Aristides handed him his shard and asked him to write "Aristides" on it. Aristides asked whether the man had any grievance against him. The man said no: "I don't even know the fellow, but I am tired of hearing him everywhere called the Just" (Perrin translation). Aristides wrote his own name and gave it back.

He was ostracised. Plutarch adds that when he left, he prayed that Athens would never need him back.

What it shows: The man's only complaint was another's good name. It is envy in its plainest form: no harm done, only a reputation that stings.

Sources

  • Plutarch, Life of Aristides 7 (external link) (Perrin translation). Text read in this session; Plutarch himself calls the ostracism an outlet for "envious dislike" of Aristides' reputation.

Retold and shortened. Plutarch wrote centuries after the events, and the anecdote may be legend.
