Envy

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Envy hurts the envier first.

  • Mood. Envy goes with lower well-being, resentment and rumination.
  • Relationships. Resentment of friends and colleagues sours closeness; people feel the coldness even if you say nothing.
  • Behaviour. Malicious envy can lead to gossip, undermining or withholding help. In experiments, people have sometimes paid a cost to bring down a better-off partner.
  • Wasted signal. Benign envy can point to something you value, but malicious envy turns that into spite and wastes the information.
  • Brain. One imaging study found that envy activates a region linked to pain, and that the rival's misfortune activates reward areas.

Sources

  • Smith & Kim (2007), Psychological Bulletin 133(1), 46-64. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.46 (external link)
  • Takahashi et al. (2009), "When your gain is my pain and your pain is my gain," Science 323(5916), 937-939. doi:10.1126/science.1165604 (external link). It was a small imaging study.

Hinduism

The Hindu texts name envy as a block to peace and to knowledge.

  • No freedom from bondage. Gita 3.32 says that those who carp at the teaching and do not follow it are "deluded in all knowledge" and lost. 3.31 gives the opposite: faith and freedom from envy bring release.
  • Inner enemy. Matsarya sits among the six shadripu that rob the mind of calm.
  • Falling to lower states. Gita 16.19-20 says the haters and the cruel are cast again and again into demoniac wombs, and fall to the lowest path.
  • Peace for the free. Gita 12.13-15 describes the devotee who hates no one, is friendly and compassionate, and is "not agitated by the world nor agitating it."
  • Epic example. In the Mahabharata, Shishupala's envy of Krishna ends in his death at the Rajasuya.

Upanishads

In the Upanishads the effect of envy is the same as that of any sense of separateness: sorrow.

  • The grieving bird. Mundaka 3.1.2 says the one who is attached "grieves, deluded in his helplessness" until he sees the Lord and his greatness.
  • Fear and delusion. Isha 6-7 says that only the one who sees unity is free from shrinking, delusion and sorrow. The rest keep them.
  • Wandering. Katha 2.1.10 (also Brihadaranyaka 4.4.19) says one goes from death to death who sees difference here.
  • No peace. Katha 2.2.13 says eternal peace belongs to those who perceive the one Self, not to others.

Christianity

Scripture is stark about what envy does.

  • Wasting the body. Proverbs 14:30: "jealousy makes the bones rot" (older NRSV: "passion").
  • Disorder. James 3:14-16: where there is envy and selfish ambition, there is disorder and "wickedness of every kind."
  • Violence. Cain's envy of Abel's accepted offering ends in murder (Genesis 4:3-8). Pilate "realized that it was out of jealousy that they had handed him over" (Matthew 27:18).
  • Exclusion from the community's peace. Galatians 5:19-21 lists envy among the works of the flesh, and Galatians 5:26 urges, "Let us not become conceited, competing against one another, envying one another."
  • Contrast. Romans 12:15 asks believers to "rejoice with those who rejoice."

Buddhism

Envy damages the mind, and the community.

  • No concentration. Dhammapada 365 says that a monk who envies others' gains does not attain concentration.
  • Unwholesome action. Envy rooted in aversion leads to harsh speech and harmful acts, which in the teaching on karma bring suffering to oneself and others.
  • Quarrels. Envy and stinginess feed disputes (the "quarrels and disputes" of the Kalahavivada Sutta, Sn 4.11, are traced to what is dear to us and to stinginess).
  • Splits in the Sangha. The tradition's best-known case is Devadatta, whose envy of the Buddha's standing led him to attempt to divide the community (Vinaya, Cullavagga 7).
  • Lost joy. Where mudita is absent, others' happiness is a source of pain, not delight.

Philosophical

Philosophers say envy distorts judgment and corrodes society.

  • Pain with no gain. Aristotle (Rhetoric 2.10) notes that the envious person is pained not for any benefit to themselves but by the other's good.
  • A vice, not an emotion alone. In NE 2.7, Aristotle lists envy (phthonos) among the dispositions that are always bad, with no mean.
  • Enmity. Kant says envy, ingratitude and malice are "diabolical" in being direct hatred of humanity (Doctrine of Virtue 36).
  • Social friction. Hume and Rousseau both see relative comparison as the source of resentment between ranks.
  • Unhappiness. Bertrand Russell (The Conquest of Happiness, ch. 6) calls envy one of the strongest causes of unhappiness, since the envious person takes pain from what they have as well as what others have.
