Deadly sin

Envy

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is to turn envy into information and then into goodwill.

  1. Name it. Say "this is envy." Unnamed envy shows up as irritation or criticism.
  2. Ask what it points to. What do I actually want? A skill, recognition, security? Envy is often a signal of a value.
  3. Convert it to a step. If the want is reachable, choose one action (a course, a conversation, a draft). This is the benign form.
  4. Cut the fuel. Mute or unfollow accounts that leave you feeling behind, and cut passive scrolling.
  5. Remember the unseen. You see their result, not their cost, doubts or luck.
  6. Practise active joy. Tell the person congratulations and mean it. Do it even when it's hard.
  7. Keep a gratitude list, and compare yourself with your past self.

Sources

  • van de Ven, Zeelenberg & Pieters (2009), Emotion 9(3), 419-429. doi:10.1037/a0015669 (external link)
  • Verduyn et al. (2015), J. Exp. Psychol.: General 144(2), 480-488. doi:10.1037/xge0000057 (external link)

Steps are general advice, not a tested programme. Citations were from memory and not re-checked. If envy or comparison is affecting your mental health, talk to a professional.

Hinduism

Hindu practice answers envy with equal vision, friendliness and contentment.

  • Equal vision. Gita 5.18 and 6.9 praise the one who sees all with an equal eye, and 6.32 the one who feels another's joy and sorrow as their own.
  • Friendliness and compassion. Gita 12.13: "adveshta sarva-bhutanam, maitrah karuna eva cha", hating no being, friendly and compassionate. The Yoga Sutras (1.33) recommend friendliness toward the happy, compassion toward the unhappy, gladness toward the virtuous and equanimity toward the wicked.
  • Contentment. Gita 12.19 and Yoga Sutras 2.42 (santosha).
  • Offer your actions. Gita 3.30 and 18.66 teach acting for the Divine and letting go of "mine."
  • Satsang and service, which put attention on others' good.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic cure is to see the one Self in all.

  • See all in the Self. Isha 6-7: the one who sees all beings in the Self and the Self in all beings no longer shrinks, and for them delusion and sorrow end.
  • Do not covet. Isha 1 gives the practice: leave the wealth of others to them and enjoy through renunciation.
  • Take the witness seat. The second bird of Mundaka 3.1.1-2 looks on without eating. Practise watching your own wants.
  • Study and reflect. The three steps of shravana, manana and nididhyasana (later Vedanta, built on Brihadaranyaka 2.4.5) turn knowledge of unity into experience.
  • Give and be kind. Taittiriya 1.11 advises giving in faith and with sympathy.

Christianity

Christian practice answers envy with gratitude, love and rejoicing with others.

  • Rejoice with those who rejoice (Romans 12:15). Start by praying for the person you envy.
  • Love. 1 Corinthians 13:4 says love "is not envious or boastful or arrogant." Practise the actions of love and the feeling often follows.
  • Gratitude. 1 Thessalonians 5:18 and 1 Corinthians 4:7 point to what has been received.
  • Trust God's generosity. Matthew 20:15 asks "Are you envious because I am generous?", and the answer is to accept grace given to others.
  • Contentment. Philippians 4:11-13: "I have learned to be content with whatever I have."
  • Different gifts. 1 Corinthians 12:14-26 says the body has many parts, and "If one member suffers, all suffer together with it; if one member is honored, all rejoice together with it."
  • Confession. Naming the sin in confession or to a trusted person takes away its secrecy.

Buddhism

Buddhism trains the opposite of envy: mudita, sympathetic joy.

  • Practise mudita. Bring to mind someone who is doing well and wish: "May your good fortune continue." Start with someone easy and move toward the person you envy. This is one of the four brahmaviharas (MN 7).
  • Metta. Loving-kindness for all beings (Sn 1.8, Karaniya Metta Sutta) softens the aversion that envy grows from.
  • Be content. Dhammapada 365 says do not despise what you have received or envy others' gains.
  • Notice the eight winds. AN 8.6: gain and loss, praise and blame are not the measure of a life. Observe envy as it comes and goes.
  • Dedicate merit. Rejoicing in others' good deeds (anumodana) is a standard practice in Buddhist communities.
  • See not-self. SN 22.59 loosens the idea of a fixed "me" to be ranked.

Philosophical

Philosophy gives ways to turn comparison into proportion.

  • Emulation, not envy. Aristotle (Rhetoric 2.11) separates envy from emulation: the emulous person is pained at not having the good thing and works to get it, rather than wanting the other to lose it.
  • Ask the price. Epictetus (Enchiridion 25): has someone been honoured above you? Consider what they paid for it, and whether you would pay the same.
  • Mind what is yours. The Stoic split between what is up to us and what is not (Enchiridion 1) puts others' success among the things we do not control.
  • Look wider. Marcus Aurelius (Meditations 9.30) advises seeing life from a wide view in which rank means little.
  • Duty of sympathy. Kant (Doctrine of Virtue 34) asks for sympathetic joy and sorrow as a duty to cultivate.
