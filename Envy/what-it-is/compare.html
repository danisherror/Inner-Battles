Deadly sin

Envy

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Envy is the pain of seeing someone else have something you want: a quality, a success, a possession or a relationship. It is different from jealousy, which is fear of losing something you have to a rival. Envy involves two people (you and the one you envy); jealousy involves three.

Psychologists describe two forms:

  • Benign envy pushes you to improve ("I want that too, and I can work for it").
  • Malicious envy wants the other person to lose what they have, and may lead to belittling, gossip or sabotage.

The sin is mainly the malicious kind. Its everyday signs include:

  • A sting when a friend announces good news.
  • Quietly enjoying someone's failure (schadenfreude).
  • Finding fault with people who are doing well.

Hinduism

The Sanskrit word is matsarya, envy or jealousy of others' good fortune, the sixth of the inner enemies (shadripu). Related words are asuya (finding fault, begrudging) and irshya (jealousy).

The Gita names the quality in its portrait of the asuri nature: in 16.18, such people hate the Divine in themselves and in others, and are full of asuya. By contrast, the person dear to Krishna is described as "hating no creature" (12.13), and in 3.31 those who follow the teaching with faith and "without envy" (anasuyantah) are freed from the bonds of action. The wise student is called anasuya ("free from envy") in 9.1 and 18.71.

Upanishads

The Upanishads have no single word for envy. They address it through what lies underneath: seeing others as separate from yourself.

The Isha Upanishad opens with "do not covet anyone's wealth" (verse 1) and goes on to the vision that cures it: the one who sees all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, "does not shrink from anything" (verse 6). In verse 7, for the one who sees the oneness of all, "what delusion and what sorrow can there be?"

The Mundaka (3.1.1-2) and Shvetashvatara (4.6-7) Upanishads give a picture of two birds on one tree. One eats the fruit and is troubled. The other looks on without eating. When the first sees the other, and its greatness, sorrow leaves it.

Envy appears here as one form of the sorrow that goes with believing you are separate and lacking.

This is related to envy and not identical to it: the texts have no word for begrudging another's good fortune, and speak of the separateness underneath.

Christianity

Christian tradition calls it envy (Latin invidia): sadness at another's good, and the wish to see them lose it. The Catechism says envy is a capital sin and describes it as sadness at a neighbour's goods and an immoderate desire to have them for oneself, even unjustly (CCC 2538). It is named in the tenth commandment's warning against coveting, and in the Bible's lists of vices (for example Galatians 5:19-21).

Proverbs 14:30 says "a tranquil mind gives life to the flesh, but jealousy makes the bones rot" (the older NRSV reads "passion"). Proverbs 27:4 asks, "Who can stand before jealousy?" James 3:16 says that where there is envy and selfish ambition there will be disorder and wickedness of every kind.

Augustine called envy the "diabolical" sin, because it is sorrow at another's good, and Aquinas treats it in Summa II-II q.36 as sorrow at someone's good seen as diminishing one's own.

Buddhism

The Pali word is issa, envy or jealousy: a mental state that finds others' success hard to bear. In the traditional lists of mental factors (Abhidhamma) it is an unwholesome factor, rooted in aversion (dosa), and it is often paired with macchariya, stinginess.

The Dhammapada says: "One should not despise what one has received, nor envy the gains of others. The monk who envies the gains of others does not attain to meditative absorption" (365, Buddharakkhita translation).

The positive counterpart is mudita, sympathetic joy, delight in the good fortune of others. It is one of the four brahmaviharas (divine abidings) alongside loving-kindness, compassion and equanimity, as in MN 7 (Vatthupama Sutta).

Philosophical

Philosophers define envy fairly consistently.

Aristotle. In the Rhetoric (2.10) envy is pain at the apparent good fortune of others who are our equals, not to gain anything but because they have it. He separates it from emulation (2.11), which is the pain of lacking what a good person has and so strives to get it.

Plato. In the Philebus (47d-50e) envy is a mixed feeling of pain and pleasure at others' misfortune. In the Phaedrus (247a) "jealousy has no place in the celestial choir" (Jowett translation).

Kant. In the Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Virtue, sections 36-37), envy, ingratitude and malice are the vices of hatred of humanity, with envy as the tendency to see others' well-being as a loss to oneself.

Hume. In the Treatise (2.2.8) envy arises from comparison with someone close to us.
