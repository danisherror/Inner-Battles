Deadly sin

Envy

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Envy grows from comparison and from what comparison says about you.

  • Social comparison. People judge themselves by looking at others (Festinger's theory). Envy is strongest toward people close to us in age, field and background.
  • Self-relevance. We envy most in areas that matter to our self-image. Nobody envies a stranger's talent in something they don't care about.
  • Low or fragile self-worth. Someone else's success feels like proof of your own shortfall.
  • Feeling blocked. Envy is stronger when we believe the gap can't be closed, and turns malicious when it seems unfair.
  • Social media. Constant, curated views of other people's best moments feed comparison. Some studies link passive scrolling with lower mood.

Sources

  • Festinger (1954), "A theory of social comparison processes," Human Relations 7(2), 117-140. doi:10.1177/001872675400700202 (external link)
  • Smith & Kim (2007), Psychological Bulletin 133(1), 46-64. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.46 (external link)
  • Verduyn et al. (2015), "Passive Facebook usage undermines affective well-being," J. Exp. Psychol.: General 144(2), 480-488. doi:10.1037/xge0000057 (external link)

Citations from memory, not re-checked. The social-media findings are contested and effects are small on average.

Hinduism

Hindu thought traces envy to separateness, desire and the guna of rajas.

  • Duality. Seeing "me" and "them" as fundamentally apart makes another's gain feel like my loss. Gita 5.18 praises the sage who sees with an equal eye in all.
  • Unfulfilled desire. Envy follows when desire is aimed at what others have. Gita 2.62-63 describes how fixing the mind on objects leads to desire, anger and delusion.
  • Ego. Gita 16.18 links asuya with ahamkara, power and arrogance.
  • Lack of contentment. Gita 12.19 describes the dear devotee as content with whatever comes. Envy is the opposite.
  • Karma as a lens. Another's fortune is often explained by their past actions, which in tradition is a reason not to resent it.

Upanishads

For the Upanishads, the root is the mistaken sense that you are a separate, lacking self.

  • Fear and lack from a "second." Brihadaranyaka 1.4.2-3 says the Self felt fear because there was a second, and desire for that second. Where there is another, there is comparing and wanting.
  • The two birds. Mundaka 3.1.1-2: the bird caught up in the fruit grieves in its helplessness. The grief belongs to forgetting the other bird, the witness, which is the same Self.
  • Ignorance of unity. Isha 7: for the one who knows all beings have become the Self, there is no delusion or sorrow, so the lack of that knowledge is the cause.
  • Valuing outer things. Katha 2.1.1 says people look outward and not within, so they measure themselves by what they see in others.

Christianity

Christian writers find envy's roots in pride, a distorted view of one's own worth, and distrust of God's goodness.

  • From pride. Gregory the Great (Moralia 31.45) lists envy as a daughter of vainglory. If I must be the best, another's excellence threatens me.
  • Suspicion of God's fairness. In Matthew 20:1-16 the workers hired first complain about those hired last, and the owner asks, "Are you envious because I am generous?" Envy objects to grace given to someone else.
  • The devil's envy. Wisdom 2:24 says death came into the world through the devil's envy.
  • Zero-sum thinking. The older brother in Luke 15:25-32 sees the father's welcome of his brother as a loss to himself.
  • Disordered love. Aquinas (ST II-II q.36) says we grieve at another's good when we see it as diminishing our own excellence.

Buddhism

Buddhism sees envy as coming from aversion, attachment to self and gain, and attention to comparison.

  • Rooted in aversion. In the Abhidhamma analysis, issa is a factor of the hatred (dosa) family: dislike of another's good.
  • Attachment to gain and status. AN 8.6 lists the eight worldly winds: gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. A mind pushed by them envies those who get more of what it likes.
  • Conceit. Comparison ("they have more than I") is a form of mana, as in SN 22.89.
  • Stinginess. Macchariya, clinging to what one has, goes with envy at what others have.
  • Not seeing not-self. If you take the self as fixed and in need of defending, others' rise feels like your fall (SN 22.59).

Sources

Philosophical

Philosophers see envy as an effect of comparison and of the love of honour.

  • Equals. Aristotle (Rhetoric 2.10) says we envy those near to us in time, place, age and reputation, and those who compete with us for the same honour.
  • Wounded self-love. Kant (Doctrine of Virtue, 36) describes envy as the tendency to view others' well-being as a reproach to ourselves.
  • Relative standing. Hume (Treatise 2.2.8) says envy comes from comparing our situation with that of someone only a little ahead, not with someone far above us.
  • Love of honour and rank. Rousseau's amour-propre (Discourse on Inequality) describes the habit of valuing oneself by how one stands against others.
  • Valuing what is not in our control. Epictetus (Enchiridion 25) says to ask what price the other paid for the honour, and whether you would pay it.
