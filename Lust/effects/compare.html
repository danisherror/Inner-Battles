Deadly sin

Lust

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

When lust runs a person's choices, the costs tend to show up in four places.

  • Relationships. Trust breaks when desire leads to secrecy, infidelity or treating a partner as a means to an end. Even without a breach, constant objectifying can make real intimacy feel flat.
  • Time and focus. Hours go to chasing or consuming, and attention for work, study and people shrinks.
  • Mood and self-image. The pattern of promise, lapse and regret feeds shame, which tends to make the cycle worse rather than better.
  • Real-world harm. In more serious cases it leads to risk-taking, financial cost, or harm to other people.

The ICD-11 entry for compulsive sexual behaviour disorder names this directly: it requires marked distress or significant impairment in personal, family, social, educational or occupational life. If you recognise that level of impact, speaking to a doctor or therapist is a sound step, not a failure.

Sources

  • WHO ICD-11, 6C72 (summary of the entry) (external link). Secondary site; confirm the wording at icd.who.int.

Hinduism

The Gita describes the consequences as a falling staircase. In 2.62-63, after desire comes anger, then delusion, then confusion of memory, then loss of discernment, and from that loss the person is destroyed.

Other effects the tradition points to:

  • Being devoured. In 3.37 desire is called mahashano, all-devouring: the more it is fed, the more it demands, and it is never filled.
  • Covered knowledge. In 3.38-39 Krishna compares knowledge being covered by desire to fire by smoke, a mirror by dust, and an embryo by the womb. The discerning mind is clouded and cannot see clearly.
  • Self-destruction. Along with anger and greed, desire is called a gate to hell (16.21), meaning ruin of the self.

The effect is less a punishment from outside than a loss of clarity and freedom. The person who was meant to steer the senses is steered by them.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe the effects in terms of where desire takes you.

  • Destiny follows desire. According to the Brihadaranyaka (4.4.5), as a person's desire is, so is their will; as their will, so their deed; as their deed, so what they become. A life led by lust is steered toward what lust leads to.
  • Wandering senses. In the Katha chariot image, the undisciplined person's senses run like unmanageable horses (1.3.5), so they are carried wherever the horses go.
  • Missing the good. Choosing the pleasant over the good means losing what is of lasting value (Katha 1.2).
  • Staying in the cycle. The tradition holds that those caught up in desire remain bound to repeated birth and death, while the one free from desire is freed (a theme in the Brihadaranyaka and Katha).

In short, the cost is loss of direction and of freedom, and in the long view, of liberation (moksha).

Christianity

The Bible speaks of the effects of lust in terms of damage to oneself, to others and to one's relationship with God.

  • Death as the end of the path. James 1:15 says that sin, when it is full-grown, brings forth death, and the sequence begins with desire.
  • Harm to one's own body. Paul says sexual sin is a sin against one's own body, which he calls a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:18-19).
  • Harm to others. Using a person as an object wrongs them. The Catechism says pornography "perverts the conjugal act, the intimate giving of spouses to each other" and does grave injury to the dignity of those involved (2354).
  • Exclusion from the good life. Galatians 5:19-21 lists sexual immorality among works of the flesh and contrasts them with the fruit of the Spirit (5:22-23): love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness and self-control.
  • The seriousness of the heart. Jesus uses strong language in Matthew 5:29-30, meaning that what feeds sin should be cut away at whatever cost.

Christianity also keeps open the way back: the same Scriptures stress forgiveness and a new start for those who turn from sin.

Buddhism

The Buddhist account of what lust does is blunt and practical.

  • It never satisfies. The Dhammapada says that there is no satisfying sensual desires, even with a rain of gold coins, and that sensual pleasures give little satisfaction and much pain (Dhp 186-187).
  • It brings grief and fear. Verses 215-216 say that lust and craving give rise to sorrow and fear, and that for the one free of craving there is neither.
  • It binds. Attachment creates bonds, and the person who is attached to what they love suffers when they are parted from it (Dhp 210-212).
  • It clouds the mind. In the classic teaching, sensual desire is the first of the Five Hindrances that block clear meditation, along with ill will, sloth, restlessness and doubt.
  • It keeps the cycle turning. Craving is what drives rebirth in the traditional view, so unchecked desire keeps suffering going.

The effect is a restless dissatisfaction, which the Buddha calls dukkha.

Philosophical

Philosophers focus on what lust does to freedom, character and our view of other people.

  • Loss of freedom. The person ruled by appetite is not choosing. In the Republic (Book IX) Plato compares the tyrannical person, whose soul is full of "meanness and slavery", to a tyrannised city.
  • Damaged character. For Aristotle, repeated indulgence builds a vice. Each choice makes the next excess easier and moderation harder (Nicomachean Ethics II).
  • Instrumentalising people. In Kant's ethics, treating a person merely as a means is a wrong. Sexual desire without regard for the person is a standard example in his lectures on ethics.
  • Pain in the craving. Aristotle says the self-indulgent person craves all pleasant things and is led by appetite to choose them at the cost of everything else, so "he is pained both when he fails to get them and when he is merely craving for them" (Nicomachean Ethics III).

The common thread is that excess does not buy happiness. It trades long-term flourishing (eudaimonia) for short-term pleasure.
