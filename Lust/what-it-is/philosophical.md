Philosophers have long treated lust as a question of **reason versus appetite**.

**Plato** pictured the soul as a charioteer (reason) driving two horses, one noble and one unruly (appetite). Lust is the unruly horse pulling the chariot off course.

**Aristotle** gave the most careful account. In *Nicomachean Ethics* Book III he defines temperance (*sophrosyne*) as the right middle ground concerning bodily pleasures, mainly those of touch and taste, including food, drink and sex. Its two failures are *self-indulgence* (too much, too eagerly) and, more rarely, insensibility (too little). In Book VII he separates the person who *knows* the right thing but is carried away anyway (*akrasia*, weakness of will) from the person who feels the pull yet holds firm (*enkrateia*, self-control).

**The Stoics** agreed that the aim is not to kill desire but to keep judgment in charge of it.

Across these traditions the lesson is shared. Lust is less about sex than about a **loss of freedom**, where wanting decides for you.
