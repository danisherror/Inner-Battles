Lust

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Today lust is usually described as sexual desire that has stopped being a healthy part of life and has started to run it. Desire itself is normal. The modern question is not "do you feel desire?" but "who is in charge, you or the urge?"

Psychologists draw a line between a high sex drive and a loss of control. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 lists compulsive sexual behaviour disorder as a persistent pattern of failing to control intense, repetitive sexual urges, leading to repeated sexual behaviour, with marked distress or serious harm to daily life. It is classed as an impulse-control problem, and the diagnosis excludes distress that comes only from moral disapproval of one's own urges. Whether it should be called an addiction is still debated among researchers.

Most people who struggle with lust never reach that clinical level. For them it shows up as:

  • seeing people as bodies rather than whole persons,
  • hours lost to explicit content or endless scrolling,
  • using desire to escape stress, loneliness or boredom,
  • choices that hurt a partner or break a commitment.

Hinduism

In Hindu thought the word is kama, and it is not simply bad. Kama, meaning desire, pleasure and love, is one of the four legitimate aims of life (purusharthas), alongside dharma, artha and moksha. It becomes a problem when it breaks free of dharma and turns into craving that cannot be satisfied.

That unruly form is named as one of the six inner enemies (shadripu): kama, krodha (anger), lobha (greed), moha (delusion), mada (pride) and matsarya (envy).

The Bhagavad Gita is direct about it. In 3.37, Krishna says it is desire and anger, born of rajas (the quality of restless passion), that are all-devouring and greatly sinful, and that one should know them as the enemy here. In 16.21 desire, anger and greed are called the three gates to self-destruction.

The same Gita also says, in 7.11, that desire which is not against dharma is divine. So the teaching is about disciplined versus uncontrolled desire, not about hating the body.

Upanishads

The Upanishads, the philosophical texts at the end of the Vedas, rarely talk about "sin" as such. They speak about desire (kama) and about two paths a person can choose.

The Katha Upanishad presents them as shreyas, the good, and preyas, the pleasant. The wise choose the good, while those who are shortsighted choose the pleasant (Katha 1.2.1-2). Lust is the clearest example of the pleasant that pulls against the good.

The same text gives the famous chariot image (Katha 1.3.3-9). The Self is the rider, the body the chariot, the intellect the charioteer, the mind the reins, the senses the horses, and the sense-objects the roads. A person without discernment has unmanageable horses. A person with discernment has well-trained ones.

The Brihadaranyaka Upanishad adds that a person is shaped by what they desire: as the desire, so the will; as the will, so the deed; as the deed, so the destiny (4.4.5). Desire is therefore not a small matter: it forms who we become.

So in this view lust is desire that has taken the reins from the intellect, and has pointed the self toward the pleasant instead of the good.

This is related to lust and not identical to it: the texts speak of desire (kama) in general, not of sexual desire as a separate sin.

Christianity

Lust is one of the seven capital (deadly) sins. The Catechism of the Catholic Church defines it as "disordered desire for or inordinate enjoyment of sexual pleasure," and says sexual pleasure is morally disordered "when sought for itself, isolated from its procreative and unitive purposes" (paragraph 2351).

In the Bible the concern reaches beyond the act to the heart. In the Sermon on the Mount, Jesus teaches that looking at someone with lustful intent is already adultery in the heart (Matthew 5:27-28). Paul calls believers to flee sexual immorality and describes the body as a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Corinthians 6:18-20). James traces the path: desire, when it is fed, gives birth to sin (James 1:14-15).

The key idea is treating a person as an object. Christianity holds that every person bears the image of God, so using someone as a means to pleasure, even only in thought or through a screen, offends both their dignity and your own. The Catechism says in its discussion of pornography that it "does grave injury to the dignity of its participants" because each one "becomes an object of base pleasure" (2354).

Christianity also affirms that sexuality is a good gift of creation. Lust is its distortion, not the thing itself.

Buddhism

In Buddhism the nearest idea is kama-tanha, craving for sensual pleasure, and the related kama-raga, sensual lust. It is one of the three forms of craving the Buddha names in his first sermon, alongside craving for existence and craving for non-existence.

It sits inside the Second Noble Truth: the origin of suffering (dukkha) is craving, and sense-craving is the most familiar kind. Lust is also one of the traditional "three poisons" (greed or lust, hatred, delusion) that keep the mind in a cycle of dissatisfaction.

Buddhism does not teach hatred of the body or of pleasure. The Buddha describes the Middle Path, which avoids both sense-indulgence and self-mortification. The problem is not that pleasure exists, but that the mind clings to it and expects it to deliver lasting satisfaction, which it cannot.

For lay followers, the Five Precepts include a commitment to refrain from sexual misconduct, meaning harm through sexuality. Monastics follow stricter celibacy.

Philosophical

Philosophers have long treated lust as a question of reason versus appetite.

Plato pictured the soul in the Phaedrus as a charioteer (reason) driving two horses, one "noble and of noble breed" and the other "ignoble and of ignoble breed". The ignoble horse is usually read as appetite, and lust as that horse pulling the chariot off course.

Aristotle gave the most careful account. In Nicomachean Ethics Book III he defines temperance (sophrosyne) as the right middle ground concerning bodily pleasures, mainly those of touch and taste, including food, drink and sex. Its two failures are self-indulgence (too much, too eagerly) and, more rarely, insensibility (too little). In Book VII he separates the person who knows the right thing but is carried away anyway (akrasia, weakness of will) from the person who feels the pull yet holds firm (enkrateia, self-control).

The Stoics put the weight on judgment. Epictetus advises that when you receive the impression of a pleasure you "guard yourself against being carried away by it" and let it wait (Encheiridion 34, Long's numbering). This is a summary of how the Stoics deal with the impulse, and not a claim that they had a separate account of lust.

Across these traditions the lesson is shared. Lust is less about sex than about a loss of freedom, where wanting decides for you.
