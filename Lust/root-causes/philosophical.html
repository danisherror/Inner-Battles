Deadly sin

Lust

Desire that overrides respect, commitment and judgment.

Philosophical lens

Root causes

Philosophers locate the cause in an imbalance inside the person.

  • Plato: in the Phaedrus the soul is a charioteer driving two horses, one "noble and of noble breed" and the other "ignoble and of ignoble breed"; when appetite is not governed by reason, the charioteer is pulled off course. (The reading of the ignoble horse as appetite is the standard interpretation, not a phrase in that passage.)
  • Aristotle: self-indulgence is a vice formed by habit. We become self-indulgent by repeatedly choosing excess, and temperate by repeatedly choosing the mean. He also separates akrasia, where a person knows better but is overcome by passion or impulsiveness, from vice proper, where the person has come to think the excess is good (Nicomachean Ethics II and VII).
  • The Stoics: passions come from mistaken judgments, such as the belief that this pleasure is essential to my happiness. In Epictetus's words, people are disturbed "not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things" (Encheiridion 5, Long translation). Correct the belief and the passion loosens.

Put together: lust grows from habit, false belief about what will make us happy, and a weak hold of reason over appetite.
