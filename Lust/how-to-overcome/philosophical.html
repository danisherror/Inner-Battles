How to overcome it

The philosophical schools agree on one thing: self-mastery is a skill built by practice.

  1. Form habits (Aristotle). We become temperate by doing temperate acts. Start small and repeat, since character is shaped by what we do over and over.
  2. Aim for the mean, not for zero. Temperance is not hatred of pleasure. The goal is enjoyment that stays under the guidance of reason.
  3. Examine the belief (Stoics). Ask: do I really need this to be happy? Epictetus says people are disturbed "not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things" (Encheiridion 5, Long translation). On pleasure in particular he advises: "If you have received the impression of any pleasure, guard yourself against being carried away by it; but let the thing wait for you" (Encheiridion 34 in Long's numbering).
  4. Choose your company and environment. Aristotle says "a certain training in virtue arises also from the company of the good" (Nicomachean Ethics IX.12), and Epictetus advises resorting "to the society of noble and just men" (Discourses 2.18, Long translation).
  5. Know your weak points. Since the akratic person knows the good but fails, plan ahead for the moments you are weakest, rather than relying on resolve in the moment.
  6. Redirect toward a worthwhile life. The point of self-control is eudaimonia, a flourishing life: work, friendship, learning and love that you can respect.
