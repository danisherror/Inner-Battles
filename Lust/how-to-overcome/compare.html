Modern

These are general, practical habits that therapists and self-help guides commonly suggest. They are not a treatment plan.

  1. Find your triggers. Keep a simple log for two weeks: time, place, mood and what you were doing before an urge. Patterns usually appear fast.
  2. Change the environment, not just your willpower. Use content blockers, keep the phone out of the bedroom, and avoid being alone and bored at night with a screen.
  3. Meet the real need. If it is loneliness, schedule time with people. If it is stress, use exercise, sleep or a walk.
  4. Delay and redirect. When an urge comes, wait ten minutes and do something physical or absorbing. Urges often rise and fall without being acted on.
  5. Drop the shame spiral. After a slip, return to the plan the same day. Self-hatred tends to restart the cycle.
  6. Build real intimacy. Honest conversation, shared activity and trust do more than any rule.
  7. Get help if it is out of control. A therapist or doctor can help, especially if the pattern harms your relationships or daily life or feels compulsive. A talk with a trusted friend counts as a first step too.

Hinduism

Hindu teaching offers a path of discipline, redirection and devotion.

  1. Know the enemy. In 3.37 Krishna names desire and anger as the foe. In 3.41-43 he advises starting with control of the senses, then understanding that the senses are subordinate to the mind, the mind to intellect, and intellect to the Self, and finally steadying the mind by the Self.
  2. Practice and detachment. To Arjuna's worry that the mind is restless (6.35), Krishna answers: through abhyasa (repeated practice) and vairagya (non-attachment).
  3. Guard the input. Since the chain begins with dwelling on sense objects (2.62), watch what you look at and think about, and who you keep company with (satsang).
  4. Give desire a higher object. Redirect energy into service (seva), study, meditation, japa or devotion (bhakti), so that the mind has something worthier to hold.
  5. Live by dharma. Desire that stays within one's duties and commitments is not the enemy (7.11). Many traditions also value brahmacharya (moderation or restraint of sexual energy) at different stages of life.
  6. Cultivate sattva. Regular routines, simple food, and early rising are said to calm rajas and tamas.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic remedy is to put discernment back in the driver's seat, and to turn desire toward the highest object.

  1. Choose the good over the pleasant. Katha 1.2 presents this as the basic act of wisdom.
  2. Train the chariot. Let the intellect be the charioteer, hold the mind's reins firmly, and keep the senses under control, like well-trained horses (Katha 1.3.3-6).
  3. Reshape your desire. Since desire becomes will, deed and destiny (Brihadaranyaka 4.4.5), change what you desire at the root, and the rest follows.
  4. Study and reflect (shravana, manana, nididhyasana). Hear the teaching, think it through, and meditate on it until it becomes your own.
  5. Practise self-restraint and brahmacharya. The Upanishads praise discipline of the senses and of sexual energy as part of the student's training.
  6. Realise the Self. The deepest answer is knowing that you are already complete, which removes the need for outer fulfilment. The Brihadaranyaka and Katha both speak of a person becoming free when the desires of the heart are cast away.
  7. Seek a teacher. The Upanishads are taught person to person, and Katha 1.2.8 and 1.2.9 stress the need for a qualified guide.

Christianity

Christianity pairs effort with grace: you act, and you also ask for help beyond yourself.

  1. Flee and don't flirt with it. Paul says to flee sexual immorality (1 Corinthians 6:18) and to flee the passions of youth while pursuing righteousness, faith, love and peace with others (2 Timothy 2:22).
  2. Guard your eyes and heart. Job made a covenant with his eyes not to look lustfully (Job 31:1). Proverbs 4:23 says to guard the heart, because from it flow the springs of life.
  3. Watch and pray. Jesus says to watch and pray so you do not fall into temptation (Matthew 26:41).
  4. Rely on the Spirit. Paul says to walk by the Spirit and you will not carry out the desires of the flesh (Galatians 5:16), and the fruit of the Spirit includes self-control (5:22-23).
  5. Expect a way out. 1 Corinthians 10:13 promises that no temptation is beyond what you can bear, and that God provides a way of escape.
  6. Renew the mind. Romans 12:2 speaks of being transformed by the renewing of the mind.
  7. Seek community and confession. The Catechism names self-knowledge, ascesis, obedience to God's commandments, the moral virtues and fidelity to prayer as means to chastity (2340), and says chastity "blossoms in friendship" (2347). James 5:16 urges confessing sins to one another.
  8. Remember forgiveness. A fall is not the end. 1 John 1:9 promises forgiveness to those who confess.

Buddhism

The Buddha's answer is the Fourth Noble Truth, the Noble Eightfold Path, a middle way between indulgence and harsh self-denial.

  1. See it clearly. Right understanding means seeing that craving brings suffering and that it can end.
  2. Keep the precepts. Right action includes avoiding sexual misconduct, which protects you and others from harm.
  3. Make effort. Right effort means not feeding the unwholesome state that has arisen and cultivating wholesome states in its place.
  4. Practise mindfulness. Right mindfulness means noticing the urge, the sensation and the thought as they arise, without being carried off by them. Craving rises and passes when it is watched.
  5. Guard the senses. Be careful what you look at and linger on.
  6. Reflect on impermanence. Remember that pleasure fades, and that clinging to it brings sorrow (Dhp 186-187, 215-216).
  7. Develop loving-kindness (metta). Seeing others as persons with their own lives counters objectifying.
  8. Meditate. Concentration steadies the mind, which is why sensual desire is treated as the first hindrance to overcome in practice.
  9. Seek good friends. The tradition calls spiritual friendship (kalyana-mitta) the whole of the path.

Philosophical

The philosophical schools agree on one thing: self-mastery is a skill built by practice.

  1. Form habits (Aristotle). We become temperate by doing temperate acts. Start small and repeat, since character is shaped by what we do over and over.
  2. Aim for the mean, not for zero. Temperance is not hatred of pleasure. The goal is enjoyment that stays under the guidance of reason.
  3. Examine the belief (Stoics). Ask: do I really need this to be happy? Epictetus says people are disturbed "not by the things which happen, but by the opinions about the things" (Encheiridion 5, Long translation). On pleasure in particular he advises: "If you have received the impression of any pleasure, guard yourself against being carried away by it; but let the thing wait for you" (Encheiridion 34 in Long's numbering).
  4. Choose your company and environment. Aristotle says "a certain training in virtue arises also from the company of the good" (Nicomachean Ethics IX.12), and Epictetus advises resorting "to the society of noble and just men" (Discourses 2.18, Long translation).
  5. Know your weak points. Since the akratic person knows the good but fails, plan ahead for the moments you are weakest, rather than relying on resolve in the moment.
  6. Redirect toward a worthwhile life. The point of self-control is eudaimonia, a flourishing life: work, friendship, learning and love that you can respect.
