Perfectionism

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The effects depend on which side of perfectionism is stronger.

  • Strivings can help. Stoeber and Otto's review found that perfectionistic strivings are associated with positive characteristics, and that "healthy perfectionists" show higher levels of positive characteristics than "unhealthy perfectionists" and non-perfectionists.
  • Worry about mistakes changes that. The same review concludes that strivings are positive only "if perfectionists are not overly concerned about mistakes and negative evaluations by others."
  • Rising demands. Curran and Hill's results mean more young people report that others demand more of them and that they demand more of themselves, which makes the second pattern more common.
  • Commonly reported costs. People describe putting work off for fear it will not be good enough (see procrastination), never calling a piece of work finished, sleeplessness and exhaustion. These are common descriptions, not findings from the papers cited here.

If perfectionism comes with low mood, anxiety, an eating problem or constant exhaustion, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Stoeber and Otto (2006), Personality and Social Psychology Review 10(4), 295-319. doi:10.1207/s15327957pspr1004_2 (external link)
  • Curran and Hill (2019), Psychological Bulletin 145(4), 410-429. doi:10.1037/bul0000138 (external link)

The findings were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full papers. The last bullet is a standard summary and cites no study; no research on procrastination, sleep or exhaustion was read for this article.

Hinduism

The Gita links over-attachment to results with agitation, and the fear of defects with giving up.

  • Loss of equanimity. Gita 2.48 asks for "abandoning attachment to success and failure", and calls the resulting "equanimity" Yog. By implication, attachment to the outcome costs us that evenness; the verse does not describe the cost directly.
  • Abandoning the work. 18.48 says: "One should not abandon duties born of one's nature, even if one sees defects in them." The translator's commentary adds that people recoil from their duty when they see a defect in it. Holding out for flawless work can end in doing nothing.
  • Skill is not lost by letting go. The commentary on 2.50 says that working without attachment to results "does not reduce the quality of our work; instead we become even more skillful than before".

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The verses speak of duty and attachment; the effects listed here are our reading of them.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.48 (external link), 2.50 (external link), 18.48 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The two statements attributed to the commentary are the translator's words and not part of the verses. The reading of equanimity being lost is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not list the effects of perfectionism. Two passages show what a life tied to achievement can lack, and what is said to ease it.

  • Sternness and conceit. Svetaketu comes home from his studies "conceited, considering himself well-read, and stern" (Chandogya 6.1.2). His father's question shows that all his learning has left something out.
  • Sorrow and its end. Isha 7: "When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?" The Upanishad ties the end of sorrow to seeing unity, and says nothing here about performance or standards.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. Neither passage is about perfectionism, and the effects above are drawn by reading them for what they say about grief and attainment. That reading is ours.

Sources

  • Isha (Vâgasaneyi-samhitâ) Upanishad (external link) 7 (Max Müller's translation)
  • Chandogya Upanishad, sixth Prapâthaka (external link) 1.1-4 (Müller)

Quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14.

Christianity

The Bible warns that straining after righteousness or wisdom can harm a person, and shows Paul pressing on without claiming to have arrived.

  • Self-destruction. Ecclesiastes 7:16: "Do not be too righteous, and do not act too wise; why should you destroy yourself?" The verse can be read as a warning against over-demanding standards, though commentators differ on what it means.
  • Not yet there. Philippians 3:12-14: "Not that I have already obtained this or have already reached the goal, but I press on". Paul does not claim to be finished and does not stop either, "forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead".
  • Strength in weakness. 2 Corinthians 12:9-10: "My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness. ... whenever I am weak, then I am strong."

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The verses speak of righteousness, pride and weakness, and applying them to perfectionism is a reading.

Sources

  • Ecclesiastes 7:16-18 (external link), Philippians 3:12-14 (external link), 2 Corinthians 12:9-10 (external link)

The quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway on 2026-11-14. The ellipsis in the 2 Corinthians quotation marks the omitted words ("So I will boast all the more gladly of my weaknesses..."). Scholars differ on the meaning of Ecclesiastes 7:16, and no commentary was read.

Buddhism

The harp image in AN 6.55 names the cost of driving too hard.

  • Restlessness. The Buddha tells Soṇa: "when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness." Strain does not produce calm; it produces the agitation the practice is meant to settle.
  • Wanting to quit. Soṇa, who has driven himself hard and not seen the result he wants, thinks of resigning the training and going back to "a lesser life". Over-striving that does not pay off can end in giving up.
  • Two ways to miss. The harp is "not resonant and playable" if strung too tight or too slack, so the text says that too much effort is as much of a problem as too little.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The sutta concerns effort in meditation, and the match to perfectionism is ours.

Sources

  • AN 6.55, Soṇa Sutta (external link)

Philosophical

The philosophers describe what failing to meet a standard does to a person, and the cost of seeking more exactness than a matter allows.

  • Disgust, discouragement and dissatisfaction. Marcus Aurelius names these three as the reaction to failing to do "everything according to right principles". He tells himself to "return back again" instead.
  • Misplaced exactness. Aristotle (Nicomachean Ethics 1.3) says that "precision is not to be sought for alike in all discussions, any more than in all the products of the crafts", and that in subjects with "variety and fluctuation" we should "indicate the truth roughly and in outline". Demanding more precision than a matter allows is a mistake of method.
  • Ignoring the person. Aristotle's mean is "the mean relative to us" (2.6): the right amount depends on the person, not on a single flawless standard.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The effects are our reading of what each philosopher says about exactness and failure.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.3 (external link) and 2.6 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The phrase "indicate the truth roughly and in outline" is from the same passage as the first Aristotle quotation. The reading of the mean as "not a single flawless standard" is ours; Aristotle does not say it in those words.
