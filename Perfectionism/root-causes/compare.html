Perfectionism

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Researchers point to the culture people grow up in, and to how worth gets tied to performance.

  • Rising demands. Curran and Hill pooled 164 samples and 41,641 college students in the United States, Canada and the United Kingdom, tested between 1989 and 2016. Self-oriented, socially prescribed and other-oriented perfectionism all rose over time, and young people "perceive that others are more demanding of them, are more demanding of others, and are more demanding of themselves." The authors suggest that competitive individualism, emphasised since the 1980s, is part of the reason.
  • Worth tied to results. The "concerns" side of perfectionism is worry about mistakes and about being judged (Stoeber and Otto). When a mistake feels like a verdict on you, avoiding mistakes becomes the goal.
  • Family and school. Early criticism, praise only for results and a fear of disappointing people are commonly given as roots. These are common explanations, not findings from the papers below.

Sources

  • Curran and Hill (2019), Psychological Bulletin 145(4), 410-429. doi:10.1037/bul0000138 (external link)
  • Stoeber and Otto (2006), Personality and Social Psychology Review 10(4), 295-319. doi:10.1207/s15327957pspr1004_2 (external link)

The findings were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full papers. The cohort study measures college students in three countries and cannot show that culture caused the rise; the abstract says the authors "seemingly" see people responding to cultural change. The third bullet is common explanation and is not drawn from a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita finds the roots in attachment to results and in comparing one's own path with another's.

  • Attachment to the fruit. Gita 2.47: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, nor be attached to inaction." When the result is what the self depends on, anything less than a perfect result feels like a threat.
  • Attachment to success and failure. 2.48 names "attachment to success and failure" as what equanimity gives up.
  • Looking at another's path. 3.35: "It is far better to perform one's natural prescribed duty, though tinged with faults, than to perform another's prescribed duty, though perfectly." The pull to measure oneself against someone else's performance is the root in view here.
  • Seeing only the defect. 18.48 says that people recoil from their duty when they see its defects, and states that "all endeavors are veiled by some evil, as fire is by smoke".

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The verses speak of attachment to results and of duty; the link to the modern pattern is ours.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (external link), 2.48 (external link), 3.35 (external link), 18.48 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The statement that people recoil from duty when they see defects is from the translator's commentary on 18.48, not from the verse. Reading 3.35 as being about comparison is an interpretation.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not analyse perfectionism, but two passages touch on its roots: wanting what belongs to someone else, and tying one's worth to learning and achievement.

  • Covetousness. Isha 1 ends: "Do not covet the wealth of any man!" Measuring ourselves against another's accomplishment is a kind of covetousness, though the verse speaks of wealth.
  • Worth through attainment. In the Chandogya Upanishad (6.1), Svetaketu returns from twelve years of study "having then studied all the Vedas,—conceited, considering himself well-read, and stern". His father asks: "have you ever asked for that instruction by which we hear what cannot be heard, by which we perceive what cannot be perceived, by which we know what cannot be known?" The story shows a person whose standing rests on achievement and who still lacks something.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. Neither passage speaks of fear of mistakes; they are read here for the root they share with perfectionism, which is worth that depends on what we have achieved. That connection is ours.

Sources

  • Isha (Vâgasaneyi-samhitâ) Upanishad (external link) 1 (Max Müller's translation)
  • Chandogya Upanishad, sixth Prapâthaka (external link) 1.1-4 (Müller)

Quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. The text does not say that Svetaketu's sternness comes from a need to be flawless, and reading it so is ours.

Christianity

The Christian texts see the root in trying to earn one's standing, and in forgetting what we are.

  • Earning acceptance. Ephesians 2:8-9: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God—not the result of works, so that no one may boast." The verse is answering the idea that standing before God has to be earned by flawless works.
  • Forgetting what we are. Psalm 103:13-14: "As a father has compassion for his children, so the LORD has compassion for those who fear him. For he knows how we were made; he remembers that we are dust." A demand for perfection overlooks that.
  • Pride in one's own record. The words "so that no one may boast" place the harm in a wish to stand on one's own achievement.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The verses speak of grace, works and pride in theological terms, and do not describe anxiety about mistakes. Linking them to perfectionism is a reading.

Sources

  • Ephesians 2:8-9 (external link), Psalm 103:13-14 (external link)

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts have no account of the roots of perfectionism, but the story of Soṇa (AN 6.55) shows how over-striving starts.

  • Effort measured against the result. Soṇa thinks: "I am one of the Buddha's most energetic disciples. Yet my mind is not freed from defilements by not grasping." He has put in the effort and the result is not there, so he decides the effort or the training must be wrong.
  • Comparison. He compares himself with other disciples ("one of the Buddha's most energetic"). The sutta does not treat this as a fault; it is a fact about how he reasons.
  • An all-or-nothing choice. His thought is to give up and return to "a lesser life", not to adjust his practice.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The sutta does not use the language of standards or perfection, and the roots listed above are our reading of Soṇa's thought, not statements in the text.

Sources

  • AN 6.55, Soṇa Sutta (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-14. Other Buddhist accounts of what drives striving, such as craving and conceit, were not read for this article.

Philosophical

The Stoics place the root in what we think we own and what we think we must achieve.

  • Putting our worth in what we do not control. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1: "Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions." If a flawless result or other people's praise is what we need, we have put our peace in something we cannot command.
  • The demand to succeed every time. Marcus Aurelius warns against being "disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied, if thou dost not succeed in doing everything according to right principles". That he needs to say so suggests that failing to meet one's own principles was a real source of distress.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. Epictetus and Marcus are speaking of how to live well, not describing a pattern of harsh self-judgement; the link is ours.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)
  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The Epictetus page names Elizabeth Carter as translator. The inference that Marcus needed to warn himself because failure caused him distress is ours, not stated in the text.
