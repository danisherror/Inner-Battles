Perfectionism

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Perfectionism is holding yourself to standards so high, or so rigid, that anything short of flawless feels like failure. Psychologists do not treat it as one thing:

  • Perfectionistic strivings: setting very high standards and working hard to meet them.
  • Perfectionistic concerns: worry about mistakes and about how others will judge you.
  • Three directions: the Multidimensional Perfectionism Scale (Hewitt and Flett, 1991) measures self-oriented perfectionism (demands on yourself), socially prescribed perfectionism (the sense that others demand perfection of you) and other-oriented perfectionism (demands on other people).

High standards are not the problem in themselves. Stoeber and Otto's review found that perfectionistic strivings are associated with positive characteristics, and concluded that self-oriented strivings are positive "if perfectionists are not overly concerned about mistakes and negative evaluations by others". The trouble starts when the standard becomes a test of your worth.

Perfectionism is not a diagnosis, and it can come with anxiety, depression or eating problems. If it is taking over your life, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Stoeber and Otto (2006), "Positive conceptions of perfectionism: approaches, evidence, challenges," Personality and Social Psychology Review 10(4), 295-319. doi:10.1207/s15327957pspr1004_2 (external link)
  • Curran and Hill (2019), "Perfectionism is increasing over time," Psychological Bulletin 145(4), 410-429. doi:10.1037/bul0000138 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word for perfectionism, but the Bhagavad Gita speaks closely to its two sides: being tied to success and failure, and refusing to act unless the work can be flawless.

On the first, Krishna says in 2.48: "Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog."

On the second, 18.48 says: "One should not abandon duties born of one's nature, even if one sees defects in them, O son of Kunti. Indeed, all endeavors are veiled by some evil, as fire is by smoke."

And 18.47 puts the standard in a different place: "It is better to do one's own dharma, even though imperfectly, than to do another's dharma, even though perfectly."

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The Gita is speaking of attachment to the results of action and of duty, and it does not describe the modern pattern of self-judgement over mistakes. The Gita also speaks of attaining "perfection" through one's duties (18.45), so the word there refers to spiritual fulfilment and not to flawless performance; that point is a reading of the verse, not checked against the Sanskrit.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not discuss perfectionism, and no verse here is about it directly. What they do offer is a picture of what is whole and flawless, and it is not the person's performance.

The Isha Upanishad describes the Self in verse 8: "He (the Self) encircled all, bright, incorporeal, scatheless, without muscles, pure, untouched by evil; a seer, wise, omnipresent, self-existent". Verse 2 says of work: "Though a man may wish to live a hundred years, performing works, it will be thus with him; but not in any other way: work will thus not cling to a man."

Read together, the two verses suggest that what is untouched and complete is the Self, while work is something done that need not cling to us. That is a reading and not a statement in the text. Müller himself says in his introduction that this Upanishad is "one of the most difficult to understand properly" and that commentators have disputed verse 2.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The Upanishad speaks of the Self and of action without clinging, not of standards or of fear of mistakes.

  • Isha (Vâgasaneyi-samhitâ) Upanishad (external link) (Max Müller's translation), verses 2 and 8 and Müller's introductory note

Christianity

Christian texts do not name perfectionism, but the word "perfect" is in the New Testament, and some people take it as a demand for flawlessness. Jesus says in Matthew 5:48: "Be perfect, therefore, as your heavenly Father is perfect."

The same texts also describe weakness and unfinished progress as the setting of faith. Paul writes in Philippians 3:12: "Not that I have already obtained this or have already reached the goal, but I press on to lay hold of that for which Christ has laid hold of me." And in 2 Corinthians 12:9 he reports the answer he was given: "My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness."

Many readers take "perfect" in Matthew 5:48 to mean complete or mature and not faultless. That reading was not checked here against a Greek lexicon, so treat it as one common view.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The verses speak of holiness, grace and weakness, and not of anxiety about mistakes.

Buddhism

Buddhist texts have no word for perfectionism, but one famous sutta describes over-striving closely. In AN 6.55, Venerable Soṇa thinks in private retreat: "I am one of the Buddha's most energetic disciples. Yet my mind is not freed from defilements by not grasping." He considers giving up the training and going back to a "lesser life".

The Buddha asks him about the harp he played as a layman. Strings tuned too tight, or too slack, did not make it "resonant and playable". Then he says: "In the same way, Soṇa, when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness."

Elsewhere the Buddha speaks of "two extremes" and a "middle way of practice" (SN 56.11); there the extremes are sensual indulgence and self-mortification.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The sutta is about effort in meditation, and the texts do not discuss standards, mistakes or the fear of judgement as a pattern of its own.

Philosophical

The ancient philosophers did not name perfectionism, but they argued about how exact we should expect to be.

Aristotle opens the Nicomachean Ethics with a point about precision: "Our discussion will be adequate if it has as much clearness as the subject-matter admits of, for precision is not to be sought for alike in all discussions". Later he describes virtue as a mean, "the mean relative to us", between excess and defect, which is not the same for everyone.

Marcus Aurelius speaks to the person who fails to live up to his own principles: "Be not disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied, if thou dost not succeed in doing everything according to right principles; but when thou hast failed, return back again, and be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature."

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. Aristotle is writing about method and about virtue, and Marcus about moral practice; neither describes the modern pattern of harsh self-judgement.
