A story

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how perfectionism works and how people loosen its grip.

Meera's story

Meera, a junior designer, was asked to show a first draft of a new app screen to her team on Friday. By Wednesday she had redone the layout four times. On Thursday night she was still adjusting the spacing of one button, and she had told no one that the draft was not ready.

On Friday she sent in a screen she called "not finished", and spent the meeting waiting for someone to say what was wrong with it. A colleague pointed to a real problem in the flow, which none of her polishing had touched. She went home feeling that the whole week had been wasted.

What helped:

  1. She asked which side was driving. The wish to do well was fine. The dread of being seen to get it wrong was what had her redoing the layout.
  2. She set a finish line. For the next piece she wrote down what "done" meant (the flow works, the main screens exist) and a time limit of two days.
  3. She shared it early. She showed a rough version on day one. The first comments were about the flow, which was what mattered.
  4. She talked to herself as she would to a friend. "The draft has two problems. That is what a draft is for."
  5. She noticed the cost. After a month of poor sleep and constant dread before presentations, she spoke to her doctor, who helped her find a counsellor.

The flow was fixed in a day. The dread eased over the following weeks, though it did not vanish.

What it shows: Polishing felt like care, but it was a way of avoiding the one test that would help. A clear finish line, early sharing and a kinder voice moved Meera from guarding the work to improving it.
