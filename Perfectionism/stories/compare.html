Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how perfectionism works and how people loosen its grip.

Meera's story

Meera, a junior designer, was asked to show a first draft of a new app screen to her team on Friday. By Wednesday she had redone the layout four times. On Thursday night she was still adjusting the spacing of one button, and she had told no one that the draft was not ready.

On Friday she sent in a screen she called "not finished", and spent the meeting waiting for someone to say what was wrong with it. A colleague pointed to a real problem in the flow, which none of her polishing had touched. She went home feeling that the whole week had been wasted.

What helped:

  1. She asked which side was driving. The wish to do well was fine. The dread of being seen to get it wrong was what had her redoing the layout.
  2. She set a finish line. For the next piece she wrote down what "done" meant (the flow works, the main screens exist) and a time limit of two days.
  3. She shared it early. She showed a rough version on day one. The first comments were about the flow, which was what mattered.
  4. She talked to herself as she would to a friend. "The draft has two problems. That is what a draft is for."
  5. She noticed the cost. After a month of poor sleep and constant dread before presentations, she spoke to her doctor, who helped her find a counsellor.

The flow was fixed in a day. The dread eased over the following weeks, though it did not vanish.

What it shows: Polishing felt like care, but it was a way of avoiding the one test that would help. A clear finish line, early sharing and a kinder voice moved Meera from guarding the work to improving it.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If perfectionism is causing constant anxiety, low mood or exhaustion, please see a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Krishna's lesson on defects and duty

The Gita is a conversation between Krishna and Arjuna, who is hesitating over his duty on the eve of a battle.

Near the end of the dialogue, Krishna speaks of the work each person is born to: "It is better to do one's own dharma, even though imperfectly, than to do another's dharma, even though perfectly" (18.47).

He then answers the objection that the work has faults: "One should not abandon duties born of one's nature, even if one sees defects in them, O son of Kunti. Indeed, all endeavors are veiled by some evil, as fire is by smoke" (18.48).

The verses are not addressed to perfectionism, but the image fits it: fire gives light and heat, and smoke is part of how it burns.

What it shows: A flaw in the work is not a reason to put the work down. The image of fire and smoke says that every action comes with some imperfection, and the teaching is to act without attachment to a flawless result.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 18.47 (external link), 18.48 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The setting (a conversation with Arjuna hesitating before a battle) is a standard summary and was not checked against the opening chapters. Taking the fire-and-smoke verse as a lesson for perfectionism is our reading and not stated in the text.

Upanishads

Svetaketu and the instruction not yet heard

The Chandogya Upanishad tells of Svetaketu, whose father says to him: "go to school; for there is none belonging to our race, darling, who, not having studied (the Veda), is, as it were, a Brâhmana by birth only" (6.1.1).

He begins at twelve and returns at twenty-four, "having then studied all the Vedas,—conceited, considering himself well-read, and stern" (6.1.2).

His father asks: "have you ever asked for that instruction by which we hear what cannot be heard, by which we perceive what cannot be perceived, by which we know what cannot be known?" (6.1.3). Svetaketu does not know what it is, and the father explains with an image: "as by one clod of clay all that is made of clay is known" (6.1.4).

Svetaketu answers: "Surely those venerable men (my teachers) did not know that. For if they had known it, why should they not have told it me? Do you, Sir, therefore tell me that" (6.1.7). He asks to be taught.

What it shows: Svetaketu has done everything asked of him and is full of knowledge, yet his father shows him that something is still missing. The story does not mention mistakes or perfectionism; it shows a person whose standing rests on achievement, and who learns by asking.

Sources

  • Chandogya Upanishad, sixth Prapâthaka (external link) 1.1-7 (Max Müller's translation)

The quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource (page images of volume 1) on 2026-11-14. Only 6.1 was read; the father's teaching in the rest of chapter 6 was not. Connecting the story to perfectionism is a reading and the text does not make it.

Christianity

Paul and the thorn

In 2 Corinthians 12, Paul writes of a "thorn" he was given: "a thorn was given me in the flesh, a messenger of Satan to torment me, to keep me from being too elated." He says: "Three times I appealed to the Lord about this, that it would leave me" (12:7-8).

The answer he reports: "My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness" (12:9). And he concludes: "Therefore I am content with weaknesses, insults, hardships, persecutions, and calamities for the sake of Christ, for whenever I am weak, then I am strong" (12:10).

The text does not say what the thorn was, and readers have suggested many things. The story is not about perfectionism, but it shows a person asking for a flaw to be removed and being told that it stays, and that his standing does not depend on being rid of it.

What it shows: A flaw can remain and the person can still be accepted and useful. The story does not say that effort is pointless; it says that a person's strength is not measured by the absence of weakness.

Sources

  • 2 Corinthians 12:7-10 (external link)

Buddhism

Soṇa and the harp

Venerable Soṇa has been practising hard. In private retreat he thinks: "I am one of the Buddha's most energetic disciples. Yet my mind is not freed from defilements by not grasping. But my family has wealth. I could enjoy that wealth and make merit. Why don't I resign the training and return to a lesser life, so I can enjoy my wealth and make merit?"

The Buddha knows his thought and appears in front of him. He asks: "When you were still a layman, weren't you a good player of the arched harp?" Soṇa says yes. "When your harp's strings were tuned too tight, was it resonant and playable?" "No, sir." The Buddha asks the same about strings tuned too slack. Then: "But when your harp's strings were tuned neither too tight nor too slack, but fixed at an even tension, was it resonant and playable?"

The Buddha says: "In the same way, Soṇa, when energy is too forceful it leads to restlessness. When energy is too slack it leads to laziness. So, Soṇa, you should focus on energy and serenity, find a balance of the faculties, and learn the character of this situation."

Soṇa does so, and "soon realized the supreme culmination of the spiritual path in this very life."

What it shows: Soṇa was not lazy; he was straining, and he took the lack of a result to mean that he should quit. The Buddha does not tell him to try less or to try harder, but to tune the effort. The sutta is about meditation, and its use for perfectionism is a reading.

Sources

  • AN 6.55, Soṇa Sutta (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-14. The retelling leaves out some lines (for example, Soṇa's answers about the slack strings) and does not describe other versions of Soṇa's story elsewhere in the canon, which were not read.

Philosophical

Marcus Aurelius and the sponge

In the Meditations, the emperor Marcus Aurelius writes notes to himself. In one of them he considers what to do on days when he has not lived up to his own principles.

He does not tell himself to try harder. He writes: "Be not disgusted, nor discouraged, nor dissatisfied, if thou dost not succeed in doing everything according to right principles; but when thou hast failed, return back again, and be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature, and love this to which thou returnest."

Then he adds an image: "do not return to philosophy as if she were a master, but act like those who have sore eyes and apply a bit of sponge and egg, or as another applies a plaster, or drenching with water."

What it shows: Marcus treats philosophy as a remedy to be used, and failure as a reason to return to it without drama. The passage does not use the word perfectionism; it speaks to the habit of being disgusted with oneself, and offers a lighter way back.

Sources

  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)
