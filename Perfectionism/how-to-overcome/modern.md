The aim is to keep high standards and loosen the grip of the fear behind them.

1. **Tell the two sides apart.** Stoeber and Otto separate strivings (wanting to do well) from concerns (worry about mistakes and judgement). Ask which one is driving you today. Keep the first and work on the second.
2. **Choose where "good enough" will do.** Aristotle's point that precision should match the subject is a useful rule: decide which tasks deserve your best and which only need to be finished. This is common advice, not a research finding.
3. **Set the finish line before you start.** Decide what "done" looks like and a time limit, then stop when you reach either.
4. **Treat a mistake as information.** Ask what you would learn from it, as you would for a friend. Self-talk in the third person helped in other studies on rumination (see [Overthinking](../../Overthinking/how-to-overcome/modern.html)); that evidence is not about perfectionism.
5. **Try a small experiment.** Hand in something at 80%, or leave a small flaw, and see what actually happens. This is a common step in cognitive behavioural therapy for perfectionism; no trial of it was read for this article.
6. **Expect a rising demand and name it.** Curran and Hill found that young people report others as more demanding than before. Part of the pressure comes from outside, and it is worth asking whose standard you are meeting.
7. **Get help for persistent cases.** If perfectionism comes with constant anxiety, low mood, an eating problem or exhaustion, a doctor or therapist can help.

- Curran and Hill (2019), *Psychological Bulletin* 145(4), 410-429. [doi:10.1037/bul0000138](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000138)
