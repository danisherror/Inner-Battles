Inner battle

Perfectionism

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is to keep high standards and loosen the grip of the fear behind them.

  1. Tell the two sides apart. Stoeber and Otto separate strivings (wanting to do well) from concerns (worry about mistakes and judgement). Ask which one is driving you today. Keep the first and work on the second.
  2. Choose where "good enough" will do. Aristotle's point that precision should match the subject is a useful rule: decide which tasks deserve your best and which only need to be finished. This is common advice, not a research finding.
  3. Set the finish line before you start. Decide what "done" looks like and a time limit, then stop when you reach either.
  4. Treat a mistake as information. Ask what you would learn from it, as you would for a friend. Self-talk in the third person helped in other studies on rumination (see Overthinking); that evidence is not about perfectionism.
  5. Try a small experiment. Hand in something at 80%, or leave a small flaw, and see what actually happens. This is a common step in cognitive behavioural therapy for perfectionism; no trial of it was read for this article.
  6. Expect a rising demand and name it. Curran and Hill found that young people report others as more demanding than before. Part of the pressure comes from outside, and it is worth asking whose standard you are meeting.
  7. Get help for persistent cases. If perfectionism comes with constant anxiety, low mood, an eating problem or exhaustion, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Stoeber and Otto (2006), Personality and Social Psychology Review 10(4), 295-319. doi:10.1207/s15327957pspr1004_2 (external link)
  • Curran and Hill (2019), Psychological Bulletin 145(4), 410-429. doi:10.1037/bul0000138 (external link)

Only steps 1 and 6 rest on the two abstracts read (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14). Steps 2, 3, 4 and 5 are common advice and not drawn from a study read here. The paper on cognitive behavioural therapy for perfectionism was not opened, and no claim is made about how well these steps work.

Hinduism

The Gita's answer is to put the effort into the work and to let go of the result.

  1. Do the work, release the fruit. 2.47: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions." Standards stay; the demand on the result goes.
  2. Practise equanimity. 2.48: "Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog."
  3. Work skilfully. 2.50: "strive for Yog, which is the art of working skillfully (in proper consciousness)." The commentary says that working without attachment does not lower the quality of the work.
  4. Accept that no work is free of defect. 18.48: "all endeavors are veiled by some evil, as fire is by smoke". The point is to act anyway.
  5. Do your own work, even imperfectly. 18.47: "It is better to do one's own dharma, even though imperfectly, than to do another's dharma, even though perfectly."

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The Gita's guidance is about acting without attachment to results, and applying it to perfectionism is our reading.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.47 (external link), 2.48 (external link), 2.50 (external link), 18.47 (external link), 18.48 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The statement about the quality of work comes from the translator's commentary on 2.50 and is not part of the verse. If perfectionism is causing distress, a doctor or therapist can also help.

Upanishads

The Isha Upanishad offers a short way of acting without clinging.

  1. Surrender the result. Verse 1: "All this, whatsoever moves on earth, is to be hidden in the Lord (the Self). When thou hast surrendered all this, then thou mayest enjoy."
  2. Keep acting. Verse 2: "Though a man may wish to live a hundred years, performing works, it will be thus with him; but not in any other way: work will thus not cling to a man."
  3. See the one Self in everything. Verses 6-7: "he never turns away from it", and "what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"

Müller's note says the verse allows continued work "provided they are performed without any selfish motives, without any desire of reward". He also reports that Śaṅkara read verse 2 differently, so the meaning is disputed.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The Upanishad does not discuss standards or fear of mistakes, and using it for perfectionism is a reading.

Sources

  • Isha (Vâgasaneyi-samhitâ) Upanishad (external link) 1, 2, 6, 7 and Müller's introductory note (Max Müller's translation)

The quotations and the note were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. Müller's note is one scholar's view and other commentators read these verses differently.

Christianity

The Christian texts offer grace, honesty about weakness and balance.

  1. Receive grace. Ephesians 2:8-9: "For by grace you have been saved through faith, and this is not your own doing; it is the gift of God—not the result of works, so that no one may boast."
  2. Remember compassion. Psalm 103:13-14: "he knows how we were made; he remembers that we are dust."
  3. Admit weakness. 2 Corinthians 12:9: "My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness."
  4. Hold both. Ecclesiastes 7:16-18: "Do not be too righteous, and do not act too wise; why should you destroy yourself? ... It is good that you should take hold of the one without letting go of the other". The passage warns against both extremes.
  5. Press on without being finished. Philippians 3:13-14: "forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal".

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. Christian counsel for a person weighed down by guilt or anxiety also includes pastoral and medical help, which these verses do not discuss.

Buddhism

The advice of AN 6.55 is to tune the effort, as a harp is tuned.

  1. Neither too tight nor too slack. The Buddha asks Soṇa about strings "tuned neither too tight nor too slack, but fixed at an even tension" and whether the harp then sounded well. It did.
  2. Balance energy and serenity. "So, Soṇa, you should focus on energy and serenity, find a balance of the faculties, and learn the character of this situation."
  3. Look at the result. The sutta reports that Soṇa did this, and then, "living alone, withdrawn, diligent, keen, and resolute, soon realized the supreme culmination of the spiritual path". The effort stayed; the strain went.
  4. Avoid both extremes. SN 56.11 describes a "middle way of practice" that avoids "indulgence in sensual pleasures" and "indulgence in self-mortification". The extremes there are not about perfectionism, but the pattern of avoiding both is the same.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. The suttas give guidance for practice, and applying the harp to everyday standards is a reading.

Philosophical

The philosophers advise fitting the standard to the matter and returning after failing.

  1. Match the precision to the subject. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 1.3: "Our discussion will be adequate if it has as much clearness as the subject-matter admits of".
  2. Find the mean for you. Virtue is "the mean relative to us", between excess and defect (2.6).
  3. Return without disgust. Marcus Aurelius: "when thou hast failed, return back again, and be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature, and love this to which thou returnest".
  4. Keep it light. He adds: "do not return to philosophy as if she were a master, but act like those who have sore eyes and apply a bit of sponge and egg". Treat the practice as a remedy, not a taskmaster.
  5. Sort what you control. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1: opinion, pursuit, desire and aversion are in our control; "reputation" is not.

This is related to perfectionism and not identical to it. These are rules for living and reasoning, and applying them to perfectionism is a reading.
