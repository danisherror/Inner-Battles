Deadly sin

Greed

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Greed seldom begins with a love of things. It usually begins with a feeling that something is missing.

  • Scarcity and insecurity. People who grew up short of money or safety often over-accumulate later. Feeling short of resources narrows attention to what you lack.
  • Comparison. Status is relative. You feel poor next to someone richer, so "enough" keeps rising.
  • Hedonic adaptation. New purchases and raises stop feeling special quickly, so the mind asks for the next one.
  • Culture and advertising. Messages tie identity and worth to owning and displaying things.
  • Using wealth to fix feelings. Buying or winning can soothe fear, loneliness or low self-worth for a moment, which trains the habit.

Hinduism

Hindu thought traces greed to desire fed by ignorance.

  • Desire grows by feeding. Gita 3.37-39 says desire born of rajas is insatiable, "like fire", and covers knowledge. Manusmriti 2.94 is often quoted for the same idea: desire is never quenched by indulging it, as fire is not put out by ghee.
  • Attachment to objects. Gita 2.62-63 describes the chain: dwelling on objects produces attachment, then desire, anger, delusion and loss of reason.
  • The sense of "mine". Mamata (possessiveness) rides on ahamkara. Gita 16.13-14 gives the thought, "this wealth is mine, and more will be mine."
  • Rajas. Gita 14.17 says greed (lobha) arises from the quality of rajas, along with restless activity.

Upanishads

The Upanishads see greed as a search for fulfilment in the wrong place.

  • Pleasant versus good. Katha 1.2.1-2 distinguishes preyas (the pleasant) from shreyas (the good). The less wise choose the pleasant for the sake of getting and keeping.
  • Fear from a second. Brihadaranyaka 1.4.2-3 says the Self felt fear, and then no delight, because it was alone. It desired a second. The sense of lack, and so the urge to acquire, comes from separateness.
  • Not knowing what is enough. Katha 1.1.27 says no one is satisfied by wealth, and 1.1.26 has Nachiketa refuse offered riches and long life because they wear out the senses.
  • Looking outward. Katha 2.1.1 says the self-existent pierced the openings of the senses outward, so people look at the outside and not the inner Self.

Sources

Christianity

Christian writers trace greed to fear, misplaced trust and disordered love.

  • Anxiety about security. In Matthew 6:25-34 Jesus links worry about food and clothes to a lack of trust, and tells people to seek first the kingdom of God.
  • Trusting in riches. In Luke 12:16-21 the rich man's plan is to store everything and tell himself, "Relax, eat, drink, be merry." God calls him a fool. The root is a false sense of security.
  • Loving the wrong thing. 1 Timothy 6:9-10 says those who want to be rich fall into temptation, and that some have wandered from the faith through "love of money." Augustine, in City of God XIV.13, speaks of the soul loving lower goods above higher ones.
  • The pull of appetite. The tradition describes covetousness as never having enough, the desire growing with what is fed; Ecclesiastes 5:10 says the lover of money will not be satisfied with money.

Buddhism

Buddhism's answer is craving rooted in ignorance.

  • Craving. The first sermon (SN 56.11) says suffering arises from craving: for sense pleasures, for becoming and for non-becoming. Greed is craving aimed at possessions.
  • Ignorance of impermanence and not-self. We grasp because we believe things can make a lasting "me" secure. Seeing impermanence loosens the grip, as in the teaching on the five aggregates (SN 22.59).
  • Dependent origination. In the standard chain, contact leads to feeling, feeling to craving, craving to clinging (SN 12.2). Greed grows at the step from pleasant feeling to craving.
  • Unwise attention. Clinging builds on repeatedly dwelling on what is attractive. The suttas call attending to the attractive sign a feeder of sensual desire (SN 46.51).

Philosophical

Philosophers give several causes.

  • Unlimited aim. Aristotle (Politics 1.9) says that when wealth becomes an end and not a means to a good life, there is no limit to it, and desire spreads without end.
  • Mistaking wants for needs. Epicurus divides desires into natural and necessary, natural but unnecessary, and empty. Greed feeds on the empty kind (Letter to Menoeceus, 127-130).
  • Fear of lacking. Seneca (Letters 119) argues that desire for more is a restlessness of the mind, and that "nature demands nothing except mere food" (Letters 119.13, Gummere translation).
  • Social structure. Plato (Republic 8, 550c-555a) describes the oligarchic person who has made money-making the single aim, with the other desires pushed down.
