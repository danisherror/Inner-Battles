Deadly sin

Greed

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Greed tends to cost the things money can't buy back.

  • Well-being. People who put wealth and image first report more anxiety and less satisfaction on average.
  • Relationships. Treating people as means to an end, or fighting over money, wears trust down.
  • Ethics. Some studies link a strong focus on self-interest and wealth with more rule-breaking, although this research is debated and not every result has replicated.
  • Time and health. Chasing more means long hours, stress and less rest.
  • The treadmill. Because satisfaction fades, the gains rarely feel like enough. Society pays too when greed drives fraud, exploitation or harm to the environment.

Sources

  • Kasser & Ryan (1993), JPSP 65(2), 410-422. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.65.2.410 (external link)
  • Piff et al. (2012), "Higher social class predicts increased unethical behavior," PNAS 109(11), 4086-4091. doi:10.1073/pnas.1118373109 (external link). Later replications have been mixed.

Hinduism

The Gita describes where greed leads.

  • Restlessness and fear. Gita 16.13-16 shows the greedy mind caught in "many thoughts", ending in anxiety and attachment to enjoyment, and falling "into a foul hell."
  • Self-destruction. Gita 16.21 names desire, anger and greed as three gates to the ruin of the self.
  • Unending hunger. Gita 3.39 calls desire an insatiable fire.
  • Loss of the sharing that holds society together. Gita 3.12 says one who enjoys what the gods have given, without giving in return, is a thief. The Mahabharata shows the social side in Duryodhana, whose refusal to share land contributes to a war that destroys his family.
  • No peace. Gita 2.70-71 compares a mind that is full without being agitated to the ocean, and says peace does not come to the one who craves.

  • Bhagavad Gita 3.12, 3.39 (external link)
  • Bhagavad Gita 2.70-71 (external link)

Upanishads

In the Upanishads the cost of greed is staying on the wrong path.

  • Death in the end. Katha 1.1.26-27 has Nachiketa tell Death that wealth lasts only till tomorrow and wears away the vigour of the senses.
  • Choosing the pleasant. Katha 1.2.2 says that the one who picks the pleasant (preyas) loses the goal.
  • Wealth cannot save. Brihadaranyaka 2.4.3 (repeated at 4.5.3) reports the point as Yajnavalkya states it: "there is no hope of immortality by wealth" (Müller translation).
  • Small things, small joy. Chandogya 7.23-24 says there is no happiness in the small; the one who sees only the finite lives in the finite.

Christianity

The Bible and tradition list greed's costs.

  • Ruin and grief. 1 Timothy 6:9-10 says those who want to be rich fall into "ruin and destruction" and, in 6:10, some "pierced themselves with many pains."
  • Loss of the soul. "What will it profit them to gain the whole world and forfeit their life?" (Mark 8:36). The rich fool is told, "This very night your life is being demanded of you" (Luke 12:20).
  • Hardness to the poor. James 5:1-6 condemns the rich who withhold wages. 1 John 3:17 asks how God's love abides in one who sees a brother in need and closes their heart.
  • Division in the community. Acts 5:1-11 tells of Ananias and Sapphira, who lied about what they gave.
  • Servitude. Matthew 6:24 says you cannot serve two masters. Wealth becomes a master.

Buddhism

Greed keeps the cycle of suffering turning.

  • Never satisfied. Dhammapada 186-187: "There is no satisfying sensual desires even with the rain of gold coins" (Buddharakkhita translation).
  • Wealth that destroys. Dhammapada 355 says wealth destroys the foolish who do not seek the far shore.
  • Harm to self and others. Greed is one of three unwholesome roots, and actions driven by it produce suffering for oneself and others in the traditional teaching on karma.
  • Fear of loss. Clinging brings worry about losing what is held; the Buddha's teaching on impermanence says everything held will change (SN 22.59).
  • Stinginess. Dhammapada 177 says misers do not go to the heavenly worlds, and fools do not praise giving.

Philosophical

Philosophers say greed damages character and community.

  • Corrupted character. Aristotle (NE 4.1) says illiberality is incurable and more natural than prodigality, so the habit of taking is hard to unlearn. He adds that those who take too much are often willing to take from unworthy sources.
  • Endless lack. Seneca (Letters 2.6, 119) holds that the greedy are poor in the way that matters: they always want.
  • An empty life. Epicurus says a life spent on empty desires brings anxiety and not pleasure (Principal Doctrines 29-30).
  • Loss of justice. Plato (Republic 8) argues that a city ruled by love of wealth splits into the rich and the poor, with unrest following.
  • Using people. Kant says avarice conflicts with the duty of benevolence, treating others' needs as no concern of ours.
