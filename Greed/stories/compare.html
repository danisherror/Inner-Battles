Greed

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how greed works and how people step out of it.

Anil's story

Anil was 38 and ran a small import business that did well. Each year he told himself he'd slow down once he reached a number. When he reached it, the number moved. He bought a bigger house, then a second property he hardly visited, and checked his business rivals' growth every morning.

He missed his daughter's school recital for a supplier call. He pushed a partner out of a deal on terms that were legal but not fair, and the partner stopped speaking to him. Money was up and he felt worse.

The turning point was small. His father, who had little, was ill, and Anil visited and found him calm. "Why aren't you afraid?" he asked. His father said, "I have enough."

What helped:

  1. He wrote down "enough": income, savings and what he would need for the family.
  2. He fixed the partner deal, paying what he should have, and said sorry.
  3. He gave on a schedule, a fixed share to a school fund, so it didn't depend on mood.
  4. He blocked time off, with no supplier calls after seven.
  5. He stopped watching rivals, and checked his own numbers weekly instead.

The business grew more slowly. He slept better and was at the next recital.

What it shows: Greed has no finish line unless you draw one. "Enough" is a decision, not a number the world gives you.

Hinduism

Duryodhana and the five villages

In the Mahabharata, the Pandavas lose their kingdom through a rigged dice game and spend years in exile. When they return, the kingdom is not given back.

Before war, Krishna goes to the Kaurava court as peace envoy (Udyoga Parva). He lowers the Pandavas' demand again and again, down to just five villages, enough for each brother to live in. Elders, including Bhishma, Drona and Vidura, urge Duryodhana to accept.

Duryodhana refuses. He says he will not give up even as much land as the point of a needle can pierce. The war follows, and by its end Duryodhana, his brothers and nearly his whole army are dead.

What it shows: Greed turns a small and fair share into a loss. Duryodhana could have lived in peace with most of what he had, but "mine" had no limit, and he lost it all.

Sources

  • Mahabharata, Udyoga Parva (Book 5) (external link), Krishna's embassy

Retold from memory; the "needle-point" line is the traditional summary of his refusal, and versions of the epic differ in detail.

Upanishads

Nachiketa refuses wealth

The Katha Upanishad (Part 1) tells of a boy, Nachiketa, whose father Vajashrava performs a sacrifice in which he gives away only old, useless cows. Nachiketa sees the gift is empty and asks, "To whom will you give me?" His angry father says, "To Death."

Nachiketa goes to Death's house and waits three days without food. Death, ashamed, grants him three wishes. For the third, the boy asks what happens after death.

Death tries to talk him out of it. He offers sons and grandsons who will live a hundred years, cattle, elephants, gold, a vast land, long life, chariots and music. "Ask for anything else," he says. Nachiketa answers that all this lasts only until tomorrow, and wears out the senses. Wealth cannot satisfy a person, and he will live only as long as Death allows. He repeats his question.

Death, seeing his steadiness, praises him for choosing the good (shreyas) over the pleasant, and teaches him.

What it shows: The choice is between what feels full now and what is lasting. Nachiketa's freedom from greed is what lets him learn.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link), Part 1 (1.1.1-1.2.2)

Retold and shortened. Verse numbers and key quotations were checked against Max Müller's translation (Sacred Books of the East 1 and 15) on sacred-texts.com on 2026-11-14; other translations differ in wording and sometimes in numbering.

Christianity

Zacchaeus

Luke 19:1-10 tells of Zacchaeus, a chief tax collector in Jericho, and rich. Tax collectors were widely seen as collaborators who took more than they should, and Zacchaeus was disliked.

Hearing Jesus would pass by, he wanted to see him. Being short, and blocked by the crowd, he ran ahead and climbed a sycamore tree. Jesus stopped under it, looked up and said, "Zacchaeus, hurry and come down; for I must stay at your house today."

People grumbled that Jesus was the guest of a sinner. Zacchaeus stood and said, "Look, half of my possessions, Lord, I will give to the poor; and if I have defrauded anyone of anything, I will pay back four times as much."

Jesus replied, "Today salvation has come to this house."

What it shows: The cure is not only feeling sorry. Zacchaeus' change shows up in what he does with his money. The story is a contrast with the rich ruler of Luke 18:18-25, who could not let go.

Buddhism

The golden goose

The Suvannahamsa Jataka (Jataka 136) is a tale of one of the Buddha's past lives. A Brahmin dies and is reborn as a golden goose that remembers its past life. Seeing his wife and three daughters in poverty, the goose visits them and gives them one golden feather, then returns from time to time to give another. The family's life improves.

The wife begins to think the goose could be slow and that they should get more. She tells her daughters she will take all the feathers at once. When the goose next arrives, she grabs it and plucks every feather. But the feathers, taken by force, turn into ordinary feathers. When new ones grow, they are white, not gold. The goose flies away and does not return.

The family's wealth ends, and they have lost even their steady supply.

What it shows: Greed destroys the very source it wants more of. The goose, the Buddha explains in the frame story, was himself, and the wife was a past life of the woman who is greedy in the present.

Sources

  • Jataka 136, Suvannahamsa Jataka, in Cowell's Jataka translation, volume 2 (external link) (overview; the story appears in the list of Jatakas)

Retold from memory; details of the frame story differ between editions, and the Jatakas are folk narrative, not historical record.

Philosophical

King Midas

The Greek myth, told in Ovid's Metamorphoses (Book 11), is about King Midas of Phrygia. He did a kindness to Silenus, a companion of the god Dionysus, and in return Dionysus offered him a wish. Midas asked that everything he touched turn to gold.

At first he was delighted, and turned twigs, stones and apples to gold. Then he sat to eat. The bread became gold in his hand, the wine became liquid gold in his mouth. Some versions add that his daughter turned to gold when he embraced her.

Starving and horrified, Midas begged to be released. Dionysus told him to wash in the river Pactolus. The gold passed into the water, and the river's sands have been golden since.

Aristotle uses the story in the Politics (1.9, 1257b) to make a point about money: wealth is not what a person needs to live, and a man with plenty of it may die of hunger.

What it shows: Wanting without a limit can destroy the thing you wanted wealth for, which is living well.

Sources

  • Ovid, Metamorphoses 11.85-145 (Midas and the golden touch); Aristotle, Politics (external link) 1.9
  • Ovid's text is available in many translations; not linked directly.

Retold from memory. The daughter detail is from later retellings and is not in Ovid. Passage numbers were not re-checked.
