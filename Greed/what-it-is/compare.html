Deadly sin

Greed

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Greed is wanting more than you need, and more than is good for you or fair to others, while never feeling you have enough. It is not the same as ambition or enjoying nice things. The test is whether wanting has turned into a compulsion that pushes out honesty, generosity and contentment.

Signs include:

  • Satisfaction that fades as soon as a goal is reached ("hedonic adaptation"), so the target keeps moving.
  • Hoarding, or being unable to give when you could easily afford to.
  • Cutting corners, or taking from others, to get more.
  • Measuring your worth by what you own.

Researchers find that people who rank wealth and image above relationships and growth report lower well-being, on average.

Sources

  • Brickman, Coates & Janoff-Bulman (1978), "Lottery winners and accident victims: is happiness relative?", Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 36(8), 917-927. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.36.8.917 (external link)
  • Kasser & Ryan (1993), "A dark side of the American dream," JPSP 65(2), 410-422. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.65.2.410 (external link)

Hinduism

The Sanskrit word is lobha, greed, one of the six inner enemies (shadripu). Its opposite is aparigraha, non-possessiveness, one of the yamas (restraints) in Patanjali's Yoga Sutras (2.30, 2.39).

The Gita names it with desire and anger as a gate to self-destruction (16.21). In 16.12-13 it describes people who, bound by hundreds of hopes, try to amass wealth unjustly to satisfy desires, and who think "this much I have today, and I will get more." In 3.12, the one who enjoys the gifts of life without giving anything back is called a thief.

Hinduism does not condemn wealth. Artha, prosperity honestly earned, is one of the four aims of life. The fault is in clutching it without limit or without sharing.

Upanishads

The Upanishads open with a warning against grasping. Isha 1 says that all this is to be hidden in the Lord: "When thou hast surrendered all this, then thou mayest enjoy. Do not covet the wealth of any man!" (Müller translation). Other translators render the first half as "enjoy through renunciation" or "enjoy what is given."

The texts also argue that wealth cannot satisfy. In Katha 1.1.27, Nachiketa tells Death that no one can be made content by wealth. In Brihadaranyaka 2.4.1-3, Yajnavalkya is about to leave his household and offers his wealth to his wife Maitreyi, who asks whether it would make her immortal. He says no: she would live as the rich do, with no hope of immortality through wealth. She asks for the teaching instead.

Chandogya 7.23 adds a principle: the infinite (bhuma) is happiness; there is no happiness in the small. Greed, in this reading, is a search for the infinite in finite things.

Christianity

Christian tradition calls it avarice or covetousness (Latin avaritia): an excessive desire for money and possessions. The tenth commandment forbids coveting a neighbour's goods (Exodus 20:17), and the Catechism treats avarice as a sin against it (CCC 2534-2536).

The New Testament is direct:

  • "The love of money is a root of all kinds of evil" (1 Timothy 6:10). The target is the love of money, not money itself.
  • "You cannot serve God and wealth" (Matthew 6:24).
  • "Be on your guard against all kinds of greed; for one's life does not consist in the abundance of possessions" (Luke 12:15).
  • Greed is called idolatry in Colossians 3:5.

Aquinas places avarice among the capital sins (Summa II-II q.118) as an immoderate love of possessing.

Buddhism

The Pali word is lobha, greed or grasping. With hatred (dosa) and delusion (moha) it is one of the three unwholesome roots from which harmful action grows. Its wider form is tanha (craving), which the First Noble Truth's teaching names as the origin of suffering (SN 56.11, the first sermon).

The Dhammapada puts the problem plainly: "There is no satisfying sensual desires even with the rain of gold coins" (verses 186-187, Buddharakkhita translation). Another verse says wealth destroys the foolish who do not seek the far shore (355). Generosity (dana) is the standard remedy, and the Dhammapada (177) says misers do not go to the heavenly realms.

Buddhism does not demand poverty from lay people. It asks you to earn rightly, use wealth wisely and not cling.

Philosophical

Philosophers describe greed as a failure of proportion about wealth.

Aristotle. In the Nicomachean Ethics (4.1) the virtue about wealth is liberality, giving and taking rightly. Its excess, in taking, is illiberality or meanness. In the Politics (1.8-9) he adds that the art of getting wealth has no natural limit once money becomes the aim, and he uses the story of Midas.

Epicurus. Natural wealth is limited and easy to get; it is empty desire that has no limit (Principal Doctrines 15, 29-30).

Seneca. "It is not the man who has too little, but the man who craves more, that is poor" (Letters 2.6).

Kant. In the Metaphysics of Morals (Doctrine of Virtue), avarice (Geiz) is a failure of the duty to use wealth in line with benevolence.
