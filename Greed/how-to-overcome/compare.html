Deadly sin

Greed

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is not to be poor but to be free of the compulsion.

  1. Define "enough". Pick a number or standard for housing, savings and spending, and write it down. Without one, "more" has no end.
  2. Slow purchases. Wait 48 hours before non-essential buys.
  3. Limit comparison. Mute accounts and ads that make you feel behind.
  4. Give regularly. Fixed, planned giving of money or time weakens the grip. Studies find spending on others tends to raise happiness more than spending on yourself.
  5. Spend on experiences and people, not only on things.
  6. Practise gratitude. Write down three things you already have.
  7. Check the feeling underneath. If you shop or chase money when anxious or lonely, deal with that.

Sources

  • Dunn, Aknin & Norton (2008), "Spending money on others promotes happiness," Science 319(5870), 1687-1688. doi:10.1126/science.1150952 (external link)

Steps are general advice, not a tested programme. The citation was from memory and not re-checked. If spending, gambling or hoarding feels out of control, speak with a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Hindu practice answers greed with giving, detachment and contentment.

  • Aparigraha. Practise non-possessiveness: keep what you need and let go of the rest (Yoga Sutras 2.30, 2.39).
  • Santosha. Contentment is one of the niyamas (Yoga Sutras 2.32, 2.42).
  • Dana. Gita 17.20 describes the best gift as one given because it is right to give, to a worthy person, at the right time and place, expecting nothing in return.
  • Offer your work. Gita 3.9 and 3.30 say to act as a sacrifice and surrender actions to the Divine, free of "mine."
  • Moderation. Gita 6.16-17 advises a balanced way of living, neither too much nor too little.
  • Isha 1. Enjoy once you have surrendered all this to the Lord; do not covet.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic remedy is to look for fullness within.

  • Choose shreyas. Katha 1.2.1-2: the wise pick the good over the pleasant. Nachiketa's refusal of wealth in 1.1.26-29 is the model.
  • Renounce to enjoy. Isha 1: "When thou hast surrendered all this, then thou mayest enjoy. Do not covet the wealth of any man!" (Müller translation).
  • Seek the Infinite. Chandogya 7.23 says only the infinite brings happiness. The practice is to turn attention to the Self (atman), through study, reflection and meditation.
  • Be given to giving. Taittiriya 1.11 (the teacher's farewell advice) tells the student to give with faith, in plenty, with modesty and with sympathy.
  • Find the Self in all. Isha 6-7: for one who sees the Self in all beings and all beings in the Self, "what sorrow, what trouble can there be" (Müller translation), so there is nothing to hoard against.

Christianity

The Christian response is generosity, trust and contentment.

  • Give. Acts 20:35 quotes Jesus: "It is more blessed to give than to receive." 2 Corinthians 9:7 says God loves a cheerful giver. Proverbs 11:24-25 says the one who gives freely grows richer.
  • Be content. 1 Timothy 6:6-8: godliness with contentment is great gain; if we have food and clothing, we will be content. Hebrews 13:5 says to keep your life free from the love of money.
  • Trust. Matthew 6:19-21 says to store treasures in heaven, "for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also."
  • Share. Acts 2:44-45 describes believers sharing goods as any had need.
  • Tithing and almsgiving. Traditional disciplines that put giving first.
  • Repentance and restitution. Zacchaeus (Luke 19:8) gives half his goods to the poor and repays fourfold.

Buddhism

Buddhism's remedies are generosity, contentment and insight.

  • Dana. Giving is the first of the practices taught to lay people, and it cuts directly against grasping. Dhammapada 177 praises it.
  • Contentment. The Mangala Sutta (Sn 2.4) lists contentment (santutthi) among the highest blessings.
  • Right livelihood. Earn honestly without harming others (the fifth path factor, as in AN 5.177 on trades to avoid).
  • Reflect on impermanence and not-self. SN 22.59 and the teaching on the aggregates loosen clinging.
  • Renunciation practice. Giving things up on purpose, as on retreat or uposatha days (eight precepts), trains the mind to need less.
  • Metta and sharing merit turn the mind toward others' well-being.

Philosophical

Philosophy offers habits of proportion.

  • Set the limit. Epicurus: natural wealth is limited and easy to get (Principal Doctrines 15). Decide what is natural and necessary, and stop there.
  • Practise voluntary poverty. Seneca (Letters 18) suggests living on little for a few days at a time to learn that you can, so loss holds less fear.
  • Aim at the mean. Aristotle (NE 4.1) says to give and take at the right times, in the right amounts and for the right reasons, and to train this by habit.
  • Use wealth as a tool. Keep it as a means to a good life and not the aim (Politics 1.9).
  • Give. Kant treats helping others as a duty that cannot be dropped.
  • Ask what you are afraid of. Stoic exercises (Epictetus, Enchiridion 1) separate what is up to you from what is not, and wealth is the latter.
