Most of what helps works on the feeling of starting, not on willpower.

1. **Name what you are avoiding.** If a task feels bad, say how: boring, scary, unclear, or likely to show you a result you don't want. The mood-repair account suggests the delay is partly a way of escaping that feeling.
2. **Shrink the first step.** Make it small enough that starting costs almost nothing: open the file, write one sentence, sort ten minutes of receipts.
3. **Decide when and where in advance.** An *implementation intention* is an "if... then..." plan: "When I finish lunch, I will open the report and write for ten minutes." A meta-analysis of 94 tests found that forming such plans had a medium-to-large effect (d = .65) on reaching goals.
4. **Move the reward closer.** Because distant rewards pull weakly, pair the task with something immediate: a fixed hour, a coffee, a person waiting.
5. **Cut the easy escapes.** Put the phone in another room or close the tabs before you begin.
6. **Forgive the slip.** In a study of first-year students, those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before a first exam tended to procrastinate less before the next one. Harsh self-attack adds more feeling to escape from.
7. **Check for other causes.** If low mood, worry or poor sleep sits behind the delay, treating that comes first.

- Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes," *Advances in Experimental Social Psychology* 38, 69-119. [doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1)
- Wohl, Pychyl and Bennett (2010), "I forgive myself, now I can study: how self-forgiveness for procrastinating can reduce future procrastination," *Personality and Individual Differences* 48(7), 803-808. [doi:10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029)

Not checked against the papers: the d = .65 figure was read only in a mirror of the Gollwitzer and Sheeran abstract, and a later, larger synthesis reported smaller averages after correcting for bias (reported in a search summary, not read), so treat the figure as an upper estimate. The Wohl study was read only through secondary summaries; those add that the effect appeared mainly for students who procrastinated a lot on the first exam, and that participants were not randomly assigned. Steps 1, 2, 4, 5 and 7 are common advice and not tied to one study. Checked 2026-11-14.
