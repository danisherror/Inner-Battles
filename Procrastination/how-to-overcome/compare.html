Inner battle

Procrastination

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Most of what helps works on the feeling of starting, not on willpower.

  1. Name what you are avoiding. If a task feels bad, say how: boring, scary, unclear, or likely to show you a result you don't want. The mood-repair account suggests the delay is partly a way of escaping that feeling.
  2. Shrink the first step. Make it small enough that starting costs almost nothing: open the file, write one sentence, sort ten minutes of receipts.
  3. Decide when and where in advance. An implementation intention is an "if... then..." plan: "When I finish lunch, I will open the report and write for ten minutes." A meta-analysis of 94 tests found that forming such plans had a medium-to-large effect (d = .65) on reaching goals.
  4. Move the reward closer. Because distant rewards pull weakly, pair the task with something immediate: a fixed hour, a coffee, a person waiting.
  5. Cut the easy escapes. Put the phone in another room or close the tabs before you begin.
  6. Forgive the slip. In a study of first-year students, those who forgave themselves for procrastinating before a first exam tended to procrastinate less before the next one. Harsh self-attack adds more feeling to escape from.
  7. Check for other causes. If low mood, worry or poor sleep sits behind the delay, treating that comes first.

Sources

  • Gollwitzer and Sheeran (2006), "Implementation intentions and goal achievement: a meta-analysis of effects and processes," Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38, 69-119. doi:10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1 (external link)
  • Wohl, Pychyl and Bennett (2010), "I forgive myself, now I can study: how self-forgiveness for procrastinating can reduce future procrastination," Personality and Individual Differences 48(7), 803-808. doi:10.1016/j.paid.2010.01.029 (external link)
  • Sirois and Pychyl (2013), Social and Personality Psychology Compass 7(2), 115-127. doi:10.1111/spc3.12011 (external link)

Not checked against the papers: the d = .65 figure was read only in a mirror of the Gollwitzer and Sheeran abstract, and a later, larger synthesis reported smaller averages after correcting for bias (reported in a search summary, not read), so treat the figure as an upper estimate. The Wohl study was read only through secondary summaries; those add that the effect appeared mainly for students who procrastinated a lot on the first exam, and that participants were not randomly assigned. Steps 1, 2, 4, 5 and 7 are common advice and not tied to one study. Checked 2026-11-14.

Hinduism

The Gita's answer is steady, unattached action, not waiting for the mood.

  • Act. Verse 3.8: "action is superior to inaction."
  • Do the work as duty, without clinging to results. Verse 3.19 begins: "Therefore, giving up attachment, perform actions as a matter of duty". This removes the fear of the outcome that feeds delay.
  • Aim for the steady doer. Verse 18.26 describes the performer in the mode of goodness as one "free from egotism and attachment, endowed with enthusiasm and determination, and equipoised in success and failure."
  • Lift yourself. Verse 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself."
  • Keep the body regular. Verse 6.17 praises those "temperate in eating and recreation, balanced in work, and regulated in sleep".
  • Arise. Krishna's first word to Arjuna in 2.3 is: "Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise."

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 3.8 (external link), 3.19 (external link), 18.26 (external link), 6.5 (external link), 6.17 (external link), 2.3 (external link)

All quotations were read on those pages (Swami Mukundananda's translation) on 2026-11-14. Reading 3.19 as a remedy for fear of results is an interpretation.

Upanishads

The Upanishadic call is to wake up now and to strive with strength.

  • Rise and awake. Katha 1.3.14: "Rise, awake! having obtained your boons, understand them! The sharp edge of a razor is difficult to pass over; thus the wise say the path (to the Self) is hard." The call to rise is made together with the warning that the way takes effort.
  • Choose the good. Katha 1.2.1-2: distinguish the good from the pleasant and cling to the good.
  • Take the reins. Katha 1.3.6: "he who has understanding and whose mind is always firmly held, his senses are under control, like good horses of a charioteer."
  • Bring strength and earnestness. Mundaka 3.2.4 says the Self is gained by one who strives "by strength, earnestness, and right meditation".

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 1.2.1-2, 1.3.6, 1.3.14
  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 3.2.4

The quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. Other translations differ in wording and sometimes in numbering. Taking these as advice against everyday delay is our reading.

Christianity

The Christian answer joins urgency with grace.

  • Make the most of the time. Ephesians 5:15-16: "Be careful, then, how you live, not as unwise people but as wise, making the most of the time."
  • Now. 2 Corinthians 6:2: "Look, now is the acceptable time; look, now is the day of salvation!"
  • Encourage one another daily. Hebrews 3:13: "But exhort one another every day, as long as it is called 'today'". Accountability is built into the text.
  • Work with your might. Ecclesiastes 9:10: "Whatever your hand finds to do, do with your might".
  • Do not tire of doing good. Galatians 6:9: "So let us not grow weary in doing what is right, for we will reap at harvest time, if we do not give up."
  • Receive help and grace. After describing his divided will, Paul asks, "Who will rescue me from this body of death?" and answers, "Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!" (Romans 7:24-25). Romans 8:1 adds: "there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus." Failure does not end the path.

Sources

Buddhism

The Buddhist remedies are to begin now, with vigour, and to use the practical steps the Buddha gave.

  • Begin today. MN 131: "Today's the day to keenly work— who knows, tomorrow may bring death!"
  • Strive. Dhp 313: "If one is to do what should be done, one should staunchly strive."
  • Stay with the present. MN 131: "Don't run back to the past, don't anticipate the future."
  • Don't hide behind the weather. DN 31 praises the one "who considers heat and cold as no more than blades of grass", who does their duties, and "happiness never fails".
  • Practise steadily. Dhp 23: "They who regularly meditate, always staunchly vigorous; the attentive realize extinguishment."
  • Use a ladder of remedies. For drowsiness, the Buddha gave Moggallana a sequence from the lightest to the most active (AN 7.61): shift attention, think over the teaching, recite it aloud, rub the limbs, wash the eyes, walk about. The same idea applies to delay: try the smallest change first, then a bigger one. See A story in sloth.

Sources

  • MN 131 (external link), Dhp 306-319 (external link), DN 31 (external link), Dhp 21-32 (external link), AN 7.61 (external link)

Philosophical

The philosophers' remedies are to act now, to build the habit by acting, and to stop waiting for a better moment.

  • Hold the hour. Seneca: "hold every hour in your grasp. Lay hold of to-day's task, and you will not need to depend so much upon to-morrow's."
  • Begin as you are. Epictetus: "This instant, then, think yourself worthy of living as a man grown up, and a proficient." When pain or pleasure is set before you, "remember that now is the combat, now the Olympiad comes on, nor can it be put off."
  • Become by doing. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2: "we learn by doing them, e.g. men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." Action builds the habit, and habit builds the character that acts.
  • Answer the morning excuse. Marcus Aurelius's reply to himself when he does not want to get up is in A story.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion (external link) (Higginson)
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 2 (external link) (Ross)
