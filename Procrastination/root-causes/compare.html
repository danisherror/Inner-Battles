Modern

Research points to several causes that often work together.

  • The task feels bad. In a meta-analysis of 691 correlations, the strong and consistent predictors of procrastination were task aversiveness, task delay, self-efficacy and impulsiveness, plus conscientiousness and its facets: self-control, distractibility, organisation and achievement motivation. Neuroticism, rebelliousness and sensation seeking showed only a weak connection.
  • Now beats later. The authors explain these results with temporal motivation theory, which combines expectancy theory with hyperbolic discounting. In plain terms: a reward loses pull the further away it is, so the relief of avoiding a task today can outweigh a payoff that comes later.
  • Mood repair. Sirois and Pychyl argue that procrastination has a great deal to do with short-term mood repair and emotion regulation: putting a task off makes an uncomfortable feeling go away for now. They add that the costs of delay fall mainly on the future self.
  • Doubt about being able to do it. Low self-efficacy, the belief that you can carry the task out, is on the list above. Starting feels risky when you expect to do it badly.

Sources

  • Steel (2007), Psychological Bulletin 133(1), 65-94. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65 (external link)
  • Sirois and Pychyl (2013), "Procrastination and the priority of short-term mood regulation: consequences for future self," Social and Personality Psychology Compass 7(2), 115-127. doi:10.1111/spc3.12011 (external link)

The Steel list was read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14). The plain-language gloss of temporal motivation theory is ours. For Sirois and Pychyl, the abstract's argument was read only as summarised in search results (the accepted manuscript is at eprints.whiterose.ac.uk (external link)); the exact wording was not checked, and the paper's own title was taken from memory.

Hinduism

The Gita traces the habit of putting things off to ignorance, attachment and the pull of comfort.

  • Ignorance. Verse 14.8 says tamas is "born of ignorance". Verse 14.17 adds: "from the mode of ignorance arise negligence and delusion."
  • Comfort mistaken for happiness. Verse 18.39 describes the happiness of tamas as the kind "derived from sleep, indolence, and negligence". It is pleasant now, which is the same pull that makes delay attractive.
  • Fear of the outcome. Verse 2.47 says: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, nor be attached to inaction." One reading is that worry about results can freeze a person into inaction, which is why the verse names both attachment to fruits and attachment to inaction.
  • The mind as its own enemy. Verse 6.5 says the mind "can be the friend and also the enemy of the self".

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 14.8 (external link), 14.17 (external link), 18.39 (external link), 2.47 (external link), 6.5 (external link)

All quotations were read on those pages (Swami Mukundananda's translation) on 2026-11-14. The reading of 2.47 as being about fear freezing action is an interpretation, not stated in the verse.

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the root in the uncontrolled mind and in the pull of what pleases now.

  • The pleasant over the good. Katha 1.2.2: "the wise prefers the good to the pleasant, but the fool chooses the pleasant through greed and avarice."
  • Delusion by wealth and comfort. Katha 1.2.6: "The Hereafter never rises before the eyes of the careless child, deluded by the delusion of wealth. 'This is the world,' he thinks, 'there is no other.'"
  • Reins that are not held. Katha 1.3.3-6 compares the self to a rider in a chariot: the body is the chariot, the intellect the charioteer, the mind the reins and the senses the horses. "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer."

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) (Max Müller's translation) 1.2.2, 1.2.6, 1.3.3-6

The quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. Reading these passages as roots of everyday delay is an interpretation; the text speaks of the pursuit of the Self.

Christianity

Christian writers find the roots of delay in a divided will, in the force of habit, and in fear.

  • A divided will. In Romans 7:15-19 Paul writes: "I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate... For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do."
  • The force of habit. Augustine describes how he kept putting off a change he knew he needed. In the Confessions (8.5) he says: "the law of sin is the violence of custom, whereby the mind is drawn and held, even against its will; deserving to be so held in that it so willingly falls into it."
  • Waiting for perfect conditions. Ecclesiastes 11:4: "Whoever observes the wind will not sow, and whoever regards the clouds will not reap."
  • Fear. In the parable of the talents, the third servant says: "I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground" (Matthew 25:25).

Sources

  • Romans 7:15-19 (external link), Ecclesiastes 11:4 (external link), Matthew 25:24-25 (external link)
  • Augustine, Confessions 8 (external link) (Pilkington translation)

The Bible quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway, and the Augustine quotation in the Pilkington translation at New Advent, on 2026-11-14. The section number "8.5" for the Confessions passage comes from the chapter heading at New Advent and was not compared with a printed edition. Reading Romans 7 as being about delay is an interpretation; Paul is writing about the struggle with sin in general.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts point to excuses, a sunken mind and a mind pulled away from the present.

  • Excuses. In the Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), the Buddha says of habitual laziness: "You don't get your work done because you think: 'It's too cold! It's too hot. It's too late! It's too early! I'm too hungry! I'm too full!'"
  • A sunken, heavy mind. Dhp 280: "They don't get going when it's time to start; they're young and strong, but given to sloth. Their mind depressed in sunken thought, lazy and slothful, they can't discern the path."
  • Heedlessness. Dhp 21 contrasts heedfulness with heedlessness, "the state of death".
  • A mind that is elsewhere. MN 131 begins: "Don't run back to the past, don't anticipate the future. What's past is left behind, the future has not arrived; and any present phenomenon you clearly discern in every case." The advice implies that attention drifting to what was or will be keeps us from the work in front of us. That reading is ours.

Sources

  • MN 131 (external link)

Philosophical

The philosophers offer several explanations of why we know and still do not act.

  • Ignorance (Socrates). In Aristotle's report, Socrates held that "no one... when he judges acts against what he judges best" and that people act so "only by reason of ignorance". On that view delay is a failure to see clearly what is best.
  • Emotion overcoming a decision (Aristotle). Aristotle separates two kinds of incontinence: "impetuosity" and "weakness". In the second, "some men after deliberating fail, owing to their emotion, to stand by the conclusions of their deliberation". This is the one closest to the person who plans well and does not follow through.
  • Carelessness (Seneca). In his first letter to Lucilius he writes: "The most disgraceful kind of loss, however, is that due to carelessness."
  • Waiting for someone else (Epictetus). "What other master, then, do you wait for, to throw upon that the delay of reforming yourself?"
