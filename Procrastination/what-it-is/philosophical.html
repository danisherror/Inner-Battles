Procrastination

Putting off what matters until later, though you know the delay will cost you.

Philosophical lens

What it is

Philosophers do not use the word procrastination, but they have long described acting against what you judge best. The Greek word is akrasia, weakness of will, and it is the closest philosophical idea.

Hesiod is blunt about delay in Works and Days: "Do not put your work off till to-morrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work: industry makes work go well, but a man who puts off work is always at hand-grips with ruin" (Evelyn-White's translation).

Aristotle treats akrasia in Nicomachean Ethics 7. He notes that Socrates denied it could happen: "no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best—people act so only by reason of ignorance." Aristotle says this "plainly contradicts the observed facts", and sets out to explain how a person can know the better and still not do it.

Marcus Aurelius writes to himself: "Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things, and how often thou hast received an opportunity from the gods, and yet dost not use it" (Meditations 2, Long's translation).

Akrasia is related to procrastination and not identical to it. It covers any case of acting against one's judgment of what is best, and procrastination is the form in which the action is delayed.
