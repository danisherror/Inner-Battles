Procrastination

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Procrastination is putting off something you intend to do, even though you expect to be worse off for the delay. That last part matters. Doing a more urgent task first, or waiting for information you really need, is planning. Procrastination is delay you do not endorse and usually end up paying for.

It tends to take a few forms:

  • Avoiding a task that feels unpleasant, boring or frightening, such as a tax form, a hard email or a first draft.
  • Substituting easier work, such as tidying, checking messages or "just a bit more research", so you feel busy without starting.
  • Waiting to feel ready, motivated or in the right mood.
  • Leaving everything to the last stretch and relying on deadline pressure.

Researchers treat it as a problem of self-regulation, not of time management. Steel's 2007 review calls it "a prevalent and pernicious form of self-regulatory failure".

It is related to sloth but not identical. Sloth is a wider neglect of what matters; procrastination is the specific habit of delay, and it is common among people who care a great deal and are far from lazy. Persistent trouble starting things can also have other causes, such as low mood, anxiety or attention difficulties, which a doctor or therapist can help to tell apart.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no sin called procrastination and no single word equal to it, but the Bhagavad Gita names the procrastinator directly. In 18.28, a performer of action "in the mode of ignorance" (tamas) is described as "undisciplined, vulgar, stubborn, deceitful, slothful, despondent, and a procrastinator". The Sanskrit word glossed "procrastinator" there is dirgha-sutri.

Tamas is one of the three gunas, the qualities of inertia, dullness and heaviness. In 14.8 the Gita says it is born of ignorance and binds embodied beings through negligence (pramada), laziness (alasya) and sleep (nidra).

This is related to procrastination and not identical to it. The Gita is describing a quality that shapes how a person acts, to be balanced and outgrown, and not a sin. See also sloth in Hinduism, which covers alasya more broadly.

Upanishads

The Upanishads have no word for procrastination, and their concern is wider than tasks and deadlines. The nearest idea is heedlessness and the habit of choosing what is pleasant now over what is good.

In the Katha Upanishad, Death (Yama) tells Nachiketa: "The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man." The wise person "prefers the good to the pleasant, but the fool chooses the pleasant through greed and avarice" (Katha 1.2.1-2). Delay is one way of choosing the pleasant.

Katha 1.2.6 speaks of "the careless child, deluded by the delusion of wealth", for whom the Hereafter "never rises before the eyes".

This is related to procrastination and not identical to it: the Upanishads are speaking of postponing the pursuit of knowledge of the Self, and of the pleasant over the good in general, and not of everyday tasks.

Christianity

The Bible has no word for procrastination, but it describes the habit again and again in the figure of the sluggard, the person who always means to start later.

Proverbs 6:9-11 asks: "How long will you lie there, O lazybones? When will you rise from your sleep? A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come upon you like a robber."

Ecclesiastes 11:4 gives the excuse of waiting for the perfect moment: "Whoever observes the wind will not sow, and whoever regards the clouds will not reap."

James 4:17 makes the link to conscience: "Anyone, then, who knows the right thing to do and fails to do it commits sin."

Procrastination is related to the Christian idea of sloth (acedia) but not identical to it. The sluggard texts are about neglecting work and duty, and acedia is a deeper weariness of spirit; see sloth in Christianity.

Buddhism

Buddhist texts have no word for procrastination as such, but they speak about it in several ways: heedlessness (pamada) and its opposite heedfulness (appamada), laziness (kosajja) and the hindrance of sloth and torpor (thina-middha). See sloth in Buddhism.

The Dhammapada says: "Heedfulness is the state free of death; heedlessness is the state of death. The heedful do not die, while the heedless are like the dead" (Dhp 21).

The urgency of "now" is stated in the Bhaddekaratta Sutta (MN 131): "Today's the day to keenly work— who knows, tomorrow may bring death!"

And Dhp 313 says: "If one is to do what should be done, one should staunchly strive."

This is related to procrastination and not identical to it: the texts speak of heedlessness in practice and in livelihood, and not of tasks and deadlines in general.

Philosophical

Philosophers do not use the word procrastination, but they have long described acting against what you judge best. The Greek word is akrasia, weakness of will, and it is the closest philosophical idea.

Hesiod is blunt about delay in Works and Days: "Do not put your work off till to-morrow and the day after; for a sluggish worker does not fill his barn, nor one who puts off his work: industry makes work go well, but a man who puts off work is always at hand-grips with ruin" (Evelyn-White's translation).

Aristotle treats akrasia in Nicomachean Ethics 7. He notes that Socrates denied it could happen: "no one, he said, when he judges acts against what he judges best—people act so only by reason of ignorance." Aristotle says this "plainly contradicts the observed facts", and sets out to explain how a person can know the better and still not do it.

Marcus Aurelius writes to himself: "Remember how long thou hast been putting off these things, and how often thou hast received an opportunity from the gods, and yet dost not use it" (Meditations 2, Long's translation).

Akrasia is related to procrastination and not identical to it. It covers any case of acting against one's judgment of what is best, and procrastination is the form in which the action is delayed.
