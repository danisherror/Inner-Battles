Procrastination

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The costs usually arrive later than the relief.

  • Stress and health. In two studies of students, Tice and Baumeister found that procrastinators reported less stress and less illness early in the semester, but more stress and more illness late in the term, and were sicker overall. They also received lower grades on all assignments.
  • A loop. Avoiding a task brings quick relief, and the guilt and pressure come after. That is consistent with the mood-repair account in Root causes, where the cost is carried by the future self. Reading it as a loop is our inference, not a finding of these studies.
  • A growing habit. Steel notes that the prevalence of procrastination appears to be growing.
  • Everyday costs. Rushed work, late fees and strained trust with people who were waiting on you are common results, though these are general observations and not from the studies above.

These studies were of students at one time and place, and they show association, not proof that delay caused the illness or the grades.

Sources

  • Tice and Baumeister (1997), "Longitudinal study of procrastination, performance, stress, and health: the costs and benefits of dawdling," Psychological Science 8(6), 454-458. doi:10.1111/j.1467-9280.1997.tb00460.x (external link)
  • Steel (2007), Psychological Bulletin 133(1), 65-94. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.65 (external link)

The Tice and Baumeister results were read in the abstract as quoted in a search result on 2026-11-14, not in the journal page or full text. The Steel remark on prevalence was read in the abstract. If stress or low mood is building up, please talk to a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

In the Gita the effects of tamas, the inertia behind procrastination, are described as a binding and a clouding.

  • It binds. Verse 14.8 says tamas "binds" through negligence, laziness and sleep.
  • It clouds judgment. Verse 14.17: "from the mode of ignorance arise negligence and delusion."
  • It hides the self. Verse 18.39 says happiness derived from sleep, indolence and negligence "covers the nature of the self from beginning to end".
  • It sits beside despair. In 18.28 the procrastinator is listed together with the "slothful" and the "despondent".
  • Doing nothing is not an option. Verse 3.8 says "action is superior to inaction. By ceasing activity, even your bodily maintenance will not be possible." Taken at the extreme, putting everything off cannot be sustained.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 14.8 (external link), 14.17 (external link), 18.28 (external link), 18.39 (external link), 3.8 (external link)

All quotations were read on those pages (Swami Mukundananda's translation) on 2026-11-14. Applying 3.8 to everyday delay is a reading, since the verse is about complete inaction.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe the result of heedlessness as going round in circles, without arriving.

  • Staggering in darkness. Katha 1.2.5: "Fools dwelling in darkness, wise in their own conceit, and puffed up with vain knowledge, go round and round, staggering to and fro, like blind men led by the blind."
  • Falling under death's sway again and again. After the careless child thinks "there is no other" world, "thus he falls again and again under my sway" (Katha 1.2.6).
  • The goal slips away. Mundaka 3.2.4: the Self is not gained "by one who is destitute of strength, or without earnestness, or without right meditation."
  • The restless, uncontrolled mind never arrives. Katha 1.3.7: one "who has no understanding, who is unmindful and always impure, never reaches that place, but enters into the round of births."

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 1.2.5-6, 1.3.7
  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) (Müller) 3.2.4

The quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. The passages concern the ultimate goal; applying them to everyday delay is our reading.

Christianity

Scripture describes delay as costing a person their harvest, and sometimes their chance.

  • Poverty and loss. Proverbs 6:10-11: "A little sleep, a little slumber, a little folding of the hands to rest, and poverty will come upon you like a robber, and want, like an armed warrior."
  • A missed harvest. Proverbs 20:4: "The lazy person does not plow in season; harvest comes, and there is nothing to be found."
  • Wanting without getting. Proverbs 13:4: "The appetite of the lazy craves and gets nothing, while the appetite of the diligent is richly supplied."
  • An uncertain tomorrow. Proverbs 27:1: "Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring." James 4:14 adds: "you are a mist that appears for a little while and then vanishes."
  • Delay that feels short and is not. Augustine writes that his answer to the call was only "Presently, lo, presently; Leave me a little while. But presently, presently, had no present; and my leave me a little while went on for a long while" (Confessions 8.5).

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts describe the effects as lost livelihood, a hard life and a wasted chance.

  • Lost income. After the list of excuses, DN 31 says: "By dwelling on so many excuses for not working, you don't make any more money, and the money you already have runs out." The verses add: "When the young neglect their work like this, riches pass them by."
  • A good deed left undone. Dhp 314: "A bad deed is better left undone, for it will plague you later on. A good deed is better done, one that does not plague you."
  • A lax effort stirs up dust. Dhp 313 speaks of "the life gone forth when laxly led", which "just stirs up dust all the more". The verse is about the monastic life.
  • No bargaining with time. MN 131: "who knows, tomorrow may bring death! For there is no bargain to be struck with Death and his mighty horde."

Philosophical

The philosophers describe the cost of delay as lost time, lost character and eventually a life half lived.

  • Ruin. Hesiod: "a man who puts off work is always at hand-grips with ruin" (Works and Days, Evelyn-White).
  • Time slips away. Seneca, Letter 1: "While we are postponing, life speeds by." And: "Nothing, Lucilius, is ours, except time."
  • No progress, then no life. Epictetus warns that if you "always add procrastination to procrastination, purpose to purpose, and fix day after day in which you will attend to yourself, you will insensibly continue without proficiency, and, living and dying, persevere in being one of the vulgar" (Enchiridion, Higginson's translation).
  • Opportunities pass. Marcus Aurelius: "how often thou hast received an opportunity from the gods, and yet dost not use it" (Meditations 2).
