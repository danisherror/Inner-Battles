Procrastination

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how procrastination works and how people step out of it.

Dev's story

Dev, a freelance designer, had a tax return due at the end of the month. The receipts were in three currencies and two email accounts. Each evening he opened the folder, saw the pile, and closed it. He answered client emails instead. That was useful work, and it felt like a good reason.

The relief of closing the folder lasted about ten minutes. Then came a dull dread that followed him through dinner. He told himself he'd feel clearer on the weekend, and the weekend came and went.

Two days before the deadline he sat up until three in the morning and filed. He found two mistakes later.

The next year he tried something different.

What helped:

  1. He named the feeling. It wasn't laziness. He was dreading finding out how much he owed.
  2. He shrank the first step. The goal was ten minutes of sorting receipts into piles, nothing more.
  3. He made an if-then plan. "When I finish lunch on Tuesday and Thursday, I will put the laptop on the kitchen table and sort receipts before opening email."
  4. He told his sister, who agreed to text him on Thursday evening: "Did you sort?"
  5. He forgave the slip. When he missed a Tuesday, he wrote "missed Tuesday, doing Thursday" and moved on, instead of deciding he was hopeless.

The number at the end was no better than he feared, but it was no longer a secret. He filed a week early.

What it shows: Procrastination often protects us from a feeling. A small first step, a plan fixed in advance and some kindness did more than a fresh resolve.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If delay or dread is constant, please talk to a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Arjuna puts down his bow

The Bhagavad Gita opens on a battlefield. Arjuna, a warrior, sees his relatives and teachers on the other side and is overcome. In 1.47, "Arjun cast aside his bow and arrows, and sank into the seat of his chariot, his mind in distress and overwhelmed with grief."

In 2.9 he tells Krishna, "Govind, I shall not fight," and falls silent.

This is not procrastination in the narrow sense, since Arjuna is refusing, out of grief and fear, and not just putting off. It is related to it, though: a person who knows what duty asks of them and cannot begin.

Krishna does not argue him into action at once. His first words in 2.3 are: "O Parth, it does not befit you to yield to this unmanliness. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise, O vanquisher of enemies." Then comes the long teaching that follows, about duty, attachment to results and steadiness.

At the end, in 18.73, Arjuna says: "by Your grace my illusion has been dispelled, and I am situated in knowledge. I am now free from doubts, and I shall act according to Your instructions."

What it shows: Paralysis before a hard duty is treated as a state of confusion that can be lifted by understanding, and the answer is to rise and act, not to wait until the fear passes.

Upanishads

Nachiketa will not be put off

In the Katha Upanishad, a boy named Nachiketa comes to the house of Death. Death offers him three wishes. For his third, Nachiketa asks what happens after death, a question that even the gods have doubted.

Death tries to talk him out of it. He offers sons and grandsons who will live a hundred years, herds, elephants, gold, horses, a kingdom and long life, and says: "do not ask me about dying" (1.1.25).

Nachiketa answers: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses. Even the whole of life is short." And: "No man can be made happy by wealth." He asks only for the boon he chose (1.1.26-27), and keeps to it.

Death's reply opens the second chapter with the choice of the good over the pleasant (1.2.1).

This is a story of the opposite of procrastination: a boy who will not be distracted by the pleasant from the question that matters.

What it shows: Each offer was a reason to put off the hard question, and he declined each. Knowing what matters most and refusing the easier substitute is the Upanishadic remedy.

Sources

Christianity

Augustine in the garden

In his Confessions, Augustine tells of years in which he knew he should change his life and put it off. He remembers praying: "Grant me chastity and continency, but not yet" (8.7). He was afraid, he says, that God would hear him too soon.

The turning point comes in a garden in Milan (8.12). In tears, he cries out: "How long, how long? Tomorrow, and tomorrow? Why not now? Why is there not this hour an end to my uncleanness?"

Then he hears a child's voice from a nearby house, repeating: "Take up and read; take up and read." He takes it as a command to open the book, picks up the volume of Paul's letters, and reads the first passage his eyes fall on, from Romans 13:13-14: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof."

He writes: "No further would I read, nor did I need; for instantly, as the sentence ended — by a light, as it were, of security infused into my heart — all the gloom of doubt vanished away."

What it shows: Augustine's delay was over leaving a way of life he knew he should leave, and for years the hard part was not knowing what to do but starting. He describes it as a divided will and the force of habit, and what broke the pattern was one clear act of turning, not a stronger resolve to do it later.

Sources

Buddhism

Sigalaka and the six directions

In the Sigalovada Sutta (DN 31), the Buddha is staying in the Bamboo Grove near Rajagaha. A young householder, Sigalaka, rises early and, with his clothes and hair wet, raises his cupped palms to revere the six directions: east, south, west, north, below and above.

The Buddha sees him and asks why. Sigalaka says that on his deathbed his father told him: "My dear, please revere the quarters." He says he is honouring his father's words.

The Buddha answers: "Householder's son, that's not how the six directions should be revered in the training of the Noble One." He then teaches what it really means: giving up corrupt deeds, avoiding six drains on wealth, telling fake friends from good-hearted ones, and so "covering" the six directions in daily conduct.

One of those drains is habitual laziness. The Buddha says its drawback is that "You don't get your work done because you think: 'It's too cold! It's too hot. It's too late! It's too early! I'm too hungry! I'm too full!'"

What it shows: The sutta is not about procrastination alone, but it names its excuses very exactly. The teaching also moves the young man from a ritual that costs nothing but a wet morning to the daily conduct that would change his life, which is a version of "stop performing, start doing".

Philosophical

Marcus Aurelius and the warm bed

Marcus Aurelius was a Roman emperor and a Stoic. His Meditations are notes he wrote to himself, not a book written for readers, and in Book 5 he records an argument with himself about getting up in the morning.

He begins: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present—I am rising to the work of a human being." He then puts his own excuse in the mouth of an objector and answers it:

"Or have I been made for this, to lie in the bed-clothes and keep myself warm?—But this is more pleasant.—Dost thou exist then to take thy pleasure, and not at all for action or exertion? Dost thou not see the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders, the bees working together to put in order their several parts of the universe?"

He allows the objection that rest is necessary, and replies that nature "has fixed bounds to this too" (Long's translation).

What it shows: The delay he describes is small and ordinary, a few more minutes in bed. His method is to say the excuse aloud, to answer it, and to remind himself what the day is for, and then to get up.
