Procrastination

A story

Augustine in the garden

In his Confessions, Augustine tells of years in which he knew he should change his life and put it off. He remembers praying: "Grant me chastity and continency, but not yet" (8.7). He was afraid, he says, that God would hear him too soon.

The turning point comes in a garden in Milan (8.12). In tears, he cries out: "How long, how long? Tomorrow, and tomorrow? Why not now? Why is there not this hour an end to my uncleanness?"

Then he hears a child's voice from a nearby house, repeating: "Take up and read; take up and read." He takes it as a command to open the book, picks up the volume of Paul's letters, and reads the first passage his eyes fall on, from Romans 13:13-14: "put on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof."

He writes: "No further would I read, nor did I need; for instantly, as the sentence ended — by a light, as it were, of security infused into my heart — all the gloom of doubt vanished away."

What it shows: Augustine's delay was over leaving a way of life he knew he should leave, and for years the hard part was not knowing what to do but starting. He describes it as a divided will and the force of habit, and what broke the pattern was one clear act of turning, not a stronger resolve to do it later.
