Addiction and compulsion

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Addiction is a repeated pull toward a substance or an activity, such as alcohol, drugs, gambling, a phone or pornography, that goes on despite a wish to stop and despite the harm. Compulsion is the sense that the act is driven and not chosen. Those are standard descriptions and not taken from the papers below.

Researchers disagree about what to call it. Leshner writes that "drug addiction is a chronic, relapsing disease that results from the prolonged effects of drugs on the brain", with "behavioral and social-context aspects" that "are important parts of the disorder itself". Lewis describes Heyman's view differently: "Rather than view addiction as a disease, Heyman sees it as a choice-one that works like other choices, whereby immediate rewards outshine long-term gains." Lewis himself argues that "the disease-versus-choice debate creates a false dichotomy". These abstracts are about drugs and alcohol, and do not cover phones or gambling.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: doing more than you meant, failed attempts to cut down, hiding it, and craving when a cue appears. It overlaps with impulsivity and distraction.

Struggling to stop is common, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If you are finding it hard, please talk to a doctor, therapist or a local support service. Stopping some substances suddenly, including alcohol and certain medicines, can be dangerous, so ask a doctor before stopping on your own (a standard safety point, not drawn from the papers below). If you are in danger, or thinking of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

  • Lewis (2011), "Dopamine and the neural 'now': essay and review of Addiction: A Disorder of Choice," Perspectives on Psychological Science 6(2), 150-155. doi:10.1177/1745691611400235 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for addiction, but it describes the pull. Gita 2.60: "The senses are so strong and turbulent, O son of Kunti, that they can forcibly carry away the mind even of a person endowed with discrimination who practices self-control."

And 3.34: "The senses naturally experience attachment and aversion to the sense objects, but do not be controlled by them, for they are way-layers and foes."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The verses are about the senses and the mind in general, and do not describe a medical condition. Reading them as an account of compulsion is ours. They say that even a disciplined person can be carried away, which is a reason to see the pull as common and not shameful. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Upanishads

The Upanishads name the same pull as the choice of the pleasant over the good. Katha 1.2.1: "The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man. It is well with him who clings to the good; he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."

And 1.3.5: "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The Katha describes a choice made by a person with understanding or without it, and does not describe a condition that takes the choice away. Reading it as an account of compulsion is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Christianity

Paul describes what it is like to act against your own wish. Romans 7:15: "I do not understand my own actions. For I do not do what I want, but I do the very thing I hate."

And 7:19: "For I do not do the good I want, but the evil I do not want is what I do."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Paul is writing about sin and the law, and the verses are not about substances or behaviours. Reading them as an account of compulsion is ours. The tradition's word for what he describes is not applied to people who struggle with addiction here, and the site does not treat addiction as a sin. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name the pull as craving. SN 56.11, the first teaching: "Now this is the noble truth of the origin of suffering. It's the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights."

Dhp 334: "When a man lives heedlessly, craving grows in them like a camel's foot creeper. They jump from one thing to the next, like a langur greedy for fruit in a forest grove."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The Pali word for craving is broader than addiction, and the texts describe it as part of ordinary suffering. Reading them as an account of compulsion is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Philosophical

Aristotle describes acting against what you know. Nicomachean Ethics 7.1: "the incontinent man, knowing that what he does is bad, does it as a result of passion, while the continent man, knowing that his appetites are bad, refuses on account of his rational principle to follow them".

Plato gives the picture. Phaedrus: "the human charioteer drives his in a pair; and one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed; and the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Aristotle's "incontinence" (the Greek word is akrasia, weakness of will) is about acting against one's judgment and not about a medical condition, and Plato's image is of the soul in general. Reading them as an account of addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.
