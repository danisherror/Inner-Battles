Modern

The research read here finds that recovery is common and that help takes several forms. Kelly and colleagues, in a survey of US adults, found a "Weighted prevalence of problem resolution" of 9.1%, concluding that "Tens of millions of Americans have successfully resolved an AOD problem using a variety of traditional and non-traditional means", and that 53.9% reported "assisted" pathways, with mutual help most used (45.1%), then treatment (27.6%). Those are self-reports. The Cochrane review, for alcohol use disorder, found "high quality evidence that manualized AA/TSF interventions are more effective than other established treatments, such as CBT, for increasing abstinence", and that "Non-manualized AA/TSF may perform as well". The steps below are common advice.

  1. Tell someone. Secrecy helps the pattern. That is common advice.
  2. See a doctor. A doctor can assess what is going on, advise on stopping safely, and discuss treatment, including medicines where they are suitable. That is a standard statement, not drawn from the papers.
  3. Use support. Mutual-help groups, counselling and treatment are the main routes in Kelly and colleagues' survey, and no one route is right for everyone.
  4. Change the setting. Remove or block what triggers the habit, and tell people who can help. That is common advice; see impulsivity.
  5. Ride out urges. Urges rise and fall; a delay, a walk or a call can get you past one. That is common advice.
  6. Treat a slip as information. A slip is a reason to adjust the plan and not to give up, and shame does not help. That is common advice.
  7. Keep safe. Struggling to stop is common, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If you are finding it hard, please talk to a doctor, therapist or a local support service. Stopping some substances suddenly, including alcohol and certain medicines, can be dangerous, so ask a doctor before stopping on your own (a standard safety point, not drawn from the papers below). If you are in danger, or thinking of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Kelly, Bergman, Hoeppner, Vilsaint and White (2017), "Prevalence and pathways of recovery from drug and alcohol problems in the United States population," Drug and Alcohol Dependence 181, 162-169. doi:10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2017.09.028 (external link)
  • Kelly, Humphreys and Ferri (2020), "Alcoholics Anonymous and other 12-step programs for alcohol use disorder," Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2020(9). doi:10.1002/14651858.CD012880.pub2 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

Both papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The survey is of US adults and relies on their own reports of a resolved problem, and the Cochrane review concerns alcohol and the 12-step approach only. Steps 1 and 3 to 6 are common advice or a reading of the survey, and are not tied to a trial of those steps.

Hinduism

The Gita counsels moderation, practice and not being ruled by the senses.

  1. Do not be controlled. 3.34: "The senses naturally experience attachment and aversion to the sense objects, but do not be controlled by them".
  2. Keep a steady rhythm. 6.17: "those who are temperate in eating and recreation, balanced in work, and regulated in sleep, can mitigate all sorrows by practicing Yog."
  3. Practise, and let go. 6.35: "the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled."

It is also honest about relapse. 2.59: "Aspirants may restrain the senses from their objects of enjoyment, but the taste for the sense objects remains."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. These verses are about the disciplined life and not about treatment. Reading them as a way through addiction is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Upanishads

The Katha counsels telling the good from the pleasant, and taking the reins.

  1. Tell them apart. 1.2.2: "The good and the pleasant approach man: the wise goes round about them and distinguishes them."
  2. Take the reins. 1.3.6: "But he who has understanding and whose mind is always firmly held, his senses are under control, like good horses of a charioteer."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The passages are about wisdom and understanding, and the image of holding reins should not be read as "just control it". The site makes no claim about how to practise this, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Christianity

The Christian texts counsel not being alone in the struggle, asking to be rescued, and standing firm.

  1. You are not alone in this. 1 Corinthians 10:13: "No testing has overtaken you that is not common to everyone."
  2. Look for the way out. The same verse: "God is faithful, and he will not let you be tested beyond your strength, but with the testing he will also provide the way out so that you may be able to endure it."
  3. Ask to be rescued. Romans 7:24-25: "Wretched person that I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!"
  4. Stand firm. Galatians 5:1: "For freedom Christ has set us free. Stand firm, therefore, and do not submit again to a yoke of slavery."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The verses should not be read as saying that someone who slips lacked faith. Reading them as a way through addiction is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts counsel digging up the root, and trying again.

  1. Go to the root. Dhp 337: "dig up the root of craving, as you'd dig up grass in search of roots."
  2. Let it go. SN 56.11: "the fading away and cessation of that very same craving no residue left behind; giving it away, letting it go, releasing it, and not clinging to it."
  3. Do not be broken by a slip. Dhp 337: "Don't let Māra break you again and again, like a stream breaking a reed."
  4. Know it can fall away. Dhp 336: "their sorrows fall from them, like a drop from a lotus-leaf."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The texts speak of craving in general and of a path of practice, and do not describe treatment. Reading them as a way through addiction is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Philosophical

The philosophers' counsel is to know which horse is pulling, and to decide before the pull.

  1. Name the pull. Plato, Phaedrus: "one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed".
  2. Decide before the moment. Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 7.7: the impetuous "do not await the argument, because they are apt to follow their imagination." So the argument is best made early.
  3. Let regret lead somewhere. Nicomachean Ethics 7.8: "incontinent man is likely to repent."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Aristotle is writing about weakness of will, and step 2 is the site's own reading of his remark about those who do not wait for the argument. Plato's "ignoble" horse is an image in the text, and neither should be read as calling a person ignoble. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.
