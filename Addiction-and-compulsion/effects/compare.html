Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes costs mostly for alcohol and drugs. Kelly and colleagues write that "Alcohol and other drug (AOD) problems confer a global, prodigious burden of disease, disability, and premature mortality." The Cochrane review adds that alcohol use disorder "confers a prodigious burden of disease, disability, premature mortality, and high economic costs from lost productivity, accidents, violence, incarceration, and increased healthcare utilization." Leshner points to "the health and social costs associated with drug abuse and addiction".

These abstracts are about alcohol and other drugs, and do not cover phones, gambling or pornography.

Common effects, as people describe them and not findings from these papers: secrecy, money trouble, strained relationships, poor sleep, and a growing distance from what you used to enjoy. Shame often follows, and it tends to feed the pattern; see shame and guilt.

Struggling to stop is common, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If you are finding it hard, please talk to a doctor, therapist or a local support service. Stopping some substances suddenly, including alcohol and certain medicines, can be dangerous, so ask a doctor before stopping on your own (a standard safety point, not drawn from the papers below). If you are in danger, or thinking of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Kelly, Bergman, Hoeppner, Vilsaint and White (2017), "Prevalence and pathways of recovery from drug and alcohol problems in the United States population," Drug and Alcohol Dependence 181, 162-169. doi:10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2017.09.028 (external link)
  • Kelly, Humphreys and Ferri (2020), "Alcoholics Anonymous and other 12-step programs for alcohol use disorder," Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews 2020(9). doi:10.1002/14651858.CD012880.pub2 (external link)
  • Leshner (1997), "Addiction is a brain disease, and it matters," Science 278(5335), 45-47. doi:10.1126/science.278.5335.45 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

All three papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The list of common effects and the remark about shame are common description and cite no study.

Hinduism

The Gita names where the chain ends. 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

And 3.37 names the enemy: "It is lust alone, which is born of contact with the mode of passion, and later transformed into anger. Know this as the sinful, all-devouring enemy in the world."

The commentary on 3.37 says the Vedas use the word for lust "not only for sexual desires but also to include all desires for material enjoyment".

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The verses describe what unchecked desire does, and the harsh words ("ruined", "sinful", "enemy") are the translator's and should not be taken as a verdict on a person. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Upanishads

The Katha names the cost of the pleasant. 1.2.1: "he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."

And 1.3.7: "He who has no understanding, who is unmindful and always impure, never reaches that place, but enters into the round of births."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The passages are about what a life spent on the pleasant misses, and are not about the effects of a condition. Reading them as effects of addiction is ours, and the harsh verdict of 1.3.7 should not be taken as a verdict on anyone who struggles. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Christianity

Proverbs describes the effects of drinking without softening them: "Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has strife? Who has complaining? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes? Those who linger late over wine".

It ends with the voice of the one who cannot stop: "'They struck me,' you will say, 'but I was not hurt; they beat me, but I did not feel it. When shall I awake? I will seek another drink.'"

Romans 7:24 gives the cry: "Wretched person that I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death?"

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Proverbs is wisdom writing about wine, and Romans 7:24 is about sin. Reading them together as effects of addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts set sorrow and its falling away side by side. Dhp 335: "Whoever is beaten by this wretched craving, this attachment to the world, their sorrow grows, like grass in the rain."

Dhp 336: "But whoever prevails over this wretched craving, so hard to get over in the world, their sorrows fall from them, like a drop from a lotus-leaf."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The verses are about craving in general and say it is "so hard to get over", which is a gentle word for the difficulty. Reading them as effects of addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Philosophical

Aristotle describes what follows the act. Nicomachean Ethics 7.8: "incontinent man is likely to repent." He draws a contrast with the self-indulgent man, who "stands by his choice".

He also says: "the incontinent man curable".

Plato's picture gives the cost of the struggle: "the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Aristotle's "curable" is about weakness of will and not a medical claim, and the passage compares the two kinds of person in terms the site does not adopt. Reading these as effects of addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics, Book VII (external link), 7.8 (W. D. Ross's translation); Plato, Phaedrus (external link) (Benjamin Jowett's translation)

The Greek was not examined.
