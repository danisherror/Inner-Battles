Addiction and compulsion

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here offer accounts of why it happens, and they disagree.

  • The brain's response to repeated use. Leshner: addiction "results from the prolonged effects of drugs on the brain". Lewis: "the salience of drug-related cues is enhanced by dopamine activity in the ventral striatum, orbitofrontal cortex, and amygdala, due to the intense hedonic impact of repeated drug experiences", and "dopamine-based craving peaks when drug (or alcohol or gambling) rewards become available, in the moment, and this rapid increase in attractiveness preempts rational judgment."
  • Choice, with the odds tilted. Lewis calls addiction "a recurrent series of choices that permit negotiation, and sometimes cooperation, between immediate and long-range goals."
  • A challenge to the dopamine account. Heyman writes that "there is more to the biology of addiction than dopamine", and cites an experiment in which "rats readily gave up cocaine for saccharin, even when they had been consuming massive amounts of the drug for weeks."

Common causes, as people describe them and not findings from these abstracts: stress, trauma, loneliness, easy access, and family history. None of this makes anyone to blame. Struggling to stop is common, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If you are finding it hard, please talk to a doctor, therapist or a local support service. Stopping some substances suddenly, including alcohol and certain medicines, can be dangerous, so ask a doctor before stopping on your own (a standard safety point, not drawn from the papers below). If you are in danger, or thinking of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services now; the International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Leshner (1997), "Addiction is a brain disease, and it matters," Science 278(5335), 45-47. doi:10.1126/science.278.5335.45 (external link)
  • Lewis (2011), "Dopamine and the neural 'now': essay and review of Addiction: A Disorder of Choice," Perspectives on Psychological Science 6(2), 150-155. doi:10.1177/1745691611400235 (external link)
  • Heyman (2011), "Received wisdom regarding the roles of craving and dopamine in addiction: a response to Lewis's critique of Addiction: A Disorder of Choice," Perspectives on Psychological Science 6(2), 156-160. doi:10.1177/1745691611400243 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

All three papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The last paragraph is common description and cites no study. The abstracts are about drugs and alcohol, and the dispute they show is not settled.

Hinduism

The Gita describes a chain that starts with dwelling on the object. 2.62: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger."

2.63 continues: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The chain is a general account of how desire grows and ends in anger and confusion, and it does not mention substances or a medical cause. Reading it as a root of compulsion is ours. The word "ruined" is the translator's, and it should not be taken as a verdict on a person. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Upanishads

The Katha puts the cause in who is holding the reins. 1.3.3-4: "Know the Self to be sitting in the chariot, the body to be the chariot, the intellect (buddhi) the charioteer, and the mind the reins. The senses they call the horses, the objects of the senses their roads."

1.3.5: "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The image is of the person as a whole and is not an account of the causes of a condition. Reading it as a root of compulsion is ours, and it should not be used to say that people who struggle lack understanding. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Christianity

Paul describes a split between wanting and doing. Romans 7:18-19: "For I know that the good does not dwell within me, that is, in my flesh. For the desire to do the good lies close at hand, but not the ability."

And 7:23: "but I see in my members another law at war with the law of my mind, making me captive to the law of sin that dwells in my members."

In 1 Corinthians 6:12: "'All things are permitted for me,' but I will not be dominated by anything."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. The passages are about sin and the flesh, and the cause they name is a divided self. Reading them as roots of compulsion is ours, and the site does not treat addiction as a sin. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name craving as the origin. SN 56.11: "It's the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights. That is, craving for sensual pleasures, craving for continued existence, and craving for nonexistence."

Dhp 334 adds heedlessness: "When a man lives heedlessly, craving grows in them like a camel's foot creeper."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Craving here is the root of suffering for everyone, and the texts do not discuss addiction as a condition. Reading them as a root of compulsion is ours, and it should not be read as saying that people with an addiction have caused it by wanting. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Philosophical

Plato locates the trouble in the mixed nature of the soul. Phaedrus: "the human charioteer drives his in a pair; and one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed; and the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him."

Aristotle says who is most at risk of acting before thinking: "It is keen and excitable people that suffer especially from the impetuous form of incontinence; for the former by reason of their quickness and the latter by reason of the violence of their passions do not await the argument, because they are apt to follow their imagination."

This is related to addiction and not identical to it. Plato's image is of the soul and Aristotle's of weakness of will, and neither is about dependence on a substance. Reading them as roots of addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.
