Addiction and compulsion

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how a compulsion works and how people loosen it.

Dev's story

Dev started betting on his phone for fun. Within a year he was betting most evenings, chasing losses, and moving money between accounts so his partner would not see. Each time he said it would be the last.

What helped:

  1. He told someone. After a bad night he told his sister everything. She did not shout, and she stayed on the phone.
  2. He saw a doctor. The doctor asked about his sleep, mood and money, and put him in touch with a counsellor and a local support group.
  3. He used support. The group met weekly, and it helped to hear that others had slipped and kept going.
  4. He changed the setting. He blocked the betting sites, handed his partner a say over his accounts, and deleted the apps.
  5. He rode out urges. When the urge rose, he rang someone or went for a walk, and noticed it passing.
  6. He treated a slip as information. In the fourth month he placed a bet, and told his counsellor the next day. They worked out what had set it off.

It took many months, and the habit did not vanish all at once.

What it shows: Dev did not beat it by willpower alone. Telling people, getting support and changing the setting made the urge easier to ride out, and a slip became something to learn from and not a reason to hide.

Hinduism

The taste that remains

Gita 2.59 speaks of someone who has restrained the senses: "Aspirants may restrain the senses from their objects of enjoyment, but the taste for the sense objects remains. However, even this taste ceases for those who realize the Supreme."

The commentary explains: "When one gives up eating, as in a fast, the desires of the senses become feeble. Similarly, in sickness one loses interest toward the objects of enjoyment. These states of dispassion are temporary, for the seed of desire remains within the mind."

What it shows: The text expects the taste to remain after the act is stopped, and does not treat that as failure. This is a picture drawn from one verse and its commentary and not a tale of a person, and linking it to addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Upanishads

Nachiketa and Death

In the Katha Upanishad the boy Nachiketa asks Death a question about what lies beyond. Death tries to turn him aside: "Choose sons and grandsons who shall live a hundred years, herds of cattle, elephants, gold, and horses." "Whatever desires are difficult to attain among mortals, ask for them according to thy wish".

Nachiketa answers: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses. Even the whole of life is short. Keep thou thy horses, keep dance and song for thyself." "No man can be made happy by wealth."

Death then says: "The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man." And of Nachiketa: "Thou, O Nachiketas, after pondering all pleasures that are or seem delightful, hast dismissed them all."

What it shows: Nachiketa is offered the pleasant in full, and turns it down for what he asked. He is a boy making a choice in a teaching and not a person with an addiction, and linking the two is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Christianity

The one who seeks another drink

Proverbs 23 gives a picture and not a tale: "Who has woe? Who has sorrow? Who has strife? Who has complaining? Who has wounds without cause? Who has redness of eyes? Those who linger late over wine".

The last verse gives the voice: "'They struck me,' you will say, 'but I was not hurt; they beat me, but I did not feel it. When shall I awake? I will seek another drink.'"

Paul's cry in Romans 7 answers it from another side: "Wretched person that I am! Who will rescue me from this body of death? Thanks be to God through Jesus Christ our Lord!"

What it shows: The proverb shows how the pull sounds from inside, and the second text shows a cry for help that is answered with thanks. They are two texts about different things, and linking them to addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Buddhism

The creeper, the langur and the reed

Dhp 334-337 gives three pictures of craving. Heedlessness grows it "like a camel's foot creeper", and they "jump from one thing to the next, like a langur greedy for fruit in a forest grove."

The sorrow it brings grows "like grass in the rain" (335), and when someone prevails, "their sorrows fall from them, like a drop from a lotus-leaf" (336).

The teacher then says: "dig up the root of craving, as you'd dig up grass in search of roots. Don't let Māra break you again and again, like a stream breaking a reed." (337)

What it shows: Craving is pictured as growing, jumping and returning, and the way out as digging at the root, and the verse expects the stream to break against the reed more than once. These are similes and not a tale of a person, and linking them to addiction is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.

Philosophical

The charioteer and the two horses

In Plato's Phaedrus Socrates gives a picture of the soul: "a pair of winged horses and a charioteer." The gods' horses "are all of them noble and of noble descent, but those of other races are mixed; the human charioteer drives his in a pair; and one of them is noble and of noble breed, and the other is ignoble and of ignoble breed; and the driving of them of necessity gives a great deal of trouble to him."

What it shows: The struggle is built into the picture of being human, and the charioteer's trouble is not a sign of failure. It is an image in a dialogue and not a tale of a person, and linking it to addiction is ours. "Ignoble" is the text's word for one horse and should not be read as a word for any person. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and a doctor can help with stopping safely.
