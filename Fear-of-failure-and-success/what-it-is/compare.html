Inner battle

Fear of failure and success

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story
Lenses to show

Modern

Fear of failure is dread of not succeeding, and of what that would cost you or say about you. Fear of success is less obvious: it means associating success with negative consequences, such as more pressure, envy or change. Both can lead to the same place, which is staying out of the attempt.

Researchers have studied both. Kosakowska-Berezecka and colleagues asked students to complete scales measuring "their associations with success (fear of success) and tendency for self-promotion", and note that avoiding self-promotion "is also potentially related to associating success with negative consequences".

Fear of failure is not only harmful. Boileau and colleagues developed a scale because existing measures "typically overlook" its motivational role "in favor of its negative effects". Teevan and McGhee's 1972 paper, "Childhood development of fear of failure motivation", is an early study of how it forms.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: putting off an application, starting many things and finishing few, turning down the promotion, or undermining your own work the night before it is due. See also perfectionism and procrastination.

This is not a diagnosis. If the fear comes with constant anxiety or low mood, a doctor or therapist can help.

  • Boileau et al. (2025), "Fear of failure as motivation: a novel conceptualization and measure," Journal of Personality Assessment 108(2), 235-249. doi:10.1080/00223891.2025.2555358 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for fear of failure or of success, but the Gita speaks of treating them alike. Gita 2.38: "Fight for the sake of duty, treating alike happiness and distress, loss and gain, victory and defeat."

Gita 4.22 describes the person "content with whatever gain comes of its own accord, and free from envy", who is "equipoised in success and failure" and so "not bound by their actions, even while performing all kinds of activities".

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The Gita is describing equanimity toward results, and it does not describe the dread of the attempt. Reading it as speaking to that dread is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name fear of failure or of success, and the closest they come is the idea of acting without clinging to the result. Isha Upanishad 2: "Though a man may wish to live a hundred years, performing works, it will be thus with him; but not in any other way: work will thus not cling to a man."

The verse follows the instruction to give up the sense of ownership (Isha 1) and then to act: work does not cling to the one who acts in this way.

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The verse is about action and non-attachment, and does not describe fear of the attempt. Reading it as speaking to that fear is ours.

Christianity

The Bible does not name fear of failure, but it speaks of falling and rising. Proverbs 24:16: "for though they fall seven times, they will rise again, but the wicked are overthrown by calamity."

And Paul writes of weakness: "My grace is sufficient for you, for power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9).

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The verses speak of falling and of weakness, and not of the dread that stops a person trying. Reading them as speaking to that dread is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts do not name fear of failure, but they name the pairs that success and failure belong to. AN 8.6: "the eight worldly conditions revolve around the world, and the world revolves around the eight worldly conditions. What eight? Gain and loss, fame and disgrace, blame and praise, pleasure and pain."

The sutta says everyone meets them: "An unlearned ordinary person encounters gain and loss, fame and disgrace, blame and praise, and pleasure and pain. And so does a learned noble disciple."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The sutta is about how we meet gain and loss, praise and blame, and not about dread of the attempt. Reading success and failure as gain and loss is ours.

Philosophical

Aristotle names a version of fear of success: claiming less than you deserve. In the Nicomachean Ethics (4.3): "The man who thinks himself worthy of worthy of less than he is really worthy of is unduly humble, whether his deserts be great or moderate".

He adds: "And the man whose deserts are great would seem most unduly humble; for what would he have done if they had been less?"

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. Aristotle is speaking of honour and of rating oneself rightly, and not of fear. Reading undue humility as one face of fear of success is ours.
