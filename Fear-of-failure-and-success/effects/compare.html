Inner battle

Fear of failure and success

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here is small, mostly in athletes and students, and correlational.

  • Burnout. Yıldırım and colleagues studied 335 young athletes and found that "fear of failure had significant predictive effects on resilience, extrinsic motivation, and burnout". Resilience and extrinsic motivation "partially mediated" the link, and the authors suggest that the harm "can be mitigated by cultivating resilience and hindering extrinsic motivation".
  • Lower expectations. In Kosakowska-Berezecka and colleagues' student sample, "Both tendency for self-promotion and fear of success are related to anticipation of success in test performance and expectations concerning future earnings".
  • Not only harm. Boileau and colleagues argue that fear of failure also has "positive influences on goal pursuit and achievement".

These are self-reported questionnaires in particular groups. They do not show that the fear causes burnout or lower expectations.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: avoiding chances, self-sabotage, putting things off, and relief that is followed by regret. If the fear comes with constant anxiety or low mood, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Yıldırım et al. (2023), "Resilience and extrinsic motivation as mediators in the relationship between fear of failure and burnout," International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 20(10), 5895. doi:10.3390/ijerph20105895 (external link)
  • Kosakowska-Berezecka et al. (2017), Frontiers in Psychology 8, 1884. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01884 (external link)
  • Boileau et al. (2025), Journal of Personality Assessment 108(2), 235-249. doi:10.1080/00223891.2025.2555358 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita shows what follows when a person stops. Arjuna, facing the battle, says "Govind, I shall not fight," and "became silent" (2.9).

Gita 18.8 names a related pattern: "To give up prescribed duties because they are troublesome or cause bodily discomfort is renunciation in the mode of passion. Such renunciation is never beneficial or elevating."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. Arjuna's refusal comes from grief at the sight of his kinsmen, and 18.8 names trouble and discomfort and does not mention fear. Reading them as the effect of fear of failure is ours.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.9 (external link), 18.8 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The words "Govind, I shall not fight" are Arjuna's, reported by Sanjay in the verse, and "became silent" is the verse's wording.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad says what choosing the pleasant costs. Katha 1.2.1: "It is well with him who clings to the good; he who chooses the pleasant, misses his end."

And 1.2.4: "Wide apart and leading to different points are these two, ignorance, and what is known as wisdom."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The verses speak of the end of human life and the two roads to it, and do not mention fear, avoidance or achievement. Reading them as the effect of staying with the safe choice is ours.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.2.1, 1.2.4 (Max Müller's translation)

Quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. Müller's notes were not read.

Christianity

The parable of the talents shows what the fear costs. The two servants who traded with their money are told: "Well done, good and trustworthy slave; you have been trustworthy in a few things; I will put you in charge of many things; enter into the joy of your master" (Matthew 25:21, 23).

The servant who buried his talent returns it unchanged, and the master says: "So take the talent from him, and give it to the one with the ten talents" (25:28).

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The parable's judgement on the third servant is severe, and it should not be read as a threat to anyone who is afraid. It shows a talent that gained nothing because it was hidden, and that reading is ours.

Sources

  • Matthew 25:14-30 (external link)

The quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway on 2026-11-14. The master's words to the third servant in 25:26 and 25:30 are harsher than those quoted and were not used here. The caution about reading the parable as a threat is ours.

Buddhism

AN 8.6 says what favouring and opposing does to the mind: "So gain and loss, fame and disgrace, blame and praise, and pleasure and pain occupy their mind."

And: "Being so full of favoring and opposing, they're not freed from rebirth, old age, and death, from sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress."

In MN 36 the Buddha describes the cost of straining too hard before his awakening: "My energy was roused up and unflagging, and my mindfulness was established and lucid, but my body was disturbed, not tranquil, because I'd pushed too hard with that painful striving."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. AN 8.6 is about favouring and opposing, and MN 36 about extreme austerity; neither is about fear. Reading them as effects of the fear is ours.

Sources

  • AN 8.6 (external link)
  • MN 36 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-14. The MN 36 sentence is repeated in the sutta for several kinds of austerity and is quoted from the first of them (20.7).

Philosophical

Aristotle describes what undue humility costs. Nicomachean Ethics 4.3: "the unduly humble man, being worthy of good things, robs himself of what he deserves".

He adds that such a person "seems also not to know himself".

Epictetus describes what follows when we put our weight on things outside our control. Enchiridion 1: "then you will be hindered. You will lament, you will be disturbed, and you will find fault both with gods and men."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. Aristotle is speaking of a person who rates themselves too low, and Epictetus of disturbance in general; neither is a study of what fear of failure does. Reading them together is ours.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The first Aristotle quotation begins mid-sentence ("For the unduly humble man..."). The Epictetus page names Elizabeth Carter, but the exact edition was not established.
