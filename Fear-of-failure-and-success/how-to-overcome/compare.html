Inner battle

Fear of failure and success

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here is about what goes with fear of failure, and it does not test a treatment, so what follows is mostly common advice.

  1. Name what you fear will happen. "I will look foolish" and "I will be noticed and then fall" are different fears.
  2. Shrink the attempt. Pick a version small enough to finish, and finish it.
  3. Write down what success and failure would each mean. Include what you would learn from the second.
  4. For fear of success, list the feared costs. Check each one: is it likely, and what would you do about it?
  5. Separate your worth from the result. Boileau and colleagues argue that fear of failure can also push people to try. It can be treated as information about what matters to you, and it does not have to be a verdict.
  6. Look at who is judging. Moreno-Murcia and colleagues found that "coach autonomy support was associated with reduced fear of failure". That is an association, but it suggests asking for feedback that helps you decide, and not only for judgement.
  7. Build resilience. Yıldırım and colleagues suggest the harm "can be mitigated by cultivating resilience and hindering extrinsic motivation". That is the authors' suggestion from a correlational study.
  8. Get help if it is constant. A doctor or therapist can help when the fear comes with constant anxiety, low mood or a pattern you cannot break.

Sources

  • Boileau et al. (2025), Journal of Personality Assessment 108(2), 235-249. doi:10.1080/00223891.2025.2555358 (external link)
  • Moreno-Murcia et al. (2019), International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 16(9), 1563. doi:10.3390/ijerph16091563 (external link)
  • Yıldırım et al. (2023), International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 20(10), 5895. doi:10.3390/ijerph20105895 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. None tested a way of reducing fear of failure. Steps 1 to 4 and 8 are common advice, not tied to a study read here; step 5 is our reading of Boileau et al.

Hinduism

The Gita points to doing the work and loosening the grip on the result.

  1. Do the duty, not the outcome. Gita 2.47: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions."
  2. Do not slide into inaction. The same verse adds: "nor be attached to inaction."
  3. Stay level. Gita 2.48: "Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog."
  4. Do not be thrown by loss or gain. Gita 12.17 describes those "who neither lament for any loss nor hanker for any gain".

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The verses are about equanimity in action in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant anxiety or low mood. Reading them as advice for the dread of an attempt is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to acting with a loosened grip, and to choosing what matters.

  1. Let go of ownership, then act. Isha 1: "When thou hast surrendered all this, then thou mayest enjoy." Isha 2: "work will thus not cling to a man."
  2. Choose the good over the pleasant. Katha 1.2.2: "the wise prefers the good to the pleasant".
  3. Do not be pulled away. Of Nachiketas, Death says: "even many pleasures did not tear thee away" (Katha 1.2.4).

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. These verses are about non-attachment and choosing the good, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant anxiety or low mood. Reading them as advice for the dread of an attempt is ours.

Christianity

The Bible's answer to failure is to rise, to be held, and to press on.

  1. Get up. Proverbs 24:16: "for though they fall seven times, they will rise again".
  2. Hold on to being held. Psalm 37:24: "though we stumble, we shall not fall headlong, for the Lord holds us by the hand."
  3. Speak to your accuser. Micah 7:8: "Do not rejoice over me, my enemies; when I fall, I shall rise; when I sit in darkness, the Lord will be a light to me."
  4. Do not stay with what is behind. Philippians 3:13-14: "forgetting what lies behind and straining forward to what lies ahead, I press on toward the goal".
  5. Let weakness be. 2 Corinthians 12:9: "power is made perfect in weakness."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. These verses are about falling and rising in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant anxiety or low mood.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts teach looking at gain and loss as passing, and not siding with either.

  1. Reflect. AN 8.6: "They reflect: 'I've encountered this gain. It's impermanent, suffering, and perishable.' They truly understand it." The sutta says the same of loss, fame, disgrace, blame, praise, pleasure and pain.
  2. Do not favour or oppose. "They don't favor gain or oppose loss. They don't favor fame or oppose disgrace. They don't favor praise or oppose blame."
  3. Stay steady. The verses of AN 8.5 say of the wise: "Desirable things don't disturb their mind, nor are they repelled by the undesirable."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The suttas are about meeting gain and loss in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant anxiety or low mood. Reading them as advice for the dread of an attempt is ours.

Philosophical

The Stoics' advice is to take on contests you can win by your own effort, and to make the obstacle part of the work.

  1. Choose contests you control. Epictetus, Enchiridion 19: "You may be unconquerable, if you enter into no combat in which it is not in your own control to conquer."
  2. Wish for what happens. Enchiridion 8: "Don't demand that things happen as you wish, but wish that they happen as they do happen, and you will go on well."
  3. Turn the hindrance into help. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5: "the mind converts and changes every hindrance to its activity into an aid; and so that which is a hindrance is made a furtherance to an act; and that which is an obstacle on the road helps us on this road."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. These are practices for effort and obstacles in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant anxiety or low mood. Epictetus's "unconquerable" is about not entering contests you cannot win, and it is not advice to avoid every risk.

  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)
