Inner battle

Fear of failure and success

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read for this article touch two possible roots, and neither is a full account.

  • A controlling environment. Moreno-Murcia and colleagues studied 340 athletes and found "a significant and positive relationship between coaches' controlling style and athletes' fear of failure whereas coach autonomy support was associated with reduced fear of failure". Hu and colleagues, with 252 athletes, describe a controlling coaching style as "a key predictor of athletes' fear of failure". These are questionnaire associations in athletes and do not show cause.
  • Expecting success to cost something. Kosakowska-Berezecka and colleagues found that, among university students, "in comparison to men, women expect lower salaries in the future, anticipate lower test performance and associate success with more negative consequences". Among younger pupils "girls and boys in high school do not differ with regard to fear of success". The authors' model suggests that women "insufficiently self-promote because they fear backlash for behavior which is incongruent with traditional gender roles".

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: shame after an earlier failure, praise that depends on results, and a belief that your worth is measured by what you achieve.

Sources

  • Moreno-Murcia et al. (2019), "Coaches' motivational style and athletes' fear of failure," International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health 16(9), 1563. doi:10.3390/ijerph16091563 (external link)
  • Hu et al. (2023), "Impact of a controlling coaching style on athletes' fear of failure," Frontiers in Psychology 14, 1106916. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1106916 (external link)
  • Kosakowska-Berezecka et al. (2017), Frontiers in Psychology 8, 1884. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.01884 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The abstract of Kosakowska-Berezecka et al. continues past the sentence quoted for the high school sample, and the rest of it was not read. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita places the root in being tied to results. Gita 2.47: "You have a right to perform your prescribed duties, but you are not entitled to the fruits of your actions. Never consider yourself to be the cause of the results of your activities, nor be attached to inaction."

Gita 2.49 goes further: "discard reward-seeking actions that are certainly inferior to works performed with the intellect established in divine knowledge. Miserly are those who seek to enjoy the fruits of their works."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The Gita is speaking of attachment to the fruits of action in general. Reading that attachment as the root of the fear of failing, or of succeeding, is ours.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad separates the good from the pleasant. Death says to Nachiketas (1.2.1): "The good is one thing, the pleasant another; these two, having different objects, chain a man."

And (1.2.2): "The good and the pleasant approach man: the wise goes round about them and distinguishes them. Yea, the wise prefers the good to the pleasant, but the fool chooses the pleasant through greed and avarice."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The passage is about choosing between the good and the pleasant, and not about fear. Reading the pull toward the safe and comfortable as one form of "the pleasant" is ours.

Christianity

In the parable of the talents the servant who hid his money gives a reason. Matthew 25:24-25: "Master, I knew that you were a harsh man, reaping where you did not sow and gathering where you did not scatter, so I was afraid, and I went and hid your talent in the ground. Here you have what is yours."

His fear rests on a picture of the one he serves, as harsh and sure to punish a loss. The master's reply begins: "You knew, did you, that I reap where I did not sow and gather where I did not scatter?" (25:26).

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The parable is about stewardship, and the servant's fear is of his master. Reading it as a picture of how fear of failure grows is ours, and the master's picture of himself may differ from the servant's.

Buddhism

AN 8.6 places the root in how the unlearned person meets gain and loss. They do not reflect: "'I've encountered this gain. It's impermanent, suffering, and perishable.' They don't truly understand it."

And so they take sides: "They favor gain and oppose loss. They favor fame and oppose disgrace. They favor praise and oppose blame. They favor pleasure and oppose pain."

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. The sutta is about favouring and opposing in general. Reading the opposing of loss and disgrace as the root of fear of failure is ours.

Philosophical

Epictetus puts the root in tying our worth to things we do not control. Enchiridion 1: "Things in our control are opinion, pursuit, desire, aversion, and, in a word, whatever are our own actions. Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions."

And in 19, on how people look to us: "When, therefore, you see anyone eminent in honors, or power, or in high esteem on any other account, take heed not to be hurried away with the appearance, and to pronounce him happy".

This is related to fear of failure and success and not identical to it. Epictetus is speaking of what is and is not up to us, and he does not describe fear of the attempt. Reading reputation as the thing that fear of failure protects is ours.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 and 19 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The page names Elizabeth Carter, but the exact edition was not established.
