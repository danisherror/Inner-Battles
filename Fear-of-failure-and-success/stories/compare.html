Fear of failure and success

A story, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how these fears work and how people loosen their grip.

Jonas's story

Jonas, a freelance designer, had a portfolio people praised. When a large company offered him a six-month project, he said he would think about it, then let the deadline pass.

He told himself he was busy. In truth two thoughts were taking turns. One was "I will let them down and everyone will see". The other was less welcome: "If it goes well I will be expected to do this every time, and I will have to charge more, and some people will resent it."

What helped:

  1. He named both fears. Written side by side they looked different: one about falling short, one about what would follow success.
  2. He shrank the attempt. He wrote to the company and asked for a two-week trial phase.
  3. He listed what each outcome would mean. A failed trial would cost him two weeks and teach him what the company needed. A good one would mean a harder conversation about pricing, which he could prepare for.
  4. He asked for feedback that helped him decide. "What would make the first deliverable useful to you?" replaced "is it good enough?"
  5. He noticed it was not lifting. When the dread spread to every new client and cost him sleep, he spoke to his doctor, who referred him to a counsellor.

The fear did not vanish. It got smaller, and the trial phase went ahead.

What it shows: Jonas was afraid of two different things, and each needed its own answer. Shrinking the attempt and asking what each outcome would mean did more than waiting to feel ready.

Hinduism

Arjuna lays down his bow

At the start of the Gita Arjuna cannot decide what to do. He tells Krishna: "We do not even know which result of this war is preferable for us—conquering them or being conquered by them" (2.6). Then, "Having thus spoken," he says: "Govind, I shall not fight," and falls silent (2.9).

Krishna's reply runs through the chapter. One part is: "Fight for the sake of duty, treating alike happiness and distress, loss and gain, victory and defeat" (2.38). Another: "Be steadfast in the performance of your duty, O Arjun, abandoning attachment to success and failure. Such equanimity is called Yog" (2.48).

What it shows: Arjuna is stopped by not knowing which outcome would be better, and Krishna's answer is not to promise a result but to change what he is holding on to. The Gita is about duty in war and not about fear of failure as such; the story is read here for how the question of outcome is answered.

Upanishads

Nachiketas and the offered riches

In the Katha Upanishad, Nachiketas has asked Death what lies after dying. Death tries to turn him aside with everything a person might want: "Choose sons and grandsons who shall live a hundred years, herds of cattle, elephants, gold, and horses. Choose the wide abode of the earth, and live thyself as many harvests as thou desirest" (1.1.23). He adds: "be waited on by them whom I give to thee, but do not ask me about dying" (1.1.25).

Nachiketas answers: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses. Even the whole of life is short. Keep thou thy horses, keep dance and song for thyself. No man can be made happy by wealth" (1.1.26-27). He says again that he wants only the answer he asked for.

What it shows: Nachiketas is offered every kind of worldly success and turns it down for what he came to learn. It is a story about desire and knowledge, and not about fear of success; the link is ours, and it shows a person who is not carried off by what success would bring.

Christianity

Elijah after the victory

In 1 Kings 19 Elijah has just done what he set out to do. Ahab tells his queen "all that Elijah had done and how he had killed all the prophets with the sword." Jezebel sends a message: "So may the gods do to me and more also, if I do not make your life like the life of one of them by this time tomorrow" (19:1-2).

"Then he was afraid; he got up and fled for his life." He goes a day's journey into the wilderness and sits under a broom tree. "He asked that he might die, 'It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life, for I am no better than my ancestors'" (19:3-4).

He lies down and sleeps. An angel touches him and says: "Get up and eat." There is "a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water". The angel comes a second time: "Get up and eat, or the journey will be too much for you." He eats, and "went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb the mount of God" (19:5-8).

What it shows: Elijah's fear comes right after a victory, and what he is given first is food and sleep before any argument. The story is about a prophet and a threat and not about fear of success as such; the link is ours, in that success did not bring him peace.

Buddhism

The Buddha changes course

In MN 36 the Buddha tells of the years before his awakening. After severe austerities he thinks: "this is as far as it goes, no-one has done more than this. But I have not achieved any superhuman distinction in knowledge and vision worthy of the noble ones by this severe, grueling work. Could there be another path to awakening?"

He remembers a pleasant meditation in childhood under a tree. Then the fear: "Why am I afraid of that pleasure, for it has nothing to do with sensual pleasures or unskillful qualities?" He answers himself: "I'm not afraid of that pleasure". He sees he will need strength: "I can't achieve that pleasure with a body so severely emaciated. Why don't I eat some solid food, some rice and porridge?"

The cost comes at once. The five mendicants who had been attending him "left disappointed in me, saying, 'The ascetic Gotama has become indulgent; he has strayed from the struggle and returned to indulgence.'"

What it shows: The Buddha counts the failure of one method, asks whether he fears the better one, and changes course although others think less of him for it. The sutta is about the path to awakening and not about fear of failure or success; the link is ours.

Philosophical

Marcus Aurelius gets out of bed

Marcus Aurelius is not telling a story in Meditations 5, but the opening is a scene. "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present- I am rising to the work of a human being. Why then am I dissatisfied if I am going to do the things for which I exist and for which I was brought into the world?"

He argues with the voice that wants to stay under the covers. "Or have I been made for this, to lie in the bed-clothes and keep myself warm?- But this is more pleasant.- Dost thou exist then to take thy pleasure, and not at all for action or exertion?"

What it shows: Marcus does not wait to feel ready; he reminds himself what the work is for and gets up. The passage is about rising and duty and not about fear of failure or success; the link is ours, and it is that action begins before the feeling does.
