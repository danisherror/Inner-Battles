Self-doubt

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Bravata and colleagues' review links impostor syndrome with several costs. It is "often comorbid with depression and anxiety and is associated with impaired job performance, job satisfaction, and burnout among various employee populations including clinicians". The authors add that studies were heterogeneous and that publication bias may be present, and the abstract gives no cause and effect.

Commonly described effects, which are not findings from that review:

  • Holding back. Not applying, not speaking up, or turning down a chance because you expect to be found out.
  • Over-working to hide the gap. Preparing far past the point of use, which feeds the cycle (see Perfectionism).
  • Discounting praise. Success is put down to luck, so it never raises confidence.

If self-doubt comes with low mood, constant anxiety or exhaustion, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Bravata et al. (2020), Journal of General Internal Medicine 35(4), 1252-1275. doi:10.1007/s11606-019-05364-1 (external link)

The quotation was read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full paper. The bulleted effects cite no study.

Hinduism

The Gita shows the effects at the moment of collapse and in the verses that follow.

  • Paralysis. Arjuna cannot act: he is "besieged with anxiety and faintheartedness" and asks to be told what is best (2.7).
  • Yielding. Krishna's first words name the pull: "it does not befit you to yield to this unmanliness" (2.3).
  • A doubting nature. 4.40: "persons who possess neither faith nor knowledge, and who are of a doubting nature, suffer a downfall. For the skeptical souls, there is no happiness either in this world or the next." This verse is about doubt of the teaching, not self-doubt, and is quoted for what the Gita says doubt costs.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.3 (external link), 2.7 (external link), 4.40 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The reading of Arjuna's state as an effect of self-doubt is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not list effects of self-doubt. The Mundaka says what stops a seeker: "Nor is that Self to be gained by one who is destitute of strength, or without earnestness, or without right meditation" (3.2.4). A person who gives up before starting has, in this sense, lost strength and earnestness.

The Katha shows the opposite effect in Nachiketas. Death offers him sons, cattle, gold and long life if he will stop asking his question, and he answers: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses" (1.1.26). He does not give up the question.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. The first passage is about the seeker's strength, and the second about steadiness in the face of temptation. Setting them against self-doubt is a reading.

Christianity

The Bible shows hesitation, fear and a plea for someone else, and it shows the answer.

  • Hesitation. Moses objects "I am slow of speech and slow of tongue" (Exodus 4:10), and Jeremiah says "Truly I do not know how to speak, for I am only a boy" (Jeremiah 1:6).
  • Fear. The reply to Jeremiah is "Do not be afraid of them, for I am with you to deliver you" (1:8), and Isaiah 41:10 says "do not fear, for I am with you".
  • Strength. Paul writes: "I can do all things through him who strengthens me" (Philippians 4:13).

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. The people in these texts hesitate over a calling, and the texts do not describe impostor feelings or their effects on work.

Sources

  • Exodus 4:10-12 (external link), Jeremiah 1:6-8 (external link), Isaiah 41:10 (external link), Philippians 4:13 (external link)

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts do not describe the effects of self-doubt. SN 46.51 says how doubt in general behaves: it can "increase and grow" once it has arisen. That is what many people report of self-doubt, that it feeds on itself.

Dhp 160 points to what is lost: "One is indeed the lord of oneself, for who else would be one's lord?" A person who hands the verdict to a doubting voice has, in this sense, given up being the lord of themselves. That is a reading.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it.

Philosophical

Aristotle describes what the unduly humble person loses. In Nicomachean Ethics 4.3 he says such a person "robs himself of what he deserves", and that "these people stand back even from noble actions and undertakings, deeming themselves unworthy". He adds that they are "not thought to be fools, but rather unduly retiring", and that the reputation makes them worse.

Marcus Aurelius names a quieter effect, the loss of purpose in the morning: "Why then am I dissatisfied if I am going to do the things for which I exist and for which I was brought into the world?" (Meditations 5).

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. Aristotle writes about a trait of character and Marcus about reluctance; neither describes impostor feelings.

  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)
