Lenses to show

Modern

Research on impostor feelings is still uneven, so the roots below are the ones commonly proposed, with the little the sources say.

  • Where it shows up. Bravata and colleagues found impostor feelings "particularly high among ethnic minority groups" and common across ages, from adolescents to late-stage professionals. The abstract does not explain why.
  • Measurement. Prevalence ranged from 9 to 82% depending on the tool and cut-off, so part of what looks like a cause may be how it is counted.
  • Link with other states. The review reports that impostor syndrome is often comorbid with depression and anxiety. The abstract does not say which comes first.
  • Commonly proposed roots. Early messages that worth depends on achievement, high standards (see Perfectionism), being the first or only one of your group in a setting, and attributing success to luck are all frequently given. These are common explanations and not findings from the paper read here.

Sources

  • Bravata et al. (2020), Journal of General Internal Medicine 35(4), 1252-1275. doi:10.1007/s11606-019-05364-1 (external link)

The findings were read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full paper. The final bullet cites no study. The paper says its studies were heterogeneous and that publication bias may be present.

Hinduism

The Gita traces Arjuna's collapse to confusion about duty and to the mind turned against its owner.

  • Confusion and anxiety. Arjuna's own account in 2.7: "I am confused about my duty, and am besieged with anxiety and faintheartedness."
  • The mind as enemy. 6.6: "For those who have conquered the mind, it is their friend. For those who have failed to do so, the mind works like an enemy." A mind that argues against us is the root in view.
  • Doubt without faith or knowledge. 4.40 says that "persons who possess neither faith nor knowledge, and who are of a doubting nature, suffer a downfall". The doubt is about the teaching, and the verse is not about self-doubt.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. The verses speak of duty, the mind and faith, and the roots listed are our reading.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.7 (external link), 6.6 (external link), 4.40 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. That 4.40 concerns doubt about the teaching and not about oneself is our reading of the context, which was not checked in the surrounding verses.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not describe where self-doubt comes from. Two passages suggest a root: measuring ourselves by learning, and forgetting what the Self is.

  • Measuring by learning. Mundaka 3.2.3: "That Self cannot be gained by the Veda, nor by understanding, nor by much learning." If we treat knowledge or cleverness as the measure of a person, we will always find ourselves short.
  • Lacking strength and earnestness. 3.2.4 names "destitute of strength, or without earnestness, or without right meditation" as the causes of failure to reach the Self. Doubt is not named, but a person who gives up through feeling unfit lacks strength in this sense. That is a reading.
  • Standing on achievement. In the Chandogya (6.1.2), Svetaketu comes home "conceited, considering himself well-read", and his father shows him what he lacks. Standing built on attainment is exposed when someone asks a question we cannot answer.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. These passages are read for the roots they point to and are not about impostor feelings.

Sources

  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) 3.2.3-4 (Max Müller's translation)
  • Chandogya Upanishad, sixth Prapâthaka (external link) 1.1-3 (Müller)

Quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. The links drawn between these verses and self-doubt are ours.

Christianity

The Bible places the root in looking at oneself alone, and says what the doubters of the Bible were told to look at instead.

  • Comparing self with task. Moses asks "Who am I?" (Exodus 3:11), Gideon says "I am the least in my family" (Judges 6:15), and Jeremiah says "I am only a boy" (Jeremiah 1:6). Each measures themselves against the task and finds themselves short.
  • Where adequacy comes from. Paul writes: "Not that we are qualified of ourselves to claim anything as coming from us; our qualification is from God" (2 Corinthians 3:5). The text is saying that the standard of being qualified of ourselves is not the right one.
  • Grace. "By the grace of God I am what I am, and his grace toward me has not been in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:10).

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. The passages are about calling and grace, and reading them for the roots of self-doubt is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts do not trace self-doubt itself, but SN 46.51 says how doubt in general is fed.

  • Grounds for doubt. "And what fuels the arising of doubt, or, when it has arisen, makes it increase and grow? There are things that are grounds for doubt." Doubt grows on what we give it to grow on.
  • Not knowing the self. Dhp 157: "If you knew your self as beloved, you'd look after it so well." The verse implies that failing to care for the self comes from not knowing it as dear, and that is a reading.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. The hindrance of doubt is mainly about the teaching and the path, and the roots here are our reading.

Sources

  • SN 46.51 (external link)
  • Dhp 157-166 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-14. SN 46.51 was read only where it mentions doubt; what the sutta lists as "grounds for doubt" was not read.

Philosophical

The philosophers find the root in a mistaken judgement of one's own worth, and in resting that worth on things outside us.

  • A false estimate. Aristotle's "unduly humble" person thinks himself "worthy of less than he is really worthy of" (Nicomachean Ethics 4.3). The fault is a wrong measurement.
  • Worth in what is not ours. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1: "Things not in our control are body, property, reputation, command, and, in one word, whatever are not our own actions." If our sense of worth rests on reputation or on others' approval, any slight shakes it.
  • Reluctance at the start. Marcus Aurelius notes the pull of the bed in the morning ("when thou risest unwillingly", Meditations 5). He treats it as a reluctance to be answered, and not as a verdict on himself.

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. These are accounts of character and judgement; the link to impostor feelings is ours.

Sources
