Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how self-doubt works and how people loosen its grip.

Rohan's story

Rohan was promoted to team lead after two years as an engineer. His manager said he had earned it. Rohan thought they had made a mistake.

He stayed late to double-check everything, avoided asking questions in case they showed what he did not know, and put off giving feedback to a colleague because he felt he had no right to. When the team praised the release, he told himself that the others had done the hard parts.

What helped:

  1. He named it. A colleague mentioned "impostor feelings" in passing, and Rohan recognised the pattern.
  2. He kept a list. Each Friday he wrote down three things he had done that week and one piece of feedback he had received. After a month the list was hard to argue with.
  3. He asked a friend. The friend, a manager at another company, said she had felt the same for years.
  4. He asked his questions. The first two turned out to be questions others had been afraid to ask.
  5. He noticed it was not lifting. After a few months of poor sleep and dread on Sunday evenings, he spoke to his doctor.

The doubt did not disappear. It got quieter, and he stopped letting it make decisions.

What it shows: Rohan did not argue himself out of the feeling. He put evidence beside it, spoke about it and acted before he felt ready.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If self-doubt is causing constant anxiety, low mood or exhaustion, please see a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Arjuna lays down his courage

The Gita is a conversation between Krishna and Arjuna on the eve of a battle. Arjuna, a skilled warrior, stops.

His own account is in 2.7: "I am confused about my duty, and am besieged with anxiety and faintheartedness. I am Your disciple, and am surrendered to You. Please instruct me for certain what is best for me."

Krishna's first words (2.3) are blunt: "O Parth, it does not befit you to yield to this unmanliness. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise, O vanquisher of enemies."

The rest of the Gita is the teaching that follows. Later Krishna says of the mind: "For those who have conquered the mind, it is their friend. For those who have failed to do so, the mind works like an enemy" (6.6).

What it shows: A capable person can freeze, and the first step out is to say so and ask for guidance. Arjuna is not an impostor in the poem, and his crisis is over duty, not over feeling like a fraud; the story is read here for how it shows doubt and the way through.

Upanishads

Nachiketas and the third boon

The Katha Upanishad begins with a boy. His father has promised to give away all he owns, and "faith entered into the heart of Nachiketas, who was still a boy" (1.1.2). He asks, "Dear father, to whom wilt thou give me?" three times, and the angry father says, "I shall give thee unto Death" (1.1.4).

Nachiketas goes to the house of Death, waits, and is granted three boons. For the third he asks what happens after death: "There is that doubt, when a man is dead, - some saying, he is; others, he is not. This I should like to know, taught by thee" (1.1.20).

Death tries to put him off: "On this point even the gods have doubted formerly; it is not easy to understand. ... do not press me" (1.1.21). He offers sons, cattle, gold and long life. Nachiketas answers: "These things last till tomorrow, O Death, for they wear out this vigour of all the senses" (1.1.26).

What it shows: A boy with no standing, facing the one who has the power to refuse him, keeps to his question. The story is about the seeker's resolve, and not about self-doubt as such; it shows steadiness against being told that the question is too hard.

Christianity

Gideon, "the least in my family"

In the Book of Judges, the Lord turns to Gideon and says: "Go in this might of yours and deliver Israel from the hand of Midian; I hereby commission you" (6:14).

Gideon answers: "But sir, how can I deliver Israel? My clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my family" (6:15).

The reply does not argue with him about his clan or his place. It says: "But I will be with you, and you shall strike down the Midianites, every one of them" (6:16).

What it shows: Gideon's doubt is stated plainly, and the answer moves the question from what he is to who is with him. The passage is about a calling and not about impostor feelings; it is read here because it shows doubt named openly and answered.

Buddhism

The chapter on the self

The Dhammapada is a collection of verses and has no single story of self-doubt. The twelfth chapter, on the self, comes closest to speaking to it.

It begins: "If you knew your self as beloved, you'd look after it so well" (Dhp 157). And then: "One is indeed the lord of oneself, for who else would be one's lord? By means of a well-tamed self, one gains a lord that's rare indeed" (Dhp 160).

Traditional commentaries attach narratives to these verses. They were not read for this article, so none is retold here.

What it shows: The verses do not say that doubt is wrong. They say that the person is the one who has to look after and govern their own mind, and that doing it well is rare and worth doing. This is not about self-doubt directly, and the reading is ours.

Philosophical

Marcus Aurelius gets out of bed

In the Meditations, the emperor Marcus Aurelius writes notes to himself. In one of them he describes a morning when he does not want to rise.

"In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being. Why then am I dissatisfied if I am going to do the things for which I exist and for which I was brought into the world? Or have I been made for this, to lie in the bed-clothes and keep myself warm?"

An objector answers: "But this is more pleasant." He replies: "Dost thou exist then to take thy pleasure, and not at all for action or exertion?" He goes on to point to the little plants, the little birds, the ants, the spiders and the bees, each doing its own work.

What it shows: Marcus does not wait for the feeling to pass. He reminds himself what he is for and gets up. The passage is about reluctance, and not about self-doubt as such; it shows acting before the mood is right.
