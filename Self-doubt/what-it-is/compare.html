Self-doubt

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Self-doubt is a lasting uncertainty about your ability or your worth. Its best-known form is impostor syndrome (also called the impostor phenomenon): feeling like a fraud, and expecting to be found out, despite evidence of competence. The idea goes back to a 1978 paper by Clance and Imes on high-achieving women.

Two points from the research:

  • It is common and hard to measure. A systematic review of 62 studies and 14,161 participants found that prevalence "varied widely from 9 to 82%", largely depending on the screening tool and cut-off used. It was common in men and women and across ages.
  • It is not a diagnosis. The review describes it as often occurring with depression and anxiety, and notes that no published studies had evaluated treatments for it.

Some self-doubt is healthy, because it keeps us checking our work and listening to others. The difficulty is doubt that does not respond to evidence. If it is constant, or comes with low mood or anxiety, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Bravata et al. (2020), "Prevalence, predictors, and treatment of impostor syndrome: a systematic review," Journal of General Internal Medicine 35(4), 1252-1275. doi:10.1007/s11606-019-05364-1 (external link)
  • Clance and Imes (1978), "The imposter phenomenon in high achieving women: dynamics and therapeutic intervention," Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice 15(3), 241-247. doi:10.1037/h0086006 (external link)

The review's findings were read in its abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full text. For Clance and Imes only the title and publication details were checked (Crossref); the paper was not opened, so the statement that it introduced the idea is a standard summary. The remark that some self-doubt is healthy is general knowledge, not drawn from either source.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word for self-doubt, but the Bhagavad Gita opens with a man in its grip. Arjuna says in 2.7: "I am confused about my duty, and am besieged with anxiety and faintheartedness. I am Your disciple, and am surrendered to You. Please instruct me for certain what is best for me."

Krishna's first answer in 2.3 is: "O Parth, it does not befit you to yield to this unmanliness. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise, O vanquisher of enemies."

Later the Gita speaks of the mind as both ally and obstacle. 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. Arjuna's crisis is about duty and its cost, and the verses do not speak of competence or feeling like a fraud. The wording "unmanliness" in 2.3 is the translator's.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.3 (external link), 2.7 (external link), 6.5 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. Reading Arjuna's state as related to self-doubt is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not discuss self-doubt, but the Katha Upanishad begins with a boy who is unsure of what lies ahead and goes on anyway. Nachiketas, "still a boy", asks his father three times, "to whom wilt thou give me?" and is told "I shall give thee unto Death" (Katha 1.1.2-4).

The Mundaka Upanishad speaks to those who feel unequal to the search: "That Self cannot be gained by the Veda, nor by understanding, nor by much learning. He whom the Self chooses, by him the Self can be gained" (3.2.3). And: "Nor is that Self to be gained by one who is destitute of strength, or without earnestness, or without right meditation" (3.2.4).

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. The texts speak of strength, earnestness and the nature of the Self, and not of feeling like a fraud. Taking the first verse as a comfort to the doubtful (that learning is not the measure) is a reading and not stated in the text.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.1.1-4 (Max Müller's translation)
  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) 3.2.3-4 (Müller)

Christianity

The Bible has no word for impostor syndrome, but it records several people who doubt that they are the right person for the task.

  • Moses: "Who am I that I should go to Pharaoh and bring the Israelites out of Egypt?" (Exodus 3:11). Later: "I have never been eloquent... I am slow of speech and slow of tongue" (4:10).
  • Gideon: "how can I deliver Israel? My clan is the weakest in Manasseh, and I am the least in my family" (Judges 6:15).
  • Jeremiah: "Truly I do not know how to speak, for I am only a boy" (Jeremiah 1:6).

In each case the answer is the same: "I will be with you" (Exodus 3:12; Judges 6:16; see also Jeremiah 1:8).

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. These people doubt their fitness for a calling, and the texts do not describe a general feeling of being a fraud.

Sources

The quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway on 2026-11-14. The ellipsis in the Exodus 4:10 quotation marks omitted words ("neither in the past nor even now that you have spoken to your servant, but").

Buddhism

Buddhist texts have no word for self-doubt as we use it. Doubt (vicikiccha) is the fifth of the five hindrances, but it is doubt about the teaching and the path, so it is related to self-doubt and not identical to it (see sloth in Buddhism for the list).

SN 46.51 describes how doubt feeds: "And what fuels the arising of doubt, or, when it has arisen, makes it increase and grow? There are things that are grounds for doubt."

The Dhammapada has a chapter on the self that speaks to the other side. Dhp 157: "If you knew your self as beloved, you'd look after it so well." Dhp 160: "One is indeed the lord of oneself, for who else would be one's lord?"

Sources

  • SN 46.51 (external link)
  • Dhp 157-166 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API (for example suttacentral.net (external link)) on 2026-11-14. The Pali term and the list of five hindrances are standard across the canon and no single sutta is cited for them. That doubt in the hindrances list concerns the teaching and the path is a standard summary; SN 46.51 was read only where it mentions doubt, and the sutta's own definition of doubt was not checked.

Philosophical

The Greek philosophers had a name for the opposite fault to vanity: being too modest about one's own worth.

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3: "The man who thinks himself worthy of less than he is really worthy of is unduly humble, whether his deserts be great or moderate". Self-doubt that sets one's worth below the facts fits this description.

Marcus Aurelius addresses a related reluctance: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being."

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. Aristotle is describing a trait of character, and Marcus a reluctance to get up and work, and neither describes the feeling of being a fraud.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The MIT text of the Aristotle sentence repeats "worthy of" ("worthy of worthy of less"), a typographical error corrected here. The MIT Marcus text reads "In he morning" (corrected to "In the") and has a dash after "present" (shown here as a colon). The page has no paragraph numbers for Marcus, and the Aristotle chapter number is the one printed on the page. Taking "unduly humble" as a description of self-doubt is our reading.
