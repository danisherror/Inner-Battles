Self-doubt

Doubting your ability or worth, and feeling like a fraud despite what you have done.

Philosophical lens

What it is

The Greek philosophers had a name for the opposite fault to vanity: being too modest about one's own worth.

Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.3: "The man who thinks himself worthy of less than he is really worthy of is unduly humble, whether his deserts be great or moderate". Self-doubt that sets one's worth below the facts fits this description.

Marcus Aurelius addresses a related reluctance: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present: I am rising to the work of a human being."

This is related to self-doubt and not identical to it. Aristotle is describing a trait of character, and Marcus a reluctance to get up and work, and neither describes the feeling of being a fraud.

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The MIT text of the Aristotle sentence repeats "worthy of" ("worthy of worthy of less"), a typographical error corrected here. The MIT Marcus text reads "In he morning" (corrected to "In the") and has a dash after "present" (shown here as a colon). The page has no paragraph numbers for Marcus, and the Aristotle chapter number is the one printed on the page. Taking "unduly humble" as a description of self-doubt is our reading.
