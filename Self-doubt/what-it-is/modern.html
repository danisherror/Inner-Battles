What it is

Self-doubt is a lasting uncertainty about your ability or your worth. Its best-known form is impostor syndrome (also called the impostor phenomenon): feeling like a fraud, and expecting to be found out, despite evidence of competence. The idea goes back to a 1978 paper by Clance and Imes on high-achieving women.

Two points from the research:

  • It is common and hard to measure. A systematic review of 62 studies and 14,161 participants found that prevalence "varied widely from 9 to 82%", largely depending on the screening tool and cut-off used. It was common in men and women and across ages.
  • It is not a diagnosis. The review describes it as often occurring with depression and anxiety, and notes that no published studies had evaluated treatments for it.

Some self-doubt is healthy, because it keeps us checking our work and listening to others. The difficulty is doubt that does not respond to evidence. If it is constant, or comes with low mood or anxiety, a doctor or therapist can help.

The review's findings were read in its abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full text. For Clance and Imes only the title and publication details were checked (Crossref); the paper was not opened, so the statement that it introduced the idea is a standard summary. The remark that some self-doubt is healthy is general knowledge, not drawn from either source.
