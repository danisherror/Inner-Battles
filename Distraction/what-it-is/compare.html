Distraction

Lenses to show

Modern

Distraction is your attention being pulled away from what you meant to do. Attention fragmentation is the everyday version: so many streams of messages, tabs and alerts that your attention is cut into small pieces and rarely stays on one thing.

It is not only a modern or digital problem. Killingsworth and Gilbert used a smartphone app to sample people's thoughts, feelings and actions as they went about their day, and found "that people are thinking about what is not happening almost as often as they are thinking about what is and (ii) found that doing so typically makes them unhappy."

Researchers have also asked about devices. Ophir and colleagues note that "Chronic media multitasking is quickly becoming ubiquitous, although processing multiple incoming streams of information is considered a challenge for human cognition". Wilmer and colleagues describe "a growing perception that habitual involvement with these devices may have a negative and lasting impact on users' ability to think, remember, pay attention, and regulate emotion", and call the research "intensifying, though still limited".

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: reaching for the phone mid-sentence, ten tabs open and none finished, reading a paragraph three times, and finishing the day busy but unable to say what you did. It overlaps with overthinking, where the mind pulls itself away.

Distraction is not a diagnosis. If you cannot focus even when you want to, and it is affecting work, study or relationships, a doctor can help look for causes.

Sources

  • Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010), "A wandering mind is an unhappy mind," Science 330(6006), 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439 (external link)
  • Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009), "Cognitive control in media multitaskers," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106(37), 15583-15587. doi:10.1073/pnas.0903620106 (external link)
  • Wilmer, Sherman and Chein (2017), "Smartphones and cognition: a review of research exploring the links between mobile technology habits and cognitive functioning," Frontiers in Psychology 8, 605. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00605 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for distraction, but the Gita describes a mind that branches in many directions. Gita 2.41: "O descendent of the Kurus, the intellect of those who are on this path is resolute, and their aim is one-pointed. But the intellect of those who are irresolute is many-branched."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The Gita is speaking of resolve and the pursuit of a single aim in life, and does not describe the scattered attention of a modern day. Reading "many-branched" as a picture of fragmentation is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name distraction, but the Katha Upanishad describes why attention goes outward. Katha 2.1.1: "The Self-existent pierced the openings (of the senses) so that they turn forward; therefore man looks forward, not backward into himself. Some wise man, however, with his eyes closed and wishing for immortality, saw the Self behind."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The verse is about the senses turning outward and the possibility of turning inward, and it does not describe the scattered attention of a modern day. Reading it as speaking to distraction is ours.

Christianity

The Bible does not name distraction, but it names a mind pulled in many directions. In the house of Martha and Mary, Jesus says to Martha: "you are worried and distracted by many things, but few things are needed—indeed only one" (Luke 10:41-42).

James uses a related picture of the divided mind: "being double-minded and unstable in every way" (James 1:8).

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. Luke 10 is about welcome and attention to a guest, and James 1 is about doubt in prayer. Neither describes scattered attention in the modern sense, and reading them as speaking to it is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts speak of the mind as hard to see and hard to hold. Dhp 36: "So hard to see, so subtle, alighting where it will; the discerning protect the mind, a guarded mind leads to bliss."

And in AN 1.21: "Mendicants, I do not see a single thing that is so unworkable as this mind that is not developed. An undeveloped mind is unworkable."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The texts are about the untrained mind in general, and do not describe the pull of devices or constant messages. Reading them as speaking to distraction is ours.

Philosophical

Seneca names the scattered mind in a letter to Lucilius. Letters 2.1: "You do not run hither and thither and distract yourself by changing your abode; for such restlessness is the sign of a disordered spirit. The primary indication, to my thinking, of a well-ordered mind is a man's ability to remain in one place and linger in his own company."

He applies it to reading: "Everywhere means nowhere."

This fits the philosophical lens closely, since Seneca is speaking of restlessness and discursive reading directly. He is not speaking of devices, and he is describing a habit of mind and not a disorder.
