Effects on life

The evidence on what distraction does is mixed, and the reviews say so.

  • Poorer task-switching. Ophir and colleagues found that "heavy media multitaskers performed worse on a test of task-switching ability, likely due to reduced ability to filter out interference from the irrelevant task set".
  • A cautious summary. Uncapher and Wagner conclude that "the balance of evidence suggests that heavier media multitaskers exhibit poorer performance in a number of cognitive domains, relative to lighter media multitaskers (although many studies find no performance differences between groups)". Where there is a relationship it is "often on tasks that require or are influenced by fluctuations in sustained goal-directed attention".
  • Unhappiness. Killingsworth and Gilbert found that a mind not on the task "typically makes them unhappy".

These are associations in particular groups and tasks. They do not show that distraction harms everyone, or that it causes the differences found.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: work that takes twice as long, the feeling of a day gone, and shallower conversations. If your attention problems are affecting work, study or relationships, a doctor can help look for causes.

Sources

  • Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106(37), 15583-15587. doi:10.1073/pnas.0903620106 (external link)
  • Uncapher and Wagner (2018), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115(40), 9889-9896. doi:10.1073/pnas.1611612115 (external link)
  • Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010), Science 330(6006), 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The last paragraph cites no study.
