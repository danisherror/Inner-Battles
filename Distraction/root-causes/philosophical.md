Pascal puts the root in not being able to be alone with ourselves. *Pensées* 139: "I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber."

He explains why: what people want is "the bustle which averts these thoughts of ours, and amuses us", because "nothing can comfort us when we think of it closely" about "the natural poverty of our feeble and mortal condition".

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. Pascal is speaking of "diversion" as a flight from thinking about our condition, and not of attention in the modern sense. His account is one philosopher's view, and reading it as the root of distraction is ours.
