Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here does not establish what causes chronic distraction, and it points away from blaming devices alone.

  • The mind wanders on its own. Killingsworth and Gilbert found "that people are thinking about what is not happening almost as often as they are thinking about what is". That was measured in everyday life, with no device needed.
  • Heavy multitasking goes with more interference. Ophir and colleagues found that "heavy media multitaskers are more susceptible to interference from irrelevant environmental stimuli and from irrelevant representations in memory". But Uncapher and Wagner, reviewing the field, say further research is needed "to determine the direction of causality": it is not clear whether multitasking makes people easier to distract or distractible people multitask more.
  • Devices and cognition. Wilmer and colleagues find the research on smartphone habits "still limited" and say there are domains where "the scientific literature is not yet mature enough to endorse any firm conclusions".

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: apps and feeds are built to hold attention, a hard task invites escape into an easy one, and tiredness and stress lower the threshold for giving in.

Sources

  • Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010), Science 330(6006), 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439 (external link)
  • Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106(37), 15583-15587. doi:10.1073/pnas.0903620106 (external link)
  • Uncapher and Wagner (2018), "Minds and brains of media multitaskers: current findings and future directions," Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115(40), 9889-9896. doi:10.1073/pnas.1611612115 (external link)
  • Wilmer, Sherman and Chein (2017), Frontiers in Psychology 8, 605. doi:10.3389/fpsyg.2017.00605 (external link)

All four were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita says the senses can pull the mind off course. Gita 2.67: "Just as a strong wind sweeps a boat off its chartered course on the water, even one of the senses on which the mind focuses can lead the intellect astray."

And Gita 2.66: "an undisciplined person, who has not controlled the mind and senses, can neither have a resolute intellect nor steady contemplation on God."

The translator's commentary on 2.67 notes that the Katha Upanishad says God "has made our five senses outward facing" (see the Upanishads article).

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The verses are about the senses and the intellect in general. Reading them as the root of scattered attention is ours.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad pictures the mind dispersing among differences. Katha 2.1.14: "As rain-water that has fallen on a mountain-ridge runs down the rocks on all sides, thus does he, who sees a difference between qualities, run after them on all sides."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The verse is about seeing difference between qualities, and not about attention as such. Reading "run after them on all sides" as a picture of scattered attention is ours.

Sources

Christianity

The Bible touches two roots. One is a restless appetite for new things. Ecclesiastes 1:8: "All things are wearisome, more than one can express; the eye is not satisfied with seeing or the ear filled with hearing."

The other is being taken up with many tasks. Luke 10:40: "Martha was distracted by her many tasks".

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. Ecclesiastes is a reflection on how the world never finishes, and Luke 10 is about a hostess. Reading them as roots of distraction is ours.

Sources

  • Ecclesiastes 1:8 (external link), Luke 10:38-42 (external link)

The quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway on 2026-11-14. The footnote in Ecclesiastes 1:8 on "all things" and how commentators read the book were not checked.

Buddhism

SN 46.51 names what feeds a restless, scattered mind. "And what fuels the arising of restlessness and remorse, or, when they have arisen, makes them increase and grow? There is the unsettled mind. Frequent irrational application of mind to that fuels the arising of restlessness and remorse, or, when they have arisen, makes them increase and grow."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The sutta is about restlessness and remorse as a hindrance in meditation, and is not about everyday distraction. Reading "the unsettled mind" as a root of scattered attention is ours.

Sources

  • SN 46.51 (external link)

Philosophical

Pascal puts the root in not being able to be alone with ourselves. Pensées 139: "I have discovered that all the unhappiness of men arises from one single fact, that they cannot stay quietly in their own chamber."

He explains why: what people want is "the bustle which averts these thoughts of ours, and amuses us", because "nothing can comfort us when we think of it closely" about "the natural poverty of our feeble and mortal condition".

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. Pascal is speaking of "diversion" as a flight from thinking about our condition, and not of attention in the modern sense. His account is one philosopher's view, and reading it as the root of distraction is ours.

Sources

  • Pascal, Pensées, no. 139 (external link) (Project Gutenberg edition)
