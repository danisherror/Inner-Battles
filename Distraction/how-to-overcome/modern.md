No study read here tested a way of fixing distraction. Uncapher and Wagner say research is needed "to understand whether remediation efforts are needed and effective", so most of what follows is common advice, with one source on changing your surroundings.

1. **Pick the one thing.** Decide what the next block of time is for before you start.
2. **Change the situation.** Duckworth, Gendler and Gross describe "proactively choosing or changing situations in ways that weaken undesirable impulses or potentiate desirable ones", such as "the student who goes to the library without a cell phone". Putting the phone in another room and closing the tabs you do not need is that kind of move. Their paper is about self-control in general, not distraction.
3. **Work in blocks, one thing at a time.** Ophir and colleagues found heavy multitaskers worse at "task-switching ability", which is a reason to switch less, though not a test of doing so.
4. **Turn off what you do not need.** Notifications are a common source of interruption.
5. **Notice and return.** When your mind wanders, which Killingsworth and Gilbert found it often does, bring it back without a fight. That is common advice and it echoes the tradition lenses.
6. **Rest properly.** Short breaks away from screens are common advice.
7. **Get help if it will not shift.** A doctor can help look at sleep, anxiety, low mood and other causes when you cannot focus even when you want to.
