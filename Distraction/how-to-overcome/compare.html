Distraction

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

No study read here tested a way of fixing distraction. Uncapher and Wagner say research is needed "to understand whether remediation efforts are needed and effective", so most of what follows is common advice, with one source on changing your surroundings.

  1. Pick the one thing. Decide what the next block of time is for before you start.
  2. Change the situation. Duckworth, Gendler and Gross describe "proactively choosing or changing situations in ways that weaken undesirable impulses or potentiate desirable ones", such as "the student who goes to the library without a cell phone". Putting the phone in another room and closing the tabs you do not need is that kind of move. Their paper is about self-control in general, not distraction.
  3. Work in blocks, one thing at a time. Ophir and colleagues found heavy multitaskers worse at "task-switching ability", which is a reason to switch less, though not a test of doing so.
  4. Turn off what you do not need. Notifications are a common source of interruption.
  5. Notice and return. When your mind wanders, which Killingsworth and Gilbert found it often does, bring it back without a fight. That is common advice and it echoes the tradition lenses.
  6. Rest properly. Short breaks away from screens are common advice.
  7. Get help if it will not shift. A doctor can help look at sleep, anxiety, low mood and other causes when you cannot focus even when you want to.

Sources

  • Uncapher and Wagner (2018), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 115(40), 9889-9896. doi:10.1073/pnas.1611612115 (external link)
  • Duckworth, Gendler and Gross (2016), "Situational strategies for self-control," Perspectives on Psychological Science 11(1), 35-55. doi:10.1177/1745691615623247 (external link)
  • Ophir, Nass and Wagner (2009), Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences 106(37), 15583-15587. doi:10.1073/pnas.0903620106 (external link)
  • Killingsworth and Gilbert (2010), Science 330(6006), 932. doi:10.1126/science.1192439 (external link)

All four were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. Steps 1, 4, 5, 6 and 7 are common advice, not tied to a study read here; step 3 is our reading of Ophir et al.

Hinduism

The Gita points to bringing the mind back, steady attention, and one-pointed concentration.

  1. Bring it back, every time. Gita 6.26: "Whenever and wherever the restless and unsteady mind wanders, one should bring it back and continually focus it on God."
  2. Be like a lamp without wind. Gita 6.19: "Just as a lamp in a windless place does not flicker, so the disciplined mind of a yogi remains steady in meditation on the Supreme."
  3. Concentrate on one point. Gita 6.12-13: "the yogi should strive to purify the mind by focusing it in meditation with one pointed concentration, controlling all thoughts and activities."
  4. Withdraw when you need to. Gita 2.58: "One who is able to withdraw the senses from their objects, just as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, is established in divine wisdom."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The verses are about meditation in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor if you cannot focus even when you want to. Reading them as advice for a scattered day is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to settling, to holding the senses back, and to leaving off other words.

  1. Be like pure water in pure water. Katha 2.1.15: "As pure water poured into pure water remains the same, thus, O Gautama, is the Self of a thinker who knows."
  2. Hold the senses back. Katha 2.3.10-11: "When the five instruments of knowledge stand still together with the mind, and when the intellect does not move, that is called the highest state. This, the firm holding back of the senses, is what is called Yoga. He must be free from thoughtlessness then, for Yoga comes and goes."
  3. Leave off other words. Mundaka 2.2.5: "Know him alone as the Self, and leave off other words!"

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. The verses are about the Self and yoga, and nothing here replaces a doctor if you cannot focus even when you want to. Reading them as advice for a scattered day is ours.

Christianity

The Bible's counsel is stillness, an undivided heart, and a straight gaze.

  1. Be still. Psalm 46:10: "Be still, and know that I am God!"
  2. Ask for an undivided heart. Psalm 86:11: "Teach me your way, O Lord, that I may walk in your truth; give me an undivided heart to revere your name."
  3. Look straight ahead. Proverbs 4:25: "Let your eyes look directly forward and your gaze be straight before you."
  4. Choose what to think about. Philippians 4:8: "whatever is true, whatever is honorable, whatever is just, whatever is pure, whatever is pleasing, whatever is commendable, if there is any excellence and if there is anything worthy of praise, think about these things."
  5. Keep the eye healthy. Matthew 6:22: "The eye is the lamp of the body. So if your eye is healthy, your whole body will be full of light".

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. These verses are about stillness, devotion and attention in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor if you cannot focus even when you want to.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts teach redirecting the mind, settling it, and cultivating it.

  1. Swap one thought for another. MN 20: "That mendicant should focus on some other subject connected with the skillful." The sutta compares it to "a deft mason or their apprentice who'd knock out or extract a large peg with a finer peg."
  2. Settle the mind. SN 46.51: "There is the settled mind. Frequent rational application of mind to that starves the arising of restlessness and remorse".
  3. Restrain it. Dhp 37: "Those who will restrain the mind are freed from Māra's bonds."
  4. Cultivate it. AN 1.30: "A developed and cultivated mind brings happiness."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. MN 20 is about unskillful thoughts of desire, hate and delusion, and the other passages are about the mind in general. Nothing here replaces a doctor if you cannot focus even when you want to, and applying MN 20 to ordinary distraction is ours.

Philosophical

The Stoics' advice is to stay with a few things, and to stand firm in the noise.

  1. Linger over a few. Seneca, Letters 2.2: "You must linger among a limited number of master-thinkers, and digest their works, if you would derive ideas which shall win firm hold in your mind."
  2. Own only what you can read. Seneca, 2.3: "since you cannot read all the books which you may possess, it is enough to possess only as many books as you can read."
  3. Be the promontory. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4: "Be like the promontory against which the waves continually break, but it stands firm and tames the fury of the water around it."

This is related to distraction and not identical to it. Seneca is speaking of reading, and Marcus of equanimity in trouble. Nothing here replaces a doctor if you cannot focus even when you want to, and applying them to a scattered day is ours.
