Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how distraction works and how people loosen its grip.

Ola's story

Ola, an analyst, had a report due on Friday. Every morning she opened it, wrote a sentence, and then a message pinged. Two hours later she had answered nine messages, read three articles and written four sentences.

By Thursday night she was working late and anxious, and she did not feel she had done anything with the week.

What helped:

  1. She decided on one thing. At the start of each morning she wrote down the single section that would count as a good morning's work.
  2. She changed the situation. The phone went in a bag across the room and the chat window closed.
  3. She worked in blocks. Fifty minutes on the section, then ten minutes to check messages.
  4. She noticed the pull and went back. When she caught herself reaching for the phone, she wrote the word on a note and returned to the sentence.
  5. She noticed it was not lifting. On days when she could not hold a thought even with the phone away, and was sleeping badly, she spoke to her doctor, who looked into her sleep and stress.

The pull did not vanish. It got easier to ignore, and the report was done by Thursday lunchtime.

What it shows: Ola did not rely on resolve alone. She changed what was in reach, gave the work a boundary, and asked for help when the problem lasted.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If you cannot focus even when you want to, please see a doctor.

Hinduism

Three pictures of a steady mind

The Gita has no story of distraction, so this is a set of pictures from Krishna's teaching and not a tale. The first is the tortoise: "One who is able to withdraw the senses from their objects, just as a tortoise withdraws its limbs into its shell, is established in divine wisdom" (2.58).

The second is the boat in the wind: "Just as a strong wind sweeps a boat off its chartered course on the water, even one of the senses on which the mind focuses can lead the intellect astray" (2.67).

The third is the lamp: "Just as a lamp in a windless place does not flicker, so the disciplined mind of a yogi remains steady in meditation on the Supreme" (6.19).

What it shows: The pictures describe a mind that can be pulled in, blown off course, or held still. They are not about distraction as such; the link is ours, and it is that attention can be sheltered as well as scattered.

Upanishads

The archer's aim

The Mundaka Upanishad has no narrative of distraction, so this is a picture from the teaching and not a tale. It is the picture of an archer.

"Having taken the Upanishad as the bow, as the great weapon, let him place on it the arrow, sharpened by devotion! Then having drawn it with a thought directed to that which is, hit the mark, O friend, viz. that which is the Indestructible!" (2.2.3).

And: "Om is the bow, the Self is the arrow. Brahman is called its aim. It is to be hit by a man who is not thoughtless; and then, as the arrow (becomes one with the target), he will become one with Brahman" (2.2.4).

What it shows: The aim is hit by someone who is "not thoughtless", whose attention is on the mark. The passage is about meditation and the Self and not about distraction; the link is ours.

Sources

  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) 2.2.3-4 (Max Müller's translation)

Christianity

Martha and Mary

In Luke 10, Jesus enters a village and "a woman named Martha welcomed him." Her sister Mary "sat at Jesus's feet and listened to what he was saying."

"But Martha was distracted by her many tasks, so she came to him and asked, 'Lord, do you not care that my sister has left me to do all the work by myself? Tell her, then, to help me.'"

"But the Lord answered her, 'Martha, Martha, you are worried and distracted by many things, but few things are needed—indeed only one. Mary has chosen the better part, which will not be taken away from her'" (10:38-42).

What it shows: Martha is busy with something good, and it takes her away from the person she is serving. The story is about welcome and listening and not about distraction as a modern problem; it does not say that work is wrong, and the way Jesus speaks to her is gentle: he says her name twice.

Buddhism

Bāhiya and the short teaching

In Ud 1.10, Bāhiya hears of the Buddha and hurries to Sāvatthī to see him. He finds the Buddha on his alms round and asks to be taught. The Buddha says: "It's not the time, Bāhiya, so long as I have entered an inhabited area for almsfood." Bāhiya asks a second time and a third, saying "you never know, sir, when life is at risk, either the Buddha's or my own."

The Buddha then gives a short teaching: "'In the seen will be merely the seen; in the heard will be merely the heard; in the thought will be merely the thought; in the known will be merely the known.' That's how you should train."

The sutta says that "due to this brief Dhamma teaching of the Buddha, Bāhiya's mind was right away freed from defilements by not grasping."

What it shows: The instruction is to meet what is seen or heard as just that, without chasing it into something else. The sutta is about liberation and not about distraction; the link is ours. The sutta goes on to tell what happened to Bāhiya afterwards, which is not used here.

Philosophical

Pascal's king and the hare

Pascal is not telling a story in Pensées 139, but the passage has two scenes. The first is a king. "When we imagine a king attended with every pleasure he can feel, if he be without diversion, and be left to consider and reflect on what he is, this feeble happiness will not sustain him". So "the king is surrounded by persons whose only thought is to divert the king, and to prevent his thinking of self."

The second is the hunt. People do not hunt because they want the hare: "we would not take these as a gift". They want the chase: "The hare in itself would not screen us from the sight of death and calamities; but the chase which turns away our attention from these, does screen us."

What it shows: Pascal's claim is that we chase things in order to be kept busy, and not for what they are. The passage is about diversion and our fear of being alone with our thoughts, and not about attention as a modern problem. It is one philosopher's view, and we do not endorse it as the whole truth about distraction.
