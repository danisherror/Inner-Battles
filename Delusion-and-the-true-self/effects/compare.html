Delusion and the true self

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here say little about effects, and what they say is cautious.

  • Self-knowledge has value, with limits. Wilson and Dunn write: "It is not always advantageous to hold self-perceptions that correspond perfectly with reality, but increasing awareness of nonconscious motives and personality is generally beneficial." The abstract does not list what the benefits are.
  • What this does not show. Neither abstract tests what happens to a person who holds a mistaken view of who they are, and neither shows that a "true self" exists.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: decisions that do not fit your values, being thrown by a change of job or role, and a gap between how you act and how you feel. If you feel lost or unreal, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Wilson and Dunn (2004), Annual Review of Psychology 55, 493-518. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.141954 (external link)
  • Strohminger, Knobe and Newman (2017), Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(4), 551-560. doi:10.1177/1745691616689495 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. The Strohminger abstract says only that the findings have "implications for a variety of issues in psychology", and the issues were not read. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita says what the illusion costs. Gita 7.27: "the dualities of desire and aversion arise from illusion."

And at the start of the teaching, Krishna says: "While you speak words of wisdom, you are mourning for that which is not worthy of grief" (2.11).

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. 7.27 is about desire and aversion, and 2.11 is addressed to Arjuna in a battlefield. Reading them as the cost of ignorance of the self is ours.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 7.27 (external link), 2.11 (external link)

The quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-15. The first quotation stops where the sentence continues. The Sanskrit words were not examined.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad says what unmastered senses do. Katha 1.3.5: "He who has no understanding and whose mind (the reins) is never firmly held, his senses (horses) are unmanageable, like vicious horses of a charioteer."

It gives the opposite in 1.2.22: "The wise who knows the Self as bodiless within the bodies, as unchanging among changing things, as great and omnipresent, does never grieve."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The first verse is about understanding and the senses, and the second about the wise who knows the Self. Reading the first as the cost of ignorance of the Self is ours.

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.3.5, 1.2.22 (Max Müller's translation)

Christianity

The Gospels say what it costs to lose one's life by holding it too tightly. Matthew 16:25-26: "For those who want to save their life will lose it, and those who lose their life for my sake will find it. For what will it profit them if they gain the whole world but forfeit their life?"

Paul names a limit of sight. 1 Corinthians 13:12: "For now we see only a reflection, as in a mirror, but then we will see face to face. Now I know only in part; then I will know fully, even as I have been fully known."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. Matthew 16 is about following Jesus at a cost, and 1 Corinthians 13 about partial knowledge. Reading them as the cost of ignorance of the self is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts say what taking the changing for a self brings. SN 22.59: "But because form is not-self, it leads to affliction. And you can't compel form: 'May my form be like this! May it not be like that!'"

SN 12.1 gives the whole chain: "Rebirth is a requirement for old age and death, sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress to come to be."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. SN 22.59 is about the five aggregates and SN 12.1 about dependent origination, and both speak of not-self and not of a true self. Reading them as the cost of mistaking what the self is is ours.

Sources

  • SN 22.59 (external link), SN 12.1 (external link)

The quotations were read in Bhikkhu Sujato's translation via the SuttaCentral bilarasuttas API on 2026-11-15. The SN 22.59 quotation is from the exchange about form, which the sutta repeats for the other four aggregates. The Pali terms were not examined.

Philosophical

Plato says seeing the truth first hurts. Republic 7: when a prisoner is freed and made to look towards the light, "he will suffer sharp pains; the glare will distress him, and he will be unable to see the realities of which in his former state he had seen the shadows".

Marcus Aurelius reduces what we take ourselves to be. Meditations 4: "Thou art a little soul bearing about a corpse, as Epictetus used to say."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. Plato is describing the pain of leaving illusion, and Marcus is repeating a saying of Epictetus about the body. Reading them as the cost of ignorance of the self is ours.

Sources

  • Plato, Republic 7 (external link) (Benjamin Jowett's translation)
  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 4 (external link) (George Long's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-15. The Plato page gives no Stephanus numbers, and the Marcus page gives no section number, so the passages are cited to the books only. The Epictetus saying was not checked at its source. The last sentence is ours.
