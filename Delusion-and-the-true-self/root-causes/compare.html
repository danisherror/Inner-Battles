Delusion and the true self

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here point to why knowing ourselves is hard, and they say little about a hidden "true" self.

  • Motives and the structure of the mind. Wilson and Dunn write that "people often attempt to block out unwanted thoughts and feelings through conscious suppression and perhaps through unconscious repression, though whether such attempts are successful is controversial." They add: "A more common source of self-knowledge failure is the inaccessibility of much of the mind to consciousness". This is a review of research, and its abstract is a summary.
  • A flattering picture. Strohminger and colleagues find that the true self "is perceived as positive and moral". That suggests we may imagine our true self to be better than we are, and the abstract does not say so itself.

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: being defined by others' expectations early on, taking on roles that fit a family or a job, and living busily enough that there is little time to look inward.

Sources

  • Wilson and Dunn (2004), Annual Review of Psychology 55, 493-518. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.141954 (external link)
  • Strohminger, Knobe and Newman (2017), Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(4), 551-560. doi:10.1177/1745691616689495 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full. The sentence "That suggests we may imagine our true self to be better than we are" is our inference from the abstract and is not stated by the authors. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita's root is illusion that arises from the modes of nature. Gita 7.27: "O descendant of Bharat, the dualities of desire and aversion arise from illusion. O conqueror of enemies, all living beings in the material realm are deluded by these."

Gita 14.8: "tamo guṇa, which is born of ignorance, is the cause of illusion for the embodied souls. It deludes all living beings through negligence, laziness, and sleep."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. These verses are about illusion, desire and the mode of ignorance, and do not speak of a self to be found. Reading them as roots of ignorance of the self is ours.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad names the root as taking the Self for the body and its doings. Katha 1.2.19: "If the killer thinks that he kills, if the killed thinks that he is killed, they do not understand; for this one does not kill, nor is that one killed."

And in the chariot picture, 1.3.4: "When he (the Highest Self) is in union with the body, the senses, and the mind, then wise people call him the Enjoyer."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The first verse is about the Self that neither kills nor is killed, and the second about the Self joined to body, senses and mind. Reading them as roots of ignorance of the Self is ours.

Christianity

The New Testament names ignorance and a hardened heart. Ephesians 4:17-18: "you must no longer walk as the gentiles walk, in the futility of their minds; they are darkened in their understanding, alienated from the life of God because of their ignorance and hardness of heart."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The passage is about darkened understanding and a way of life, and does not speak of ignorance of the self. The wording in the page's footnote on "gentiles" was not read. Reading the verses as a root of ignorance of oneself is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts put ignorance at the start of the chain that ends in suffering. SN 12.1: "Ignorance is a requirement for choices. Choices are a requirement for consciousness."

The sutta continues through craving and grasping to its end: "That is how this entire mass of suffering originates."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. SN 12.1 is about dependent origination, and the ignorance it names is the sutta's own term, which this article does not claim is ignorance of a true self. This topic's title does not fit the Buddhist texts, which teach not-self.

Philosophical

Plato's root is a life lived facing the wall. Republic 7: the prisoners have been in the den "from their childhood", with "their legs and necks chained so that they cannot move, and can only see before them, being prevented by the chains from turning round their heads."

Hume offers a different view, and a puzzle for any theory of a true self. Treatise 1.4.6: "when I enter most intimately into what I call myself, I always stumble on some particular perception or other, of heat or cold, light or shade, love or hatred, pain or pleasure. I never can catch myself at any time without a perception, and never can observe any thing but the perception."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. Plato's passage is about a life of illusion, and Hume's is his report of looking inward. They do not agree, and this site does not take a side. The reading of Plato as a root of ignorance of oneself is ours.

  • Hume, A Treatise of Human Nature 1.4.6 (external link) (Hume Texts Online)
