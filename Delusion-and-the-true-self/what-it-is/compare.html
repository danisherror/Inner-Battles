Inner battle

Delusion and the true self

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

"The true self" is an idea from religion and philosophy: that beneath our roles, moods and habits there is a deeper self, and that we are mostly mistaken about it. Psychology has not established that such a self exists. What it can say is how hard it is to know ourselves, and what people believe about their "true" selves.

Wilson and Dunn review the research and begin: "Because of personal motives and the architecture of the mind, it may be difficult for people to know themselves." Strohminger, Knobe and Newman describe a recent line of work in which, "even among characteristics that are internal to the self, people pick out a subset as belonging to the true self." These are "judged as making people who they really are, deep down", and they find that the true self "is perceived as positive and moral". That is a finding about what people believe, and it does not show that a true self exists.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: feeling empty when a role or job ends, being surprised by your own reaction, and feeling that the person others see is not quite you. It overlaps with ego and validation and denial.

This is not a diagnosis. If you feel unreal, lost or detached, or that you do not know who you are, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Wilson and Dunn (2004), "Self-knowledge: its limits, value, and potential for improvement," Annual Review of Psychology 55, 493-518. doi:10.1146/annurev.psych.55.090902.141954 (external link)
  • Strohminger, Knobe and Newman (2017), "The true self: a psychological concept distinct from the self," Perspectives on Psychological Science 12(4), 551-560. doi:10.1177/1745691616689495 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The statements that psychology has not established a true self, and the list of everyday signs, are a standard summary and not drawn from a source.

Hinduism

In the Bhagavad Gita delusion is taking the changing body for the self. Gita 2.13: "Just as the embodied soul continuously passes from childhood to youth to old age, similarly, at the time of death, the soul passes into another body. The wise are not deluded by this."

And 2.20: "The soul is neither born, nor does it ever die; nor having once existed, does it ever cease to be. The soul is without birth, eternal, immortal, and ageless. It is not destroyed when the body is destroyed."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The Gita speaks of the soul and of delusion in its own terms. The page for 7.27 renders words from the root moha as "illusion" and "delusion", and the Sanskrit was otherwise not examined.

Upanishads

The Upanishads name ignorance and place it against wisdom. Katha 1.2.4: "Wide apart and leading to different points are these two, ignorance, and what is known as wisdom."

And Chandogya 6.8.7, a father to his son: "Now that which is that subtile essence (the root of all), in it all that exists has its self. It is the True. It is the Self, and thou ... art it."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The Katha verse sets ignorance against wisdom, and the Chandogya verse is a teaching to one student about the Self.

Christianity

The New Testament speaks of an old self and a new self. Ephesians 4:22-24: "to put away your former way of life, your old self, corrupt and deluded by its lusts, and to be renewed in the spirit of your minds, and to clothe yourselves with the new self, created according to the likeness of God in true righteousness and holiness."

1 John 3:2 adds that identity is not yet fully seen: "Beloved, we are God's children now; what we will be has not yet been revealed."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. These verses are about a way of life and about being God's children, and do not describe a hidden self to be discovered by looking within. Reading them as speaking of the true self is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts do not teach a true self, and the topic's title does not fit them. They name the mistake of taking the changing parts of a person to be a self. SN 22.59: "But if it's impermanent, suffering, and perishable, is it fit to be regarded thus: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?" "No, sir."

AN 4.49 names it among four perversions: "Taking not-self as self."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The texts speak of not-self, and this article reads delusion (moha) and ignorance (avijjā) here only as mistaking what the self is. Whether the Buddha affirmed any self is outside what was read.

  • SN 22.59 (external link), AN 4.49 (external link)

The quotation from SN 22.59 comes from the exchange about form and is repeated in the sutta for feeling, perception, choices and consciousness. The Pali terms moha and avijjā were not examined. Philosophical

Plato pictures people who mistake shadows for the real. In the Republic Socrates asks us to behold "human beings living in a underground den", chained from childhood, who "can only see before them". When Glaucon says they are strange, Socrates answers: "Like ourselves... they see only their own shadows". And: "To them... the truth would be literally nothing but the shadows of the images."

This is related to the idea of the true self and not identical to it. The cave is a picture of ignorance of reality, and the passage is not mainly about the self. Reading it as a picture of mistaking oneself is ours.
