Delusion and the true self

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how a narrow picture of oneself works and how people widen it.

Haroon's story

For thirty years Haroon was "the one who runs the department". When it restructured and his post went, he found that he did not know what to do with a Tuesday. He said "I'm nothing without the job" so often that his family stopped answering.

What helped:

  1. He expected to be partly wrong. The sentence "I'm nothing" felt like a fact. He began to treat it as one view among others.
  2. He asked other people. A former colleague said what he had been like to work with. A neighbour said what he was like at home. Neither was the picture he had of himself.
  3. He did not trust looking inward alone. Sitting and thinking led in circles, so he wrote down what he did in a week and looked at that.
  4. He questioned the flattering picture. He had imagined his real self as the manager. He noticed he also liked teaching a younger neighbour to repair things.
  5. He checked roles against values. He listed the roles he had and asked which he had chosen and which had simply happened.
  6. He asked for help. The flat feeling did not lift, and he spoke to his doctor, who referred him to a counsellor.

The old role did not stop mattering. It stopped being the whole of him.

What it shows: Haroon did not find a hidden self. He found that the picture he had was narrower than the life he was living, and he widened it with the help of other people.

Hinduism

Krishna begins the teaching

The Gita has no tale of delusion about the self, so this is the opening of Krishna's teaching and not a story. Arjuna has told Krishna that he is confused and grieving (2.7-2.8 (external link)). Krishna answers: "While you speak words of wisdom, you are mourning for that which is not worthy of grief. The wise lament neither for the living nor for the dead" (2.11).

He goes on: "Never was there a time when I did not exist, nor you, nor all these kings; nor in the future shall any of us cease to be" (2.12). And: "The soul is neither born, nor does it ever die" (2.20).

What it shows: The teaching starts by telling Arjuna that what he mourns is not the whole of who he and the others are. The passage is about grief on a battlefield and the soul, and not about delusion as a habit; the link is ours.

Upanishads

Svetaketu and the salt

In the Chandogya Upanishad a father asks his son to do something simple: "Place this salt in water, and then wait on me in the morning." In the morning he says: "Bring me the salt, which you placed in the water last night." The son looks for it, "found it not, for, of course, it was melted."

The father says: "Taste it from the surface of the water. How is it?" "It is salt." "Taste it from the middle." "It is salt." "Taste it from the bottom." "It is salt." Then: "Here also, in this body, forsooth, you do not perceive the True (Sat), my son; but there indeed it is."

He ends: "That which is the subtile essence, in it all that exists has its self. It is the True. It is the Self, and thou ... art it" (6.13.1-3).

What it shows: What cannot be seen is still there, and it can be known by taste. The teaching is about the Self and the True, and the story is told to one student; reading it as a lesson about ignorance of oneself is ours.

Christianity

Saul on the road to Damascus

In Acts 9 Saul is "still breathing threats and murder against the disciples of the Lord", on his way to Damascus to bring any who belong to the Way "bound to Jerusalem". "Suddenly a light from heaven flashed around him. He fell to the ground and heard a voice saying to him, 'Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?'" (9:3-4).

"Saul got up from the ground, and though his eyes were open, he could see nothing" (9:8). For three days he is without sight. Then Ananias comes to him: "Brother Saul, the Lord Jesus, who appeared to you on your way here, has sent me so that you may regain your sight". And "immediately something like scales fell from his eyes, and his sight was restored" (9:17-18).

What it shows: Saul is sure of what he is doing, and the text describes his being stopped and then seeing again. The story is about a conversion and not about the true self as such; the link is ours, and it has a long history of interpretation that was not read.

Buddhism

The Buddha and the group of five

In SN 22.59 the Buddha addresses the group of five mendicants at Isipatana. He says: "form is not-self. For if form were self, it wouldn't lead to affliction. And you could compel form: 'May my form be like this! May it not be like that!'" He asks whether form is permanent, and they answer that it is impermanent, then: "But if it's impermanent, suffering, and perishable, is it fit to be regarded thus: 'This is mine, I am this, this is my self'?" "No, sir." The same is asked of feeling, perception, choices and consciousness.

He concludes: "So you should truly see any kind of form at all ... with right understanding: 'This is not mine, I am not this, this is not my self.'" At the end, "the minds of the group of five mendicants were freed from defilements by not grasping."

What it shows: The Buddha does not tell them what the self is. He asks, of each part of a person, whether it is fit to be called "my self". The sutta teaches not-self and does not speak of a true self; this topic's title does not fit it.

Philosophical

Plato's cave

In Book 7 of the Republic Socrates asks Glaucon to picture "human beings living in a underground den", chained from childhood so that they can see only the wall in front of them. Behind them a fire burns, and people carry objects along a raised way. The prisoners see only shadows. Glaucon says: "You have shown me a strange image, and they are strange prisoners." Socrates answers: "Like ourselves".

If one is freed, "he will suffer sharp pains; the glare will distress him". He comes slowly to see reflections, then things, then the sun. Remembering the den, "do you not suppose that he would felicitate himself on the change, and pity them?"

What it shows: The prisoners take the shadows for the real, and Socrates says they are "like ourselves". The cave is a picture of ignorance and education and not of the self as such; the link is ours, and it is one philosopher's image.
