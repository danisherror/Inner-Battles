Resentment

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Resentment is a bitter feeling that stays alive after a wrong, fed by replaying it. A grudge is the decision, spoken or not, to keep holding it. The wrong may be real and the anger may be fair; the trouble is when the hurt is relived again and again and the past keeps paying rent in your head.

Researchers have studied what holding on does. Witvliet and colleagues asked 71 people to rehearse "hurtful memories and nursed grudges (i.e., were unforgiving)" about a real offender, and compared it with cultivating "empathic perspective taking and imagined granting forgiveness". Gangopadhyay and Singh studied "anger, anger rumination, grudge, and hate", and found that the grudge was "the most central and influential variable" in their network of the four.

Forgiving is not the same as excusing a wrong, trusting the person again or staying in touch. That distinction is a standard summary and not drawn from these papers.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: rehearsing an argument in the shower, keeping a mental tally of who owes whom, tensing when a name comes up, and withholding warmth long after the event. It overlaps with overthinking, where the mind loops on the past.

Resentment is not a diagnosis. If it fills your days, or comes from abuse or serious harm, a therapist can help, and you do not have to forgive in order to be safe.

Sources

  • Witvliet, Ludwig and Vander Laan (2001), "Granting forgiveness or harboring grudges: implications for emotion, physiology, and health," Psychological Science 12(2), 117-123. doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00320 (external link)
  • Gangopadhyay and Singh (2026), "Mapping interpersonal hate: a network and mediation analysis of anger, rumination, and grudge," The Journal of Psychology. doi:10.1080/00223980.2026.2716095 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), and publication details were checked in Crossref. The count of 71 participants is the abstract's (35 females and 36 males). The definitions, the distinction between forgiving and excusing, and the list of everyday signs are a standard summary and not drawn from a source.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for a grudge, but the Gita names its opposites among the divine qualities. Gita 16.1-3 lists, among others: "absence of anger", "restraint from fault-finding", "forgiveness" and "bearing enmity toward none".

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The Gita is describing the qualities of a person of divine nature, and does not describe a kept grudge. Reading these as the opposite of one is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name resentment, and the closest they come is a picture of seeing all beings in the Self. Isha Upanishad 6: "And he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, he never turns away from it."

And Isha 7: "When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The verses are about unity and the end of sorrow, and do not describe a grudge. Reading them as speaking to one is ours.

Christianity

The Bible names the grudge directly. Leviticus 19:17-18: "You shall not hate in your heart anyone of your kin; you shall reprove your neighbor, or you will incur guilt yourself. You shall not take vengeance or bear a grudge against any of your people, but you shall love your neighbor as yourself: I am the Lord."

Joseph's brothers fear one after their father dies: "What if Joseph still bears a grudge against us and pays us back in full for all the wrong that we did to him?" (Genesis 50:15).

This is a direct match for the Christian lens, since the Bible uses the word. Leviticus is addressed to a community, and it also tells the person to "reprove your neighbor", so the verse does not say to say nothing about a wrong.

Buddhism

The Dhammapada opens with the grudge. Dhp 3-4: "'They abused me, they hit me! They beat me, they robbed me!' For those who bear such a grudge, hatred is never laid to rest. 'They abused me, they hit me! They beat me, they robbed me!' For those who bear no such grudge, hatred is laid to rest."

This is a direct match for the Buddhist lens, since the translation uses the word "grudge". The verses are about hatred between people, and the Pali terms behind them were not examined.

Philosophical

Aristotle describes the person who keeps a grudge. In the Nicomachean Ethics (4.5): "Sulky people are hard to appease, and retain their anger long; for they repress their passion."

He adds that "it ceases when they retaliate; for revenge relieves them of their anger, producing in them pleasure instead of pain."

This fits the philosophical lens closely, since Aristotle is describing the sulking, long-kept anger that we would call a grudge. His account is one philosopher's description and not a modern definition, and it is not about forgiveness.
