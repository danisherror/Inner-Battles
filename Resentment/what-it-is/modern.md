Resentment is a bitter feeling that stays alive after a wrong, fed by replaying it. A grudge is the decision, spoken or not, to keep holding it. The wrong may be real and the anger may be fair; the trouble is when the hurt is relived again and again and the past keeps paying rent in your head.

Researchers have studied what holding on does. Witvliet and colleagues asked 71 people to rehearse "hurtful memories and nursed grudges (i.e., were unforgiving)" about a real offender, and compared it with cultivating "empathic perspective taking and imagined granting forgiveness". Gangopadhyay and Singh studied "anger, anger rumination, grudge, and hate", and found that the grudge was "the most central and influential variable" in their network of the four.

Forgiving is not the same as excusing a wrong, trusting the person again or staying in touch. That distinction is a standard summary and not drawn from these papers.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: rehearsing an argument in the shower, keeping a mental tally of who owes whom, tensing when a name comes up, and withholding warmth long after the event. It overlaps with [overthinking](../../Overthinking/what-it-is/modern.html), where the mind loops on the past.

Resentment is not a diagnosis. If it fills your days, or comes from abuse or serious harm, a therapist can help, and you do not have to forgive in order to be safe.
