The research read here supports forgiveness work with a professional for serious hurts. The rest is common advice.

1. **Name the wrong plainly.** Say to yourself what happened and what it cost you. Being hurt does not need to be argued away.
2. **Say what you need.** An acknowledgement, a change in behaviour, or distance. Asking can be done by letter, in person, or in therapy.
3. **Separate forgiving from reconciling.** You can let go of the grudge without trusting the person again or staying in contact. If you are not safe with them, safety comes first.
4. **Try the other person's view, carefully.** Witvliet and colleagues found that cultivating "empathic perspective taking and imagined granting forgiveness" prompted "comparatively lower physiological stress responses" than nursing grudges. That was a short laboratory exercise, and it is not a reason to excuse harm.
5. **Do not wait for an apology you may not get.** That is common advice.
6. **Limit the replaying.** Gangopadhyay and Singh found rumination and grudge on the path from anger to hate. Stopping the rehearsal is one of the common ways to loosen it.
7. **Get help for serious hurts.** Wade and colleagues pooled 54 reports: "Participants receiving explicit forgiveness treatments reported significantly greater forgiveness than participants not receiving treatment", and the treatments "resulted in greater changes in depression, anxiety, and hope than no-treatment conditions". They found "treatment dosage (i.e., longer interventions)" and individual rather than group format predicted more change.

**Sources**

- Wade et al. (2014), "Efficacy of psychotherapeutic interventions to promote forgiveness: a meta-analysis," *Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology* 82(1), 154-170. [doi:10.1037/a0035268](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035268)
- Witvliet, Ludwig and Vander Laan (2001), *Psychological Science* 12(2), 117-123. [doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00320](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00320)
- Gangopadhyay and Singh (2026), *The Journal of Psychology*. [doi:10.1080/00223980.2026.2716095](https://doi.org/10.1080/00223980.2026.2716095)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The Wade et al. abstract says the individual format was ahead of the group format as a predictor of change in forgiveness. Steps 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are common advice, not tied to a study read here.
