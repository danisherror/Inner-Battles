Resentment

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here supports forgiveness work with a professional for serious hurts. The rest is common advice.

  1. Name the wrong plainly. Say to yourself what happened and what it cost you. Being hurt does not need to be argued away.
  2. Say what you need. An acknowledgement, a change in behaviour, or distance. Asking can be done by letter, in person, or in therapy.
  3. Separate forgiving from reconciling. You can let go of the grudge without trusting the person again or staying in contact. If you are not safe with them, safety comes first.
  4. Try the other person's view, carefully. Witvliet and colleagues found that cultivating "empathic perspective taking and imagined granting forgiveness" prompted "comparatively lower physiological stress responses" than nursing grudges. That was a short laboratory exercise, and it is not a reason to excuse harm.
  5. Do not wait for an apology you may not get. That is common advice.
  6. Limit the replaying. Gangopadhyay and Singh found rumination and grudge on the path from anger to hate. Stopping the rehearsal is one of the common ways to loosen it.
  7. Get help for serious hurts. Wade and colleagues pooled 54 reports: "Participants receiving explicit forgiveness treatments reported significantly greater forgiveness than participants not receiving treatment", and the treatments "resulted in greater changes in depression, anxiety, and hope than no-treatment conditions". They found "treatment dosage (i.e., longer interventions)" and individual rather than group format predicted more change.

Sources

  • Wade et al. (2014), "Efficacy of psychotherapeutic interventions to promote forgiveness: a meta-analysis," Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology 82(1), 154-170. doi:10.1037/a0035268 (external link)
  • Witvliet, Ludwig and Vander Laan (2001), Psychological Science 12(2), 117-123. doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00320 (external link)
  • Gangopadhyay and Singh (2026), The Journal of Psychology. doi:10.1080/00223980.2026.2716095 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The Wade et al. abstract says the individual format was ahead of the group format as a predictor of change in forgiveness. Steps 1, 2, 3, 5 and 6 are common advice, not tied to a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita points to forgiveness, evenness toward friend and foe, and a mind that is a friend.

  1. Be forgiving. Gita 12.13-14 describes those who are "free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate", and "ever-forgiving".
  2. Look at all evenly. Gita 6.9: "The yogis look upon all—well-wishers, friends, foes, the pious, and the sinners—with an impartial intellect."
  3. Make the mind a friend. Gita 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The verses are about forgiveness and impartiality in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for serious hurt. They are not a call to stay near someone who harms you.

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to seeing the same Self in everyone, and to the end of difference.

  1. See beings in the Self. Isha 6: "And he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, he never turns away from it."
  2. Look at the unity. Isha 7: "When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"
  3. Drop the sense of difference. Katha 2.1.11: "Even by the mind this (Brahman) is to be obtained, and then there is no difference whatsoever."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. These verses are about the Self and unity, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for serious hurt. They are not a call to stay near someone who harms you.

Christianity

The Bible's counsel is to deal with anger quickly, to forgive, and to leave vengeance alone.

  1. Do not let it settle. Ephesians 4:26: "do not let the sun go down on your anger".
  2. Put it away and forgive. Ephesians 4:31-32: "Be kind to one another, tenderhearted, forgiving one another, as God in Christ has forgiven you."
  3. Bear with each other. Colossians 3:13: "Bear with one another and, if anyone has a complaint against another, forgive each other".
  4. Leave vengeance. Romans 12:19: "Beloved, never avenge yourselves, but leave room for the wrath of God". And 12:21: "Do not be overcome by evil, but overcome evil with good."
  5. Keep forgiving. Peter asks how often to forgive, "As many as seven times?" and Jesus answers: "Not seven times, but, I tell you, seventy-seven times" (Matthew 18:21-22).

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. These verses are about forgiveness in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for serious hurt. Romans 12:18 adds "so far as it depends on you, live peaceably with all", and none of these verses asks anyone to stay in danger.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts teach answering hatred with its opposite, five ways of getting rid of resentment, and a heart of love without secret hate.

  1. Answer hatred with love. Dhp 5: "For never is hatred laid to rest by hate, it's laid to rest by love: this is an ancient teaching."
  2. Use the five ways. AN 5.161: "You should develop love for an individual you resent." Then compassion, equanimity, "disregard an individual you resent, paying no attention to them", and "focus on the ownership of deeds regarding that individual".
  3. Train the heart. MN 21: "'My mind will not degenerate. I will blurt out no bad words. I will remain full of sympathy, with a heart of love and no secret hate.'"

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. These passages are about hatred and resentment in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for serious hurt. "Disregard" in AN 5.161 is a method, and none of the passages asks anyone to stay near someone who harms them.

Philosophical

The Stoics' advice is to read the other person generously, to pick the right handle, and not to become like the wrongdoer.

  1. Read the motive. Epictetus, Enchiridion 42: "if he judges from a wrong appearance, he is the person hurt, since he too is the person deceived." So "you will meekly bear a person who reviles you, for you will say upon every occasion, 'It seemed so to him.'"
  2. Pick the handle. Enchiridion 43: "Everything has two handles, the one by which it may be carried, the other by which it cannot. If your brother acts unjustly, don't lay hold on the action by the handle of his injustice, for by that it cannot be carried; but by the opposite, that he is your brother".
  3. Do not copy the wrong. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6: "The best way of avenging thyself is not to become like the wrong doer."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. These are practices for how we meet harm in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for serious hurt. They do not say that a wrong did not happen or ask anyone to stay near someone who harms them.
