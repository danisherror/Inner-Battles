Resentment

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here describe how a grudge is kept going more than where it starts.

  • Anger that is not worked through. Gangopadhyay and Singh's survey of 740 people found that "the relationship between anger and hate is serially mediated by anger rumination and grudge". The authors suggest that "when anger is not regulated effectively, it may intensify through ruminative thinking and unresolved grudges, resulting in hate". This is a self-report survey, and it does not show cause.
  • Replaying the offence. In Witvliet and colleagues' study, people who rehearsed the hurt showed more aversive emotion than people who took the offender's perspective. The study was a short laboratory exercise.

Common explanations, not findings from these papers: feeling wronged and unacknowledged, an apology that never came, a sense of unfairness or powerlessness, and a belief that letting go means saying it did not matter.

Sources

  • Gangopadhyay and Singh (2026), The Journal of Psychology. doi:10.1080/00223980.2026.2716095 (external link)
  • Witvliet, Ludwig and Vander Laan (2001), Psychological Science 12(2), 117-123. doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00320 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita traces anger back to attachment. Gita 2.62: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger."

And 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The verses are about anger arising from attachment, and not about anger kept alive over a wrong. Reading them as the root of a grudge is ours.

  • Bhagavad Gita 2.62 (external link), 2.63 (external link)

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad names seeing difference as what keeps a person circling. Katha 2.1.10: "What is here (visible in the world), the same is there (invisible in Brahman); and what is there, the same is here. He who sees any difference here (between Brahman and the world), goes from death to death."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The verse is about the difference between Brahman and the world, and not about difference between people. Reading it as a root of a grudge, which sets "me" against "the one who wronged me", is ours.

Christianity

The Bible shows resentment growing from a sense of being passed over, and from bitterness left to grow.

  • Favour. Genesis 37:4: "when his brothers saw that their father loved him more than all his brothers, they hated him and could not speak peaceably to him."
  • A root. Hebrews 12:15: "See to it that no one fails to obtain the grace of God, that no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble and through it many become defiled."
  • Anger left overnight. Ephesians 4:26-27: "Be angry but do not sin; do not let the sun go down on your anger, and do not make room for the devil."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. Genesis 37 is about hatred between brothers, and the others are about bitterness and anger in general. Reading them as roots of a grudge is ours, and Joseph's brothers were the ones who wronged him.

  • Genesis 37:3-4 (external link), Hebrews 12:15 (external link), Ephesians 4:26-32 (external link)

Buddhism

The Dhammapada starts from the mind. Dhp 1: "Intention is the leader of things; intention is first, they're made by intention. If with corrupt intent you speak or act, suffering follows you, like a wheel, the ox's foot."

Dhp 3 shows the habit that keeps a grudge: the story told again and again. "'They abused me, they hit me! They beat me, they robbed me!' For those who bear such a grudge, hatred is never laid to rest."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. Dhp 1 is about intention in general, and Dhp 3 about the repeated tale. Reading the repeated tale as the root is ours.

Philosophical

Aristotle says why this kind of anger lasts. Nicomachean Ethics 4.5: "If this does not happen they retain their burden; for owing to its not being obvious no one even reasons with them, and to digest one's anger in oneself takes time."

Epictetus puts the root in what we assume about the other person. Enchiridion 42: "When any person harms you, or speaks badly of you, remember that he acts or speaks from a supposition of its being his duty."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. Aristotle is describing anger that is held in, and Epictetus how we read someone's motives. Reading them together as roots of a grudge is ours, and Epictetus does not say that every harm is innocent.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.5 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)
  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 42 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The Epictetus page names Elizabeth Carter, but the exact edition was not established. The last sentence is ours.
