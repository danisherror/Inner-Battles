Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here links holding on with strain, and forgiving with less of it, and its limits matter.

  • In the body, in the moment. In Witvliet and colleagues' study, "Unforgiving thoughts prompted more aversive emotion, and significantly higher corrugator (brow) electromyogram (EMG), skin conductance, heart rate, and blood pressure changes from baseline", and some effects "persisted after imagery into the recovery periods". "Forgiving thoughts prompted greater perceived control and comparatively lower physiological stress responses." The authors say this suggests "possible mechanisms through which chronic unforgiving responses may erode health".
  • Across many studies. Lee and Enright's meta-analysis of "A hundred and twenty-eight studies (N = 58,531)" found "a significant positive relationship between forgiveness of others and PH" (physical health). The authors add: "Because the results are correlational, more forgiveness interventions may be needed to examine the causal effect".
  • What this does not show. The first study was a short laboratory exercise with imagined offences, and the second is a correlation. Neither shows that a grudge makes a person ill.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: strained relationships, lost sleep, and a good day spoiled by a memory. If resentment is taking over your days, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Witvliet, Ludwig and Vander Laan (2001), Psychological Science 12(2), 117-123. doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00320 (external link)
  • Lee and Enright (2019), "A meta-analysis of the association between forgiveness of others and physical health," Psychology & Health 34(5), 626-643. doi:10.1080/08870446.2018.1554185 (external link)

Both were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full, and the details were checked in Crossref. The size of the Lee and Enright effect is in the abstract but is not quoted here because the text of that part is cut off in the version read. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita says where unchecked anger ends. Gita 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

The translator's commentary on Gita 12.13-14, on the word "ever-forgiving", says: "Devotees never think of punishing wrongdoers for their emotional satisfaction. Harboring such negative thoughts toward others ruins one's own devotion."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The commentary is the translator's, not the verse, and speaks of devotion. Reading it as the toll of a grudge is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not describe the effects of resentment, but they describe grief and bewilderment. The Mundaka Upanishad (3.1.2) says that a person "sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence", and that when he sees the other, "then his grief passes away".

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. The passage is about grief and the Self, and does not mention resentment. Reading it as the toll of a grudge is ours.

Christianity

The Bible shows what bitterness and hatred do.

  • Strife. Proverbs 10:12: "Hatred stirs up strife, but love covers all offenses."
  • Spreading trouble. Hebrews 12:15: "no root of bitterness springs up and causes trouble and through it many become defiled."
  • A list of the damage. Ephesians 4:31: "Put away from you all bitterness and wrath and anger and wrangling and slander, together with all malice."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. These are general statements about hatred and bitterness, and do not describe the toll of a kept grudge on the person who holds it.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name what a kept grudge does. Dhp 3: "For those who bear such a grudge, hatred is never laid to rest."

Dhp 1 says what follows corrupt intent: "suffering follows you, like a wheel, the ox's foot." And Dhp 2 gives the opposite: "If with pure intent you speak or act, happiness follows you like a shadow that never leaves."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. Dhp 1 and 2 are about intention in general. Reading them as the toll and the relief of a grudge is ours.

Sources

Philosophical

Aristotle names the cost of this kind of anger. Nicomachean Ethics 4.5: "Such people are most troublesome to themselves and to their dearest friends."

And he adds: "bad-tempered people are worse to live with."

This is related to resentment and not identical to it. Aristotle is speaking of sulky and bad-tempered people, and his sentences are general observations and not a study of what grudges do. Reading them as the toll of a grudge is ours.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 4.5 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14 (the page credits Ross). The second sentence is printed in the text as "but bad-tempered people are worse to live with", and the page also prints "had-tempered" for "bad-tempered" in an earlier sentence.
