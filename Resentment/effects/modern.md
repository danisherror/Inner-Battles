The research read here links holding on with strain, and forgiving with less of it, and its limits matter.

- **In the body, in the moment.** In Witvliet and colleagues' study, "Unforgiving thoughts prompted more aversive emotion, and significantly higher corrugator (brow) electromyogram (EMG), skin conductance, heart rate, and blood pressure changes from baseline", and some effects "persisted after imagery into the recovery periods". "Forgiving thoughts prompted greater perceived control and comparatively lower physiological stress responses." The authors say this suggests "possible mechanisms through which chronic unforgiving responses may erode health".
- **Across many studies.** Lee and Enright's meta-analysis of "A hundred and twenty-eight studies (N = 58,531)" found "a significant positive relationship between forgiveness of others and PH" (physical health). The authors add: "Because the results are correlational, more forgiveness interventions may be needed to examine the causal effect".
- **What this does not show.** The first study was a short laboratory exercise with imagined offences, and the second is a correlation. Neither shows that a grudge makes a person ill.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: strained relationships, lost sleep, and a good day spoiled by a memory. If resentment is taking over your days, a doctor or therapist can help.

**Sources**

- Witvliet, Ludwig and Vander Laan (2001), *Psychological Science* 12(2), 117-123. [doi:10.1111/1467-9280.00320](https://doi.org/10.1111/1467-9280.00320)
- Lee and Enright (2019), "A meta-analysis of the association between forgiveness of others and physical health," *Psychology & Health* 34(5), 626-643. [doi:10.1080/08870446.2018.1554185](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2018.1554185)
