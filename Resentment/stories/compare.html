Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how resentment works and how people loosen its grip.

Nadia's story

When their mother was ill, Nadia did most of the caring. Her brother Karim visited twice, and at the funeral he said, in front of relatives, that she had "made a fuss". They stopped speaking.

Two years later Nadia could still recite what he had said. She replayed it on her commute. When his name came up at a family dinner she went quiet and left early.

What helped:

  1. She named the wrong. She wrote down what happened and what it had cost her, including the years of care nobody had thanked her for.
  2. She wrote a letter she did not send. It said all of it, and writing it let her see what she wanted: to be acknowledged.
  3. She decided what she would ask for. She told Karim, by message, that the funeral remark had hurt her and that she needed him to know how much the caring had been.
  4. She stopped waiting for the apology. He answered briefly and without saying sorry. She decided she would not keep the tally open.
  5. She separated forgiving from reconciling. She kept her distance from him, and stopped spending her commute rehearsing the quarrel.
  6. She noticed it was not lifting. The replaying eased but the grief behind it did not, and she spoke to her doctor, who referred her to a counsellor.

The memory did not vanish. It stopped running her days.

What it shows: Nadia did not have to pretend nothing had happened, and she did not have to get an apology. Saying what she needed and putting down the tally did what replaying could not.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If resentment is taking over your days, or comes from abuse, please see a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Friend and foe alike

The Gita has no story of a grudge, so this is a portrait from Krishna's teaching and not a tale. In Chapter 12 Krishna describes the person who is dear to him: "Those, who are alike to friend and foe, equipoised in honor and dishonor, cold and heat, joy and sorrow, and are free from all unfavorable association; those who take praise and reproach alike, who are given to silent contemplation, content with what comes their way, without attachment to the place of residence, whose intellect is firmly fixed in Me, and who are full of devotion to Me, such persons are very dear to Me" (12.18-19).

What it shows: The portrait is of a person who does not keep a tally of honour and dishonour, friend and foe. It is about devotion and not about resentment; the link is ours, and the passage should not be read as asking anyone to ignore harm done to them.

Upanishads

Nachiketas and his father's anger

In the Katha Upanishad, the boy Nachiketas asks his father, who is giving away his possessions, "Dear father, to whom wilt thou give me?" He asks a second and a third time. "Then the father replied (angrily): 'I shall give thee unto Death'" (1.1.4).

Nachiketas goes to Death. When Death offers him three boons, the first thing he asks for is about his father: "that Gautama, my father, be pacified, kind, and free from anger towards me; and that he may know me and greet me, when I shall have been dismissed by thee" (1.1.10).

Death answers: "Through my favour Auddâlaki Âru n i, thy father, will know thee, and be again towards thee as he was before. He shall sleep peacefully through the night, and free from anger, after having seen thee freed from the mouth of death" (1.1.11).

What it shows: The first thing Nachiketas asks for is not for himself, but for his father's anger to end. The story is about death and knowledge and not about resentment; it shows a son who has every reason to be hurt, asking that his father be at peace with him.

Christianity

Joseph and his brothers

In Genesis 37 Joseph's brothers hated him "and could not speak peaceably to him" because their father loved him more. They sold him, and years later he became powerful in Egypt. After their father's death they are afraid: "What if Joseph still bears a grudge against us and pays us back in full for all the wrong that we did to him?" (50:15).

They send word that their father asked him to forgive, and they come to him, saying, "We are here as your slaves" (50:16-18). "Joseph wept when they spoke to him."

Joseph answers: "Do not be afraid! Am I in the place of God? Even though you intended to do harm to me, God intended it for good, in order to preserve a numerous people, as he is doing today. So have no fear; I myself will provide for you and your little ones" (50:19-21).

What it shows: Joseph has the power to take revenge, and he does not. The story is about providence and a family and not a model for every situation; Joseph does not say that what they did was acceptable, and the text records what he says and not what it cost him.

Buddhism

Vedehikā and her maid

In MN 21 the Buddha tells of a housewife in Sāvatthī, Vedehikā, who "had this good reputation: 'The housewife Vedehikā is gentle, placid, and calm.'" Her maid Kāḷī wonders: "does she actually have anger in her and just not show it? Or does she have no anger?" She decides to test her mistress.

Kāḷī gets up late in the day, then later, then later still. Each time Vedehikā grows angrier, first scowling, then blurting out angry words, and the third time she "grabbed a door-pin and hit Kāḷī on the head, cracking it open." Kāḷī denounces her to the neighbours, and "Vedehikā got this bad reputation: 'The housewife Vedehikā is fierce, ill-tempered, and not calm at all.'"

The Buddha applies it: "a mendicant may be the gentlest of the gentle, the most placid of the placid, the calmest of the calm, so long as they don't encounter any disagreeable criticism. But it's when they encounter disagreeable criticism that you'll know whether they're really gentle, placid, and calm."

What it shows: Anger that is kept out of sight is still there, and it shows when it is tested. The sutta is about anger and criticism and not about resentment as such; the link is ours. The story includes violence, and it is not a model for how to treat anyone.

Philosophical

Marcus Aurelius meets the difficult people

Marcus Aurelius is not telling a story in Meditations 2, but the opening is a scene of a morning. "Begin the morning by saying to thyself, I shall meet with the busy-body, the ungrateful, arrogant, deceitful, envious, unsocial. All these things happen to them by reason of their ignorance of what is good and evil."

He then says what he will do about it. He knows "the nature of him who does wrong, that it is akin to me", so "I can neither be injured by any of them, for no one can fix on me what is ugly, nor can I be angry with my kinsman, nor hate him". He explains: "we are made for co-operation, like feet, like hands, like eyelids, like the rows of the upper and lower teeth."

What it shows: Marcus prepares for the difficult people before they arrive, and decides in advance not to hate them. The passage is about the Stoic view of other people and not about resentment as such; the link is ours, and it is one philosopher's discipline, which does not say what to do when someone has really harmed you.
