Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how anxiety works and how people loosen its grip.

Amira's story

Amira was asked to present her team's results at a meeting of forty people. Her stomach dropped. Over the next week she rehearsed the ways it could go wrong: her voice shaking, a question she could not answer, everyone's faces.

She considered telling her manager she was ill. Each time she thought of backing out she felt a wave of relief, and then, an hour later, a heavier dread about the next time.

What helped:

  1. She named it. "This is anxiety. It is loud, and it is not a forecast."
  2. She stopped treating avoidance as the answer. The relief from backing out was short, and the dread came back bigger.
  3. She went in small steps. She presented to two colleagues, then to her team of eight, before the big meeting.
  4. She slowed her breathing for a minute before she began, and kept one page of notes.
  5. She noticed it was not lifting. Weeks of poor sleep and dread before any meeting led her to her doctor, who referred her to a therapist.

The anxiety did not vanish. It got smaller, and she presented.

What it shows: Avoiding the thing taught her alarm that it was right. Small, planned steps, and help when the dread would not ease, did more than waiting to feel ready.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If anxiety is intense or constant, please see a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Arjuna sees the universal form

In Chapter 11 of the Gita, Arjuna asks to see Krishna's universal form, and Krishna grants it. Arjuna is overwhelmed: "Having seen Your universal form that I had never seen before, I feel great joy. And yet, my mind trembles with fear. Please have mercy on me and again show me Your pleasing form" (11.45).

He asks to see Krishna in the form he knows: "I wish to see You in Your four-armed form, carrying the mace and disc, and wearing the crown" (11.46).

Krishna answers: "Be neither afraid nor bewildered on seeing this terrible form of Mine. Be free from fear and with a cheerful heart, behold Me once again in My personal form" (11.49). Sanjay then reports that Krishna "further consoled the frightened Arjun by assuming His gentle (two-armed) form" (11.50).

What it shows: Fear and joy arrive together in Arjuna, and the answer is reassurance and a form he can bear. The chapter is about the vision of the cosmic form and not about anxiety as such; the story is read here for how fear is met.

Upanishads

Nachiketas goes to Death

The Katha Upanishad opens with a boy and a rash word. Nachiketas's father is giving away his possessions at a sacrifice. The boy asks: "Dear father, to whom wilt thou give me?" He says it a second and a third time. "Then the father replied (angrily): 'I shall give thee unto Death'" (1.1.4).

Nachiketas goes. He says: "I go as the first, at the head of many (who have still to die); I go in the midst of many (who are now dying)" (1.1.5). And: "A mortal ripens like corn, like corn he springs up again" (1.1.6).

Yama, Death, is away for three nights, and the boy is not received. When Yama returns he offers three boons. Nachiketas asks first that his father be "pacified, kind, and free from anger towards me" (1.1.10), and then speaks of the heaven-world: "there is no fear" (1.1.12).

What it shows: The boy faces what most people fear, and does not run from it. He asks for his father's peace before anything for himself. The story is about death and knowledge, and not about anxiety; its link to fear is ours.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.1.4-12 (Max Müller's translation)

Quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. Christianity

Peter on the water

In Matthew 14 the disciples are in a boat "battered by the waves", with the wind against them. Jesus comes toward them walking on the sea. They are terrified and think he is a ghost, and he says: "Take heart, it is I; do not be afraid" (14:24-27).

Peter answers: "Lord, if it is you, command me to come to you on the water." Jesus says, "Come." Peter gets out of the boat and walks toward Jesus. "But when he noticed the strong wind, he became frightened, and, beginning to sink, he cried out, 'Lord, save me!'" (14:28-30).

"Jesus immediately reached out his hand and caught him, saying to him, 'You of little faith, why did you doubt?'" (14:31). When they got into the boat, the wind ceased.

What it shows: Peter walks while he is looking at Jesus, and sinks when he notices the wind, and in both cases the help is a hand held out at once. The story is about faith and not about anxiety as a condition. It does not blame him for being afraid; it shows fear arriving and a hand reaching out.

Sources

  • Matthew 14:22-33 (external link)

The quotations were read in the NRSV on BibleGateway on 2026-11-14. Buddhism

The Buddha waits for fear

In MN 4 the Buddha tells a brahmin how he faced fear and dread in remote forest lodgings before his awakening. He recalls: "As I was staying there a deer came by, or a peacock snapped a twig, or the wind rustled the leaves. Then I thought, 'Is this that fear and dread coming?'"

He then reasons: "Why do I always meditate expecting that fear to come? Why don't I get rid of that fear and dread just as it comes, while remaining just as I am?"

So he does. "Then that fear and dread came upon me as I was walking. I didn't stand still or sit down or lie down until I had got rid of that fear and dread while walking." He does the same standing, sitting and lying down.

What it shows: The Buddha does not wait for fear in a safe posture, and he does not run from it; he faces it where he is. The sutta is about fear in the wilderness and not about anxiety as a condition. It shows what facing fear can look like, and it is not advice to do this alone.

Philosophical

Socrates on the fear of death

In Plato's Apology, Socrates is on trial for his life. He tells the jury he will not abandon his post because of fear: "if I who, when I was ordered by the generals whom you chose to command me at Potidaea and Amphipolis and Delium, remained where they placed me, like any other man, facing death; if, I say, now... I were to desert my post through fear of death, or any other fear; that would indeed be strange".

Then he questions the fear itself: "For this fear of death is indeed the pretence of wisdom, and not real wisdom, being the appearance of knowing the unknown; since no one knows whether death, which they in their fear apprehend to be the greatest evil, may not be the greatest good."

What it shows: Socrates does not say there is nothing to fear. He says that the fear of death claims to know what no one knows. The speech is about death and about duty and not about anxiety as a condition; its link to fear in general is that he questions what the fear assumes.
