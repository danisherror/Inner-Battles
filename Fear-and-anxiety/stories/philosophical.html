Socrates on the fear of death

In Plato's Apology, Socrates is on trial for his life. He tells the jury he will not abandon his post because of fear: "if I who, when I was ordered by the generals whom you chose to command me at Potidaea and Amphipolis and Delium, remained where they placed me, like any other man, facing death; if, I say, now... I were to desert my post through fear of death, or any other fear; that would indeed be strange".

Then he questions the fear itself: "For this fear of death is indeed the pretence of wisdom, and not real wisdom, being the appearance of knowing the unknown; since no one knows whether death, which they in their fear apprehend to be the greatest evil, may not be the greatest good."

What it shows: Socrates does not say there is nothing to fear. He says that the fear of death claims to know what no one knows. The speech is about death and about duty and not about anxiety as a condition; its link to fear in general is that he questions what the fear assumes.
