Modern

The WHO page describes what anxiety disorders do to daily life and health.

  • Everyday life. "Anxiety disorders interfere with daily activities and can impair a person's family, social and school or working life."
  • Other conditions. "Anxiety disorders increase the risk for depression and substance use disorders as well as the risk of suicidal thoughts and behaviours."
  • Physical health. "Many of the impacts of anxiety (such as physical tension, nervous system hyperactivity or harmful use of alcohol) are also known risk factors for diseases such as cardiovascular disease."
  • Getting help. Only "about 1 in 4 people in need (27.6%) receive any treatment", although "highly effective treatments for anxiety disorders exist".

These describe anxiety disorders, not the ordinary worry everyone has. Commonly described effects, not findings from the WHO page: avoiding places and tasks, poor sleep, irritability and exhaustion from being on guard.

If you are thinking of harming yourself, contact your local emergency services or a crisis line now. For anxiety that is intense or constant, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • World Health Organization, Anxiety disorders fact sheet (external link)

The quotations were read on that page on 2026-11-14. The WHO's figures will change as the page is updated. The last paragraph of effects is common description and cites no study.

Hinduism

At the start of the Gita, Arjuna shows what fear does to the body and mind. Gita 1.28: "my limbs are giving way and my mouth is drying up."

Gita 1.29-31: "My whole body shudders; my hair is standing on end. My bow, the Gāṇḍīv, is slipping from my hand, and my skin is burning all over. My mind is in quandary and whirling in confusion; I am unable to hold myself steady any longer."

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. Arjuna's distress comes from the sight of his kinsmen across the battlefield, and the text describes grief and confusion as well as fear. Reading it as showing the toll of fear and anxiety is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not describe the effects of fear. They describe grief and bewilderment, and what lifts them. The Mundaka Upanishad (3.1.2) says that a person "sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence", and that when he sees the other, "then his grief passes away".

Katha 1.2.20 says: "A man who is free from desires and free from grief, sees the majesty of the Self by the grace of the Creator." And 1.2.22: "The wise who knows the Self as bodiless within the bodies, as unchanging among changing things, as great and omnipresent, does never grieve."

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The passages are about grief and the Self, and do not mention fear except where it is said to end. Reading them as the toll of fear is ours.

Christianity

The Bible shows fear and anxiety weighing on people.

  • Weight on the heart. Proverbs 12:25: "Anxiety weighs down the human heart".
  • Terror. When the disciples see Jesus walking on the sea: "they were terrified, saying, 'It is a ghost!' And they cried out in fear" (Matthew 14:26).
  • Sinking. Peter steps onto the water, but "when he noticed the strong wind, he became frightened, and, beginning to sink, he cried out, 'Lord, save me!'" (Matthew 14:30).

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. These are scenes of fear in danger, and a proverb about the heart, and not a description of anxiety as a condition. Reading them as its toll is ours.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts describe fear as heavy and shared by everyone.

  • Grief at death. Snp 3.8 shows people wailing as "mortals are led away one by one, like a cow to the slaughter". It says "the attentive do not grieve, for they understand the way of the world".
  • Lament that does not help. The same sutta says: "For one whose path you do not know—not whence they came nor where they went—you lament in vain, seeing neither end."
  • Sorrow and fear together. Dhp 212: "Sorrow springs from what we hold dear, fear springs from what we hold dear".

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The Dart is about death and grief, and Dhp 212 about attachment. They do not describe the toll of anxiety, and reading them as doing so is ours.

Philosophical

The philosophers describe what fear does to the mind.

  • It sets us thinking. Aristotle, Rhetoric 2.5: "fear sets us thinking what can be done, which of course nobody does when things are hopeless". Fear, on his account, can prompt action.
  • It runs ahead of events. Seneca, Letters 13.10: "It is likely that some troubles will befall us; but it is not a present fact. How often has the unexpected happened! How often has the expected never come to pass! And even though it is ordained to be, what does it avail to run out to meet your suffering?"
  • It is more than the thing itself. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.36: "neither the future nor the past pains thee, but only the present".

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. These are reflections on fear in general, and none is a study of anxiety. Seneca's and Marcus's remarks do not claim that the future is never painful.
