Fear and anxiety

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Fear is the alarm that goes off at a threat, and anxiety is the same alarm sounding about a threat that may come. Fear is usually tied to something in front of you; anxiety tends to look ahead and can be vague ("what if...?"). Some fear and anxiety is normal and useful. It becomes a problem when it is out of proportion or will not switch off.

The World Health Organization says: "Everyone can feel anxious sometimes, but people with anxiety disorders often experience fear and worry that is both intense and excessive." These feelings, it adds, "are difficult to control, cause significant distress and can last a long time if untreated". Of people with an anxiety disorder it says: "Usually they avoid the situations that make them anxious."

It is common. The WHO estimates that "5.8% of the global population currently experience an anxiety disorder", and that in 2023 "470 million people in the world had an anxiety disorder, making anxiety disorders the most common of all mental disorders".

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from the WHO page: a racing heart before a talk, a mind that rehearses disasters, putting off a phone call, lying awake with "what if". Worry that loops on one subject overlaps with overthinking.

Feeling anxious is not a failing, and an anxiety disorder is a health condition that can be treated. If your fear or anxiety is intense, constant or getting in the way of daily life, please talk to a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for anxiety as the modern lens describes it, but the Gita speaks of fear and of its absence. Gita 2.56: "One whose mind remains undisturbed amidst misery, who does not crave for pleasure, and who is free from attachment, fear, and anger, is called a sage of steady wisdom."

Gita 16.1-3 begins its list of "saintly virtues of those endowed with a divine nature" with "fearlessness". And Gita 18.30 speaks of the intellect that understands "what is to be feared and what is not to be feared".

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The Gita is describing a steady mind and its qualities, and it does not describe anxiety as a condition. Reading these verses as speaking to it is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name anxiety, and they speak of fear mostly by its absence. In the Katha Upanishad, the boy Nachiketas says of the world of heaven: "In the heaven-world there is no fear; thou art not there, O Death, and no one is afraid on account of old age" (1.1.12).

Katha 2.1.12 describes the person who knows the Self: "The person (purusha), of the size of a thumb, stands in the middle of the Self (body ?), as lord of the past and the future, and henceforward fears no more."

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The passages are about death and knowledge of the Self, and do not describe anxiety as a condition. Reading them as speaking to it is ours.

Christianity

The Bible does not hide fear and anxiety. Psalm 56:3-4: "when I am afraid, I put my trust in you. In God, whose word I praise, in God I trust; I am not afraid; what can flesh do to me?"

Proverbs 12:25 names anxiety: "Anxiety weighs down the human heart, but a good word cheers it up." And Psalm 94:19: "When the cares of my heart are many, your consolations cheer my soul."

This is related to fear and anxiety as the modern lens describes them, and not identical to them. The texts are prayers and proverbs about trust and care, and do not describe anxiety as a condition or tell anyone to stop feeling it. An anxious person is not asked to hide it.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts treat fear as something that can be met and trained. Dhp 39 describes a person with a steady mind: "One whose mind is not festering, whose heart is undamaged, who's given up right and wrong, alert, has nothing to fear."

In the Fear and Dread sutta (MN 4) the Buddha says that some people who go to remote places "summon unskillful fear and dread because of these defects in their conduct". Fear here is something the mind brings to the place.

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. Dhp 39 describes the end of fear in an awakened person, and MN 4 describes fear in the wilderness; neither describes anxiety as a condition.

Philosophical

Aristotle gives a definition of fear. In the Rhetoric (2.5): "Fear may be defined as a pain or disturbance due to a mental picture of some destructive or painful evil in the future."

In the Nicomachean Ethics (3.6) he says people "even define fear as expectation of evil", and that not all fear is wrong: "to fear some things is even right and noble, and it is base not to fear them".

This fits the philosophical lens closely, since Aristotle's definition is of fear directly. It is a definition of fear in general and not of anxiety as a disorder, and it says nothing about what to do about it.
