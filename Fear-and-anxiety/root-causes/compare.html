Fear and anxiety

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The WHO says that "Anxiety disorders, like other mental health conditions, result from a complex interaction of social, psychological and biological factors." It adds that "people who have lived through abuse, severe losses or other adverse experiences are more likely to develop one", and that anxiety disorders "are closely related to and affected by physical health".

Craske and colleagues, writing about exposure therapy, say that "anxious individuals show deficits in the mechanisms believed to underlie exposure therapy, such as inhibitory learning". In plain terms, learning that a feared thing turns out to be safe may be harder for some anxious people. The abstract does not say this is what causes anxiety.

Common explanations, not findings from these sources:

  • A cautious alarm. Some people's threat alarm fires early and often.
  • Avoidance. Steering clear of what scares you brings relief, which can teach the alarm that it was right.
  • Uncertainty. Not knowing how something will turn out leaves room for the mind to fill in the worst.

No single cause is established by these sources, and having anxiety is not a sign of weakness or of having done something wrong.

Sources

  • World Health Organization, Anxiety disorders fact sheet (external link)
  • Craske et al. (2014), "Maximizing exposure therapy: an inhibitory learning approach," Behaviour Research and Therapy 58, 10-23. doi:10.1016/j.brat.2014.04.006 (external link)

The WHO page was read on 2026-11-14. Craske et al. was read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not the full paper, and its publication details were checked in Crossref. The three common explanations cite no study.

Hinduism

The Gita pairs fear with attachment. Gita 2.56 describes the sage as "free from attachment, fear, and anger", and Gita 4.10 speaks of those "free from attachment, fear, and anger, becoming fully absorbed in Me, and taking refuge in Me".

Gita 18.30 adds judgement: the intellect in the mode of goodness understands "what is to be feared and what is not to be feared". Reading this as meaning that fear grows from mistaking what is to be feared is ours.

The verses do not say that attachment causes fear. They list the two together, and the link between them is the reader's inference.

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The Gita is speaking of the steady mind in general, and not of an anxious one.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad puts fear of death at the centre of the human situation, and speaks to it. Katha 1.2.18: "The knowing (Self) is not born, it dies not; it sprang from nothing, nothing sprang from it. The Ancient is unborn, eternal, everlasting; he is not killed, though the body is killed."

And 1.2.19: "If the killer thinks that he kills, if the killed thinks that he is killed, they do not understand; for this one does not kill, nor is that one killed."

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The verses are about the Self and death, and say nothing of anxiety or of where it comes from. Reading them as naming the fear of death as a root of fear is ours.

Christianity

The Bible touches several roots of fear.

  • Fear of other people. Proverbs 29:25: "The fear of others lays a snare, but one who trusts in the Lord is secure."
  • Fear of punishment. 1 John 4:18: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear; for fear has to do with punishment, and whoever fears has not reached perfection in love."
  • A sense of being left alone. In the storm, the disciples wake Jesus with: "Teacher, do you not care that we are perishing?" (Mark 4:38).

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. These texts speak of fear of others, of punishment and of danger, and not of anxiety as a condition. Reading them as roots of anxiety is ours, and 1 John 4:18 should not be read as saying that being afraid is a failing of love.

Sources

  • Proverbs 29:25 (external link), 1 John 4:18 (external link), Mark 4:35-41 (external link)

Buddhism

MN 4 names causes of unskillful fear in those who go to the wilderness: faults in conduct, in livelihood, and a lack of understanding. Each time the wording is the same: they "summon unskillful fear and dread because of the defects of witlessness and stupidity", or of their conduct or livelihood.

The Dart (Snp 3.8) points to a deeper root: mortality. "Unforeseen and unknown is the extent of this mortal life— hard and short and bound to pain. There is no way that those born will not die."

Dhp 212 links fear to what we hold dear: "fear springs from what we hold dear".

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. MN 4 is about fear in the forest, Snp 3.8 about death, and Dhp 212 about attachment. Reading them together as roots of anxiety is ours.

Philosophical

The Stoics place the root of fear in how we judge things, and in imagining what has not happened.

Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things."

Seneca, Letters 13.4: "There are more things, Lucilius, likely to frighten us than there are to crush us; we suffer more often in imagination than in reality." And 13.8: "it is more often the case that we are troubled by our apprehensions".

This is related to fear and anxiety and not identical to them. The Stoics are speaking of fear and judgement in general, and not of anxiety as a condition. They do not say that anxious people are imagining danger that is not there, and some dangers are real.

  • Seneca, Moral letters to Lucilius, Letter 13 (external link) (Richard Gummere's translation)
