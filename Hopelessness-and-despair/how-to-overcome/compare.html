Inner battle

Hopelessness and despair

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes the problem and mostly tests no remedy. Maier and Seligman report, in animals, that passivity "can be overcome by learning control", and their abstract only speculates about "how to treat depression". The rest is common advice.

  1. Say what it is. "I feel hopeless" is a report about now. "Nothing will ever change" is a prediction, and low mood makes poor predictions. That is common advice.
  2. Tell someone. A friend, family member, doctor or therapist. Hopelessness tends to want silence, and saying it aloud often loosens it. That is common advice.
  3. Get treatment. Doctors and therapists treat low mood and hopelessness, and persistent low mood may be depression. That is a standard statement, not tied to a study read here.
  4. Make one small thing. A short walk, a message, a meal. Maier and Seligman's animal work is about control, and linking it to small acts is the site's own idea and is untested here.
  5. Look after the basics. Sleep, food, daylight, company. That is common advice.
  6. Keep safe. Hopelessness can be a sign of depression, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If it lasts or keeps coming back, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Hinduism

The Gita offers self-effort and patience, and it should be read with care.

  1. Rise. 2.3: "Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise". This is said to a healthy warrior, and for someone who is ill it is not an instruction to try harder.
  2. Use the mind as an ally. 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."
  3. Practise. 6.35: "the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. These verses are about the disciplined life and do not speak of depression, and Krishna in 6.35 agrees that the mind is hard to restrain. Reading them as a way through hopelessness is ours, and they do not replace a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Upanishads

The Upanishads answer with strength, steadiness and knowledge, and not with a technique. Mundaka 3.2.4: "Nor is that Self to be gained by one who is destitute of strength, or without earnestness, or without right meditation. But if a wise man strives after it by those means (by strength, earnestness, and right meditation), then his Self enters the home of Brahman."

Chandogya 7.14 holds that hope can be meditated on: "Meditate on hope."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The first passage is about gaining the Self and could be misread as blaming a person who lacks strength, and it is not that. Neither passage turns into steps, and the site makes no claim about how to practise them. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Christianity

The Christian texts counsel remembering, hoping and waiting, and they do not demand cheerfulness.

  1. Talk to yourself. Psalm 42:5: "Why are you cast down, O my soul, and why are you disquieted within me? Hope in God, for I shall again praise him, my help and my God."
  2. Call to mind. Lamentations 3:21-23: "But this I call to mind, and therefore I have hope: The steadfast love of the Lord never ceases, his mercies never come to an end; they are new every morning".
  3. Wait. Lamentations 3:26: "It is good that one should wait quietly for the salvation of the Lord."
  4. Let endurance work. Romans 5:3-5: "affliction produces endurance, and endurance produces character, and character produces hope, and hope does not put us to shame".

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. These verses do not tell anyone to feel hopeful at once, and Romans 5:3 should not be read as saying that suffering is good for you. Reading them as a way through hopelessness is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts counsel good company, a path, and good done again and again.

  1. Seek good friends. Kisāgotamī's verses begin: "Associating with good friends, even a fool becomes astute."
  2. Take the path. SN 56.11 names "the noble truth of the practice that leads to the cessation of suffering", and says it "should be developed".
  3. Do good again and again. Dhp 118: "If you do something good, do it again and again, set your heart on it, for piling up goodness is joyful."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The first is from a nun's verses on grief, the second names the path without describing it here, and Dhp 118 is advice on conduct. Reading them as a way through hopelessness is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Philosophical

The philosophers' counsel is small steps, the present, and gentle remedies first.

  1. Begin with the day. Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.1: "In the morning when thou risest unwillingly, let this thought be present- I am rising to the work of a human being."
  2. Come back to the present. Seneca, Letters 5: "we do not adapt ourselves to the present, but send our thoughts a long way ahead."
  3. Start gently. Boethius' Philosophy: "it is time for thee to take and drain a draught, soft and pleasant to the taste, which, as it penetrates within, may prepare the way for stronger potions."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. Marcus is addressing reluctance to rise and not depression, and for a depressed person the advice to get up and work is not enough. Reading these passages as a way through hopelessness is ours, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.
