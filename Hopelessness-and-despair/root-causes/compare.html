Modern

The sources read here point to a few causes, and are limited.

  • A way of explaining bad events. Abela's study found that "A depressogenic attributional style interacted with negative events to predict increases in depressive symptoms in seventh-grade children but not in third-grade children." It also reports that "None of these interactions were mediated by hopelessness", so that study did not find hopelessness to be the route. It followed children for six weekly assessments.
  • Prolonged hard events. Maier and Seligman report that "Passivity in response to shock is not learned. It is the default, unlearned response to prolonged aversive events", and that it "can be overcome by learning control". This is work on animals.
  • Depression itself. Common description, not from these papers: low mood makes the future look bleak, and the bleakness then feels like evidence.
  • Loss, illness, isolation and long strain. Also common description, not from these papers.

Hopelessness is not a character flaw. Hopelessness can be a sign of depression, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If it lasts or keeps coming back, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

  • Abela (2001), Journal of Abnormal Child Psychology 29, 241-254. doi:10.1023/a:1010333815728 (external link)
  • Maier and Seligman (2016), Psychological Review 123(4), 349-367. doi:10.1037/rev0000033 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

Hinduism

The Gita's account of Arjuna's collapse names attachment and delusion. The translator's commentary on 1.47 says: "He had now surrendered to his grief and slumped into deep dejection. His condition was the creation of his own material attachments".

After the teaching Arjuna says: "by Your grace my illusion has been dispelled, and I am situated in knowledge. I am now free from doubts" (18.73), and Krishna had asked at the start: "how has this delusion overcome you in this hour of peril?" (2.2).

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The cause given is attachment and illusion, in one man's situation, and the commentary's wording is blunt. It should not be read as saying that people who feel hopeless have brought it on themselves, and low mood can have other causes. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Upanishads

Chandogya 7 builds a ladder of "better than", and at its end it puts the root of acting in a condition of the self. In 7.19-7.22 Sanatkumara says: "When one believes, then one perceives." "When one attends on a tutor (spiritual guide), then one believes." "When one performs all sacred duties, then one attends really on a tutor." And: "When one obtains bliss (in oneself), then one performs duties. One who does not obtain bliss, does not perform duties."

The chain runs from bliss back to the self's condition, and says that where it is lacking, nothing follows.

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The passage is about the order in which knowledge arises, and says nothing of depression or despair. Reading it as a root of hopelessness is ours, and it should not be used to blame anyone who finds it hard to act. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Christianity

Lamentations 3 shows how brooding feeds despair: "The thought of my affliction and my homelessness is wormwood and gall! My soul continually thinks of it and is bowed down within me."

In 1 Kings 19 the prophet Elijah, after a threat, is afraid: "he got up and fled for his life". In the wilderness he "came and sat down under a solitary broom tree. He asked that he might die, 'It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life, for I am no better than my ancestors.'"

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The causes named are affliction, fear and a sense of failure. The text does not give a single cause, and reading these as roots of hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name craving as the origin of suffering. SN 56.11: "Now this is the noble truth of the origin of suffering. It's the craving that leads to future lives, mixed up with relishing and greed, taking pleasure there wherever it alights. That is, craving for sensual pleasures, craving for continued existence, and craving for nonexistence."

The Buddha also lists, among kinds of suffering, "not getting what you wish for".

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The sutta explains suffering in general, and the origin it names is craving, which does not match the usual causes of a depressed mood. It should not be read as saying that people who feel hopeless have caused it by wanting. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Philosophical

Seneca locates the trouble in looking ahead. Letters 5: "hope and fear, dissimilar as they are, keep step together; fear follows hope." And: "the chief cause of both these ills is that we do not adapt ourselves to the present, but send our thoughts a long way ahead."

Boethius' Philosophy gives a second cause: "all sudden changes of circumstances bring inevitably a certain commotion of spirit."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. Seneca is writing about hope and fear and does not recommend giving up hope; the same letter quotes Hecato: "Cease to hope," he says, "and you will cease to fear", which is about calming desire and is not advice to despair. Reading the passages as causes of hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.
