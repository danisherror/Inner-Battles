Inner battle

Hopelessness and despair

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes effects on mood, and on risk. Abela's study reports that a depressogenic way of explaining events predicted "increases in depressive symptoms". Maier and Seligman describe passivity: in their animals it is mediated by an area of the brain that "in turn inhibits escape", and "can be overcome by learning control".

A meta-analysis by Ribeiro and colleagues looked at depression and hopelessness as risk factors for suicidal thoughts and behaviours. It found that "Overall prediction was weaker than anticipated, with weighted mean odds ratios of 1.96 (1.81-2.13) for ideation, 1.63 (1.55-1.72) for attempt and 1.33 (1.18-1.49) for death". In plain terms, these factors went with higher odds, yet the authors call the prediction "weaker than anticipated", so hopelessness alone does not tell anyone what a person will do, and the abstract adds that methodological limits were prominent.

Common effects, as people describe them and not findings from these papers: pulling away from people, doing less, sleep and appetite changing, and the future shrinking to the next hour.

Hopelessness is a good reason to reach out, and not a reason to wait. Hopelessness can be a sign of depression, and it is nothing to be ashamed of. If it lasts or keeps coming back, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Hinduism

The Gita shows what hopelessness does to action. Arjuna "cast aside his bow and arrows, and sank into the seat of his chariot" (1.47). The commentary says: "Arjun's reluctance to fight the war had now reached its climax. He had now surrendered to his grief and slumped into deep dejection."

The other side is the end of the teaching, in 18.73: "I am now free from doubts, and I shall act according to Your instructions."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The effect shown is stopping, and the recovery comes through a long teaching, not a quick fix. The text shows that dejection can end, and does not say how long it takes. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Upanishads

The Upanishads link hope and action. Chandogya 7.14: "Fired by hope does memory read the sacred hymns, perform sacrifices, desire sons and cattle, desire this world and the other." In 7.22: "When one obtains bliss (in oneself), then one performs duties. One who does not obtain bliss, does not perform duties."

The effect named is that without the inner condition, action does not follow.

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. Neither passage speaks of despair, and the second is about knowledge and not about mood. Reading them as effects of hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Christianity

Psalm 88 describes the effects without softening them: "You have caused my companions to shun me; you have made me a thing of horror to them. I am shut in so that I cannot escape; my eye grows dim through sorrow."

It ends without relief: "You have caused friend and neighbor to shun me; my companions are in darkness."

Lamentations 3 adds the loss of happiness: "I have forgotten what happiness is".

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The Bible keeps a prayer that does not resolve, and does not say it should. Reading it as a description of hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts show what loss does. In the verses of Kisāgotamī (Thig 10.1) she says: "I was on the road, near to giving birth, when I saw my husband dead. I gave birth there on the road before I'd reached my own house. My two children have died, and on the road my husband lies dead".

The teacher answers: "Oh woe is you whose family is lost, the suffering you have undergone has no measure".

The poem includes lines about women harming themselves, which are left out here.

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The poem is about grief after death and does not name hopelessness, and reading it as an effect of despair is ours. It involves the deaths of children and may be hard to read. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Philosophical

Boethius describes what grief did to him: "Old? Ah yes; swift, ere I knew it, / By these sorrows on me pressed / Age hath come; lo, Grief hath bid me / Wear the garb that fits her best." And: "And the skin hangs loose and shrivelled / On this sorrow-shrunken frame."

Philosophy names a further effect: "thou also for awhile hast been parted from thy mind's tranquillity."

This is related to hopelessness and not identical to it. The song is a poet's description of grief, and the lines that follow it, which long for death, are not quoted. Reading the passage as an effect of hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.
