Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how hopelessness works and how people find a way through it.

Priya's story

After a redundancy and months of searching for work, Priya stopped applying. "Nothing is going to change" had stopped feeling like a thought and had started feeling like a fact. She slept late, let messages pile up, and told her sister she was fine.

What helped:

  1. She said what it was. "I feel hopeless" was about now. "Nothing will change" was a prediction made by a tired mind.
  2. She told someone. Her sister came round and did not try to fix it, only stayed.
  3. She saw a doctor. She said that some nights she did not want to be here. It was the hardest sentence she had said, and the doctor took it seriously and arranged treatment and a follow-up.
  4. She made one small thing. A ten-minute walk, then one email a day.
  5. She looked after the basics. She went to bed at a fixed time, and kept the curtains open.
  6. She kept someone close. She and her sister spoke every evening for a month.

It took months and it was not a straight line, and some days she did only the walk.

What it shows: Priya did not argue herself out of hopelessness. Telling people, and getting treatment, gave her a future to act in again.

Hinduism

Arjuna sinks, and rises

In Gita 1.47 Arjuna, "his mind in distress and overwhelmed with grief", "cast aside his bow and arrows, and sank into the seat of his chariot." Krishna answers: "it does not befit you to yield to this unmanliness. Give up such petty weakness of heart and arise" (2.3).

Later, answering a remark about how hard the mind is to hold, Krishna says: "what you say is correct; the mind is indeed very difficult to restrain. But by practice and detachment, it can be controlled" (6.35).

At the end Arjuna says: "by Your grace my illusion has been dispelled, and I am situated in knowledge. I am now free from doubts, and I shall act according to Your instructions" (18.73).

What it shows: The man who sat down gets up again, and the way between is a long conversation. This is a moment in a dialogue and not a tale of despair, and linking it to hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Upanishads

Narada climbs the ladder

In Chandogya 7 Narada asks Sanatkumara to teach him. Each time Sanatkumara names something and says to meditate on it, Narada asks: "Sir, is there something better than" it, and the answer is "Yes".

In 7.14: "Hope (āśā) is better than memory." Narada asks: "Sir, is there something better than hope?" "Yes, there is something better than hope." In 7.15: "Spirit (prâṇa) is better than hope."

What it shows: Hope is a step on a ladder and not its top, and Narada keeps asking. This is a moment in a dialogue and not a tale of despair, and linking it to hopelessness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Elijah under the broom tree

In 1 Kings 19 the prophet Elijah is afraid: "he got up and fled for his life". In the wilderness he "came and sat down under a solitary broom tree. He asked that he might die, 'It is enough; now, O Lord, take away my life, for I am no better than my ancestors.'"

"Then he lay down under the broom tree and fell asleep. Suddenly an angel touched him and said to him, 'Get up and eat.' He looked, and there at his head was a cake baked on hot stones and a jar of water. He ate and drank and lay down again."

The angel comes a second time: "Get up and eat, or the journey will be too much for you." Then "He got up and ate and drank; then he went in the strength of that food forty days and forty nights to Horeb the mount of God."

What it shows: The first answer to Elijah's despair is sleep and food, and then a journey. The story is about a prophet's flight and not about hopelessness as such; the link is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Buddhism

Note: This story is about grief after the deaths of a husband and children, and may be hard to read.

Kisāgotamī

In Thig 10.1 the nun Kisāgotamī remembers: "I was on the road, near to giving birth, when I saw my husband dead. I gave birth there on the road before I'd reached my own house. My two children have died, and on the road my husband lies dead".

Her verses turn at the end: "I've developed the noble eightfold path leading to freedom from death. I've realized extinguishment, as seen in the mirror of the Dhamma. I've plucked out the dart, laid down the burden, and done what needed to be done."

The poem includes lines about women harming themselves, which are left out here, and the story of how she met the Buddha is told in later commentary and was not read.

What it shows: The poem moves from loss without measure to a burden laid down, in a nun's own words. It is a poem of grief and not a tale of hopelessness, and linking them is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, please tell someone today and contact your local emergency services if you may act on it.

Boethius in prison

Boethius writes his Consolation in prison. He begins: "Now perforce in tears and sadness / Learn a mournful strain to raise." Then a woman appears above his head, "of a countenance exceeding venerable", and she is Philosophy.

She tells him: "thou art pining with regretful longing for thy former fortune." She does not scold. "What is it, then, poor mortal, that hath cast thee into lamentation and mourning?" And she begins gently: "it is time for thee to take and drain a draught, soft and pleasant to the taste, which, as it penetrates within, may prepare the way for stronger potions."

What it shows: The first help Boethius is given is attention and a gentle remedy, and stronger medicine comes later. The scene opens a long argument and is not a tale of hopelessness as such; the link is ours, and the rest of the work was not read. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist.
