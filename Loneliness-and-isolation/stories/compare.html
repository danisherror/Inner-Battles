Loneliness and isolation

A story, read through each lens side by side.

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how loneliness works and how people ease it.

Tomás's story

Tomás moved to a new city for work. The evenings were long and the flat was quiet. A colleague invited him for a drink and he said he was busy, because he assumed the invitation was politeness. A few weeks later he noticed he had stopped checking his phone.

What helped:

  1. He named it. "I am lonely" was about now. It did not mean something was wrong with him.
  2. He checked the story. He asked what else the invitation could have been, and decided it might simply have been an invitation.
  3. He added small contacts. A weekly call with his brother, and a Thursday walk with the colleague.
  4. He found a shared task. He joined a five-a-side game, where there was something to do besides talk.
  5. He reached out first. He asked two people from the game to dinner.
  6. He asked for more help. Some evenings were still heavy, so he told his doctor, who suggested a counsellor.

It took months, and some weeks he still cancelled.

What it shows: Tomás did not need a crowd. A few regular contacts, and a different reading of one invitation, were enough to start.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. Loneliness is common and nothing to be ashamed of. If it is heavy or lasts, or comes with hopeless thoughts, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. Hinduism

A seat in seclusion, and a friend of all

Two verses of the Gita put solitude and company side by side. In 6.10: "Those who seek the state of Yog should reside in seclusion, constantly engaged in meditation with a controlled mind and body, getting rid of desires and possessions for enjoyment."

In 5.29 the Lord is described as "the selfless friend of all living beings": "My devotee attains peace."

What it shows: The yogi alone in seclusion is not described as abandoned, since the same text names a friend of all beings. This is a picture drawn from two verses and not a tale of a lonely person, and linking it to loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

Seeing all beings in the Self

In Isha 6-7 a teacher says: "he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, he never turns away from it. When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"

What it shows: The verse holds up a person for whom nothing is other, and so nothing left out. It is a picture and not a tale, and linking it to loneliness is ours. It should not be read as saying that a lonely person is missing something. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

Ruth and Naomi

In Ruth 1 Naomi, widowed and with both sons dead, sets out for home. She urges her daughters-in-law to turn back, and one does. Ruth does not: "Do not press me to leave you, to turn back from following you! Where you go, I will go; where you lodge, I will lodge; your people shall be my people and your God my God. Where you die, I will die, and there will I be buried."

At Bethlehem the women ask: "Is this Naomi?" She says: "Call me no longer Naomi; call me Mara, for the Almighty has dealt bitterly with me. I went away full, but the Lord has brought me back empty".

What it shows: Naomi arrives home with a companion, though she does not feel it yet. The story is about loyalty and loss and not about loneliness as such; the link is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddha and the bull elephant

In Ud 4.5 the Buddha is "crowded by monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen; by rulers and their chief ministers, and monastics of other religions and their disciples." He thinks: "Why don't I live alone, withdrawn from the group?" He sets out "to go wandering alone toward Pārileyyaka, with no companion."

A bull elephant, "crowded by other males, females, younglings, and cubs", has had the same thought: "Why don't I live alone, withdrawn from the herd?" He comes to the Buddha and attends on him, "clearing the vegetation from the place where the Buddha stayed, and using his trunk to set out water for drinking and washing."

The Buddha says: "The giant elephant, with tusks like draft-poles, agrees heart to heart with the spiritual giant, since each finds joy in the woods alone."

What it shows: This is solitude chosen and enjoyed, and not loneliness. The elephant who is also alone becomes a companion, and the text counts that as joy. The link to loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Zeus alone, and the children with shells

Epictetus takes the case of Zeus alone: "if being alone is enough to make solitude, you may say that even Zeus is solitary in the conflagration and bewails himself saying, 'Unhappy that I am who have neither Hera, nor Athena, nor Apollo, nor brother, nor son, nor descendant nor kinsman.'"

But he answers: "as Zeus dwells with himself, and is tranquil by himself, and thinks of his own administration and of its nature, and is employed in thoughts suitable to himself; so ought we also to be able to talk with ourselves".

And of children left alone: "They take up shells and ashes, and they build something, then pull it down, and build something else, and so they never want the means of passing the time."

What it shows: Epictetus argues that company does not prove one is not lonely, and that being alone does not prove one is. It is an argument and not a tale, and linking it to loneliness is ours. For many people being alone with their thoughts is not easy, so this should not be read as advice that a lonely person should simply be content. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Discourses, Book Three (external link), chapter 13 (the page names no translator)

The quotations were read on that page on 2026-11-15. The retelling joins three parts of the chapter and leaves out the rest, and the Greek was not examined.
