Loneliness and isolation

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

Loneliness is the painful gap between the connection you want and the connection you have. Social isolation is having few contacts. You can have either without the other, which is a standard distinction and not taken from the papers below.

Researchers describe it as "perceived social isolation (i.e. loneliness)". Cacioppo and Hawkley report research that links it to "heightened sensitivity to social threats, a confirmatory bias in social cognition that is self-protective and paradoxically self-defeating". That is one way of describing the pull to withdraw while wanting company. Holt-Lunstad and colleagues write that "Actual and perceived social isolation are both associated with increased risk for early mortality."

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers: staying in when invitations come, reading a short reply as a rejection, feeling alone in a crowd, and wanting someone but not knowing whom to ask. It overlaps with shame and guilt and self-doubt.

Loneliness is common and nothing to be ashamed of. If it is heavy or lasts, or comes with hopeless thoughts, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Cacioppo and Hawkley (2009), "Perceived social isolation and cognition," Trends in Cognitive Sciences 13(10), 447-454. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2009.06.005 (external link)
  • Holt-Lunstad, Smith, Baker, Harris and Stephenson (2015), "Loneliness and social isolation as risk factors for mortality: a meta-analytic review," Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(2), 227-237. doi:10.1177/1745691614568352 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for loneliness, but it treats both company and solitude. Gita 6.10 says: "Those who seek the state of Yog should reside in seclusion, constantly engaged in meditation with a controlled mind and body". That is solitude chosen for practice.

On the other side, 5.29 describes the Lord as "the selfless friend of all living beings": "Having realized Me as the enjoyer of all sacrifices and austerities, the Supreme Lord of all the worlds and the selfless friend of all living beings, My devotee attains peace."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. 6.10 is about a discipline of seclusion and not about the ache of being alone, and 5.29 speaks of peace and not of human company. Reading them together as an account of loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads describe separateness as a kind of small seeing. Isha 6-7: "he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, he never turns away from it. When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"

Chandogya 7.24 draws the line: "Where one sees nothing else, hears nothing else, understands nothing else, that is the Infinite. Where one sees something else, hears something else, understands something else, that is the finite."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. Both passages are about seeing the Self in all things and say nothing of lacking company, so reading them as an account of loneliness is ours. They do not say the lonely person has failed to see anything. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

Christianity says aloneness is a lack. In Genesis 2:18: "It is not good that the man should be alone; I will make him a helper as his partner."

The Psalms give the voice of the lonely: "Turn to me and be gracious to me, for I am lonely and afflicted" (25:16). And the answer: "Father of orphans and protector of widows is God in his holy habitation. God gives the desolate a home to live in" (68:5-6).

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. These passages name the want of company and a promise of a home. They do not define loneliness, and reading them together as an account of it is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts praise good company, and they also praise solitude. In SN 45.2 Ānanda says: "Sir, good friends, companions, and associates are half the spiritual life." The Buddha answers: "Not so, Ānanda! Not so, Ānanda! Good friends, companions, and associates are the whole of the spiritual life."

Dhp 328-329 gives both: "If you find an alert companion, an attentive friend to live happily together, then, overcoming all adversities, wander with them, joyful and mindful." "If you find no alert companion, no attentive friend to live happily together, then, like a king who flees his conquered realm, wander alone like a tusker in the wilds."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The texts speak of friendship and chosen solitude, and loneliness as an ache is not named. Reading them as an account of it is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Epictetus separates being alone from being lonely. Discourses 3.13: "Solitude is a certain condition of a helpless man. For because a man is alone, he is not for that reason also solitary; just as though a man is among numbers, he is not therefore not solitary."

And: "it is not the sight of a human creature which removes us from solitude, but the sight of one who is faithful and modest and helpful to us."

Aristotle says why we feel it: "without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods" (Nicomachean Ethics 8.1).

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. Epictetus' word, translated "solitude" and "solitary", means being left helpless and without anyone to trust, and the passage goes on to argue for being sufficient to oneself. Aristotle is writing about friendship. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.
