Loneliness and isolation

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read here point to a few causes, and are limited.

  • Trends. Masi and colleagues note that "Social and demographic trends are placing an increasing number of adults at risk for loneliness".
  • A threat-watching mind. Cacioppo and Hawkley report "heightened sensitivity to social threats" and "a confirmatory bias in social cognition that is self-protective and paradoxically self-defeating". A lonely mind may read cold signals into ordinary events, and avoid people, which keeps it lonely. The abstract says research indicates this and does not say which comes first.
  • Social thinking that has gone wrong. Masi and colleagues say the most successful interventions "addressed maladaptive social cognition", "consistent with current theories regarding loneliness and its etiology".
  • Everyday causes. Common description, not from these papers: moving, losing someone, a change of job or life stage, illness, shyness, and shame about being alone.

Loneliness is not a character flaw. Loneliness is common and nothing to be ashamed of. If it is heavy or lasts, or comes with hopeless thoughts, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Masi, Chen, Hawkley and Cacioppo (2011), "A meta-analysis of interventions to reduce loneliness," Personality and Social Psychology Review 15(3), 219-266. doi:10.1177/1088868310377394 (external link)
  • Cacioppo and Hawkley (2009), "Perceived social isolation and cognition," Trends in Cognitive Sciences 13(10), 447-454. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2009.06.005 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

Both papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The last point is common description and cites no study; Crossref gives 2010 as the online date of Masi and colleagues.

Hinduism

The Gita names what the beloved devotee is free from. 12.13-14: "Those devotees are very dear to Me who are free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate. They are free from attachment to possessions and egotism, equipoised in happiness and distress, and ever-forgiving."

By implication, malice, attachment and egotism are what stand between a person and others.

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The verses describe a person dear to God and do not discuss loneliness, and the inference that malice and egotism cause it is ours. It should not be read as saying that lonely people are malicious or proud. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads place the root of sorrow in seeing something other than the Self. Chandogya 7.24: "Where one sees something else, hears something else, understands something else, that is the finite. The Infinite is immortal, the finite is mortal."

Isha 7 says what the other seeing removes: "what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The root named is a way of seeing and not a lack of company, and reading it as a root of loneliness is ours. It should not be read as saying that a lonely person only needs to see differently. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Chandogya Upanishad, Seventh Prapathaka (external link), 7.24; Isha Upanishad (external link), 7 (Max Müller's translations)

Christianity

Ecclesiastes shows how toil can cut a person off: "Again, I saw vanity under the sun: the case of solitary individuals, without sons or brothers; yet there is no end to all their toil, and their eyes are never satisfied with riches. 'For whom am I toiling,' they ask, 'and depriving myself of pleasure?' This also is vanity and an unhappy business."

Elijah, after a conflict, says: "I have been very zealous for the Lord, the God of hosts, for the Israelites have forsaken your covenant, thrown down your altars, and killed your prophets with the sword. I alone am left, and they are seeking my life, to take it away" (1 Kings 19:10).

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The causes shown are overwork and a sense of being the only one left. The text does not give a general cause of loneliness, and reading these as one is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts count separation as suffering. SN 56.11: "being coupled with the disliked is suffering; separation from the liked is suffering; not getting what you wish for is suffering."

SN 45.2 shows what it means to have the opposite: "A mendicant with good friends, companions, and associates can expect to develop and cultivate the noble eightfold path."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. SN 56.11 lists kinds of suffering and does not name loneliness, though separation from those we like comes close to it, and SN 45.2 is about the practice of the path. Reading them as a root of loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Aristotle and Epictetus both root it in our nature. Aristotle, Politics 1.2: "man is by nature a political animal." And: "the individual, when isolated, is not self-sufficing; and therefore he is like a part in relation to the whole."

Epictetus: people feel it because "they set out from a certain natural principle, from the natural desire of community and mutual love and from the pleasure of conversation among men."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. Aristotle is describing the state and the individual, and "political animal" is about life in a community and not only about feelings. Both passages say the wish for company is natural, and that is not a fault. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources

  • Aristotle, Politics, Book One (external link), Part II (Benjamin Jowett's translation); Epictetus, Discourses, Book Three (external link), chapter 13 (the page names no translator)

The quotations were read on those pages on 2026-11-15. The part number of the Politics passage is taken from the order of the page's parts, which are numbered in Roman numerals, and the Greek of both authors was not examined.
