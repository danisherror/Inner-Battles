Loneliness and isolation

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here describes effects, mostly as statistical associations. Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2015) report that "social isolation odds ratio (OR) = 1.29, loneliness OR = 1.26, and living alone OR = 1.32, corresponding to an average of 29%, 26%, and 32% increased likelihood of mortality, respectively", and that "initial health status has an influence on the findings". Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton (2010), across "148 studies (308,849 participants)", report a "50% increased likelihood of survival for participants with stronger social relationships". These are group averages from observational studies and say nothing certain about any one person.

Cacioppo and Hawkley report that perceived social isolation "is a risk factor for, and may contribute to, poorer overall cognitive performance, faster cognitive decline, poorer executive functioning, increased negativity and depressive cognition".

Common effects, as people describe them and not findings from these papers: poor sleep, low mood, pulling further away, and doing less.

None of this is a reason for alarm. It is a reason to ask for company. Loneliness is common and nothing to be ashamed of. If it is heavy or lasts, or comes with hopeless thoughts, please talk to a doctor, therapist or someone you trust. If you are thinking of ending your life or harming yourself, tell someone today, and if you may act on those thoughts contact your local emergency services now. The International Association for Suicide Prevention points to a directory of verified helplines in over 150 countries (external link).

Sources

  • Holt-Lunstad, Smith, Baker, Harris and Stephenson (2015), "Loneliness and social isolation as risk factors for mortality: a meta-analytic review," Perspectives on Psychological Science 10(2), 227-237. doi:10.1177/1745691614568352 (external link)
  • Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton (2010), "Social relationships and mortality risk: a meta-analytic review," PLoS Medicine 7(7), e1000316. doi:10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316 (external link)
  • Cacioppo and Hawkley (2009), "Perceived social isolation and cognition," Trends in Cognitive Sciences 13(10), 447-454. doi:10.1016/j.tics.2009.06.005 (external link)
  • International Association for Suicide Prevention, Crisis Centres & Helplines (external link): read on 2026-11-15; the page says the directory it points to lists verified helplines in over 150 countries.

All three papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-15), not in full, and publication details were checked in Crossref. The final reading, that these are group averages from observational studies, is ours and not stated in the abstracts; Holt-Lunstad and colleagues (2010) say "comparable with well-established risk factors for mortality", which is not repeated here. The list of common effects cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita names peace as a result of knowing the friend of all beings, and affection as a result of friendliness. 5.29: "My devotee attains peace." 12.13-14: "Those devotees are very dear to Me who are free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The verses name peace and being dear to God as the effects, and say nothing of the effects of being alone. Reading them as the other side of loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Upanishads

The Upanishads name the lifting of sorrow as the effect of seeing unity. Isha 7: "what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?" Chandogya 7.25: "He who sees, perceives, and understands this, loves the Self, delights in the Self, revels in the Self, rejoices in the Self".

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The passages are about the knower and not about the lonely, and reading them as the answer to loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Christianity

Ecclesiastes shows the cost of having no one: "woe to one who is alone and falls and does not have another to help. Again, if two lie together, they keep warm, but how can one keep warm alone?"

It also names what company gives: "Two are better than one because they have a good reward for their toil. For if they fall, one will lift up the other".

The Psalmist asks for relief of the heart: "Relieve the troubles of my heart, and bring me out of my distress" (25:17).

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The passages describe a practical and an emotional cost of being alone. Reading them as effects of loneliness is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name what good friends do. SN 45.2: "A mendicant with good friends, companions, and associates can expect to develop and cultivate the noble eightfold path."

And more widely: "by relying on me as a good friend, sentient beings who are liable to rebirth, old age, and death, to sorrow, lamentation, pain, sadness, and distress are freed from all these things."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. The sutta is about the monastic path and the Buddha as a friend, and the effects named are of having good friends. The effects of being alone are not described, and reading them as the other side is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Philosophical

Epictetus lists what makes a person feel alone: "When then we have lost either a brother, or a son, or a friend on whom we were accustomed to repose, we say that we are left solitary, though we are often in Rome, though such a crowd meet us". He adds that the solitary man "is considered to be a helpless person and exposed to those who wish to harm him."

Aristotle names what friends give: "in poverty and in other misfortunes men think friends are the only refuge."

This is related to loneliness and not identical to it. Epictetus is explaining a word and Aristotle is writing about friendship, and neither lists the effects of being lonely. Reading them as effects is ours. Nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist, and loneliness that is heavy or lasting is worth telling someone about.

Sources
