Inner battle

Self-criticism

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story
Lenses to show

Modern

Self-criticism is a harsh inner voice that judges and punishes you for mistakes and shortcomings, when it could be helping you improve. It is not the same as honest self-assessment: noticing an error and asking how to fix it is one thing, and attacking yourself for it is another.

Researchers often study its opposite. Zessin and colleagues describe self-compassion as "a positive and caring attitude of a person toward her- or himself in the face of failures and individual shortcomings". Neff's 2003 paper presents self-compassion as "a healthy attitude toward oneself", and Gilbert and Procter's 2006 pilot study of a group therapy approach was designed for "people with high shame and self-criticism".

Self-criticism is close to perfectionism and often comes with shame. It is not a diagnosis, but it can go with low mood and anxiety. If the voice is relentless, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Zessin, Dickhäuser and Garbade (2015), "The relationship between self-compassion and well-being: a meta-analysis," Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 7(3), 340-364. doi:10.1111/aphw.12051 (external link)
  • Neff (2003), "Self-compassion: an alternative conceptualization of a healthy attitude toward oneself," Self and Identity 2(2), 85-101. doi:10.1080/15298860309032 (external link)
  • Gilbert and Procter (2006), "Compassionate mind training for people with high shame and self-criticism: overview and pilot study of a group therapy approach," Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy 13(6), 353-379. doi:10.1002/cpp.507 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word for the inner critic, but the Bhagavad Gita describes the mind that turns on its owner. 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self." And 6.6: "For those who have conquered the mind, it is their friend. For those who have failed to do so, the mind works like an enemy."

The translator's commentary on 6.5 adds: "We are responsible for our own elevation or debasement."

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The Gita is speaking of the undisciplined mind in general, and does not describe a harsh voice of self-judgement. Reading 6.5-6.6 as speaking to the inner critic is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name an inner critic. They speak of grief, and of a self that is not touched by what the mind says about it.

The Mundaka Upanishad (3.1.2) describes a person who "sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence", and says that when he sees the other, "then his grief passes away". Katha 1.2.22 says: "The wise who knows the Self as bodiless within the bodies, as unchanging among changing things, as great and omnipresent, does never grieve."

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The passages are about grief and the nature of the Self, and not about harsh self-judgement. Reading them as speaking to the inner critic is ours.

Christianity

Christian texts do not speak of an inner critic, but they speak of the heart that condemns. 1 John 3:19-20: "And by this we will know that we are from the truth and will reassure our hearts before him whenever our hearts condemn us, for God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything."

And Romans 8:1: "Therefore there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The verses are about conscience and standing before God, and not about a pattern of harsh self-talk. Reading them as speaking to the inner critic is ours.

Buddhism

Buddhist texts have no term for the inner critic, but SN 3.4 speaks of treating oneself as an enemy. King Pasenadi says: "Those who do bad things by way of body, speech, and mind don't love themselves... It's because they treat themselves like an enemy." And those who do good "treat themselves like a loved one". The Buddha agrees with him.

Here the point is about conduct, not self-talk. Dhp 159 turns to how we treat ourselves: "If one were to treat oneself as one instructs another, the well-tamed indeed would tame: for the self, it seems, is hard to tame."

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. Pasenadi means that harmful action is how a person treats themselves badly, and the texts do not describe a harsh inner voice. Reading Dhp 159 as speaking to it is ours.

Philosophical

The Stoics separate blaming others, blaming ourselves and blaming no one. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "When therefore we are hindered, or disturbed, or grieved, let us never attribute it to others, but to ourselves; that is, to our own principles. An uninstructed person will lay the fault of his own bad condition upon others. Someone just starting instruction will lay the fault on himself. Some who is perfectly instructed will place blame neither on others nor on himself."

So even blaming oneself, which can look like the humble course, is placed below not blaming at all.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. Epictetus is speaking of blame for our disturbance, and does not describe a harsh inner voice.
