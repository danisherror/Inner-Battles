Self-criticism

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

None of the sources read for this article explains where the inner critic comes from, so what follows is common explanation, not finding.

  • Learned from others. A harsh voice is often described as an internalised version of critical parents, teachers or peers.
  • A belief that harshness works. Many people feel that going easy on themselves would let them slip, so the critic is kept as a motivator.
  • Protection. Criticising yourself first can feel like a way of getting ahead of other people's judgement.
  • Shame and perfectionism. Gilbert and Procter's approach was designed for people with "high shame and self-criticism" (title), which suggests the two often go together; see also Perfectionism.

Sources

  • Gilbert and Procter (2006), Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy 13(6), 353-379. doi:10.1002/cpp.507 (external link)

Only the title and publication details of Gilbert and Procter were checked (Crossref); the paper was not opened. The first three bullets are common explanations and cite no study.

Hinduism

The Gita locates the root in an untrained mind.

  • The mind as enemy. 6.6: "For those who have failed to do so, the mind works like an enemy." Without training, the mind's habits run against us.
  • Responsibility. The commentary on 6.5 says we are responsible for our own elevation or debasement, so the mind's verdicts are not final.
  • Sorrow over what does not warrant it. In 2.11 Krishna tells Arjuna: "you are mourning for that which is not worthy of grief." The verse is about grief for others, but it shows a mind spending sorrow where it does not belong.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The Gita does not describe the roots of a harsh voice; these verses are read for the idea of an untrained mind.

Sources

  • Bhagavad Gita 6.5 (external link), 6.6 (external link), 2.11 (external link)

Quotations (Swami Mukundananda's translation) were read on those pages on 2026-11-14. The commentary line is the translator's. Applying 2.11 to self-criticism is a stretch noted here: it is about mourning for the living and the dead.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not give a root for self-criticism. The Mundaka's image of the grieving bird points to one: identification with the experiencer.

  • Immersion. "On the same tree man sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence" (Mundaka 3.1.2). The person who is fully taken up in what happens to them feels every blow.
  • Not seeing the other. The verse goes on: "But when he sees the other lord contented and knows his glory, then his grief passes away." The root is that the watching self is overlooked.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The verse is about grief, and its use here for the inner critic is ours.

Sources

  • Mundaka Upanishad (external link) 3.1.1-2 (Max Müller's translation)

Quotations were read in Müller's translation on Wikisource on 2026-11-14. The text reads "(an-îsâ)" after "impotence" in Müller's brackets; it is omitted here.

Christianity

The Bible shows the root in a person who judges himself by his own record and by what he has lost. In the parable of the prodigal son (Luke 15:17-24), the young man who has wasted his inheritance plans what to say: "Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; I am no longer worthy to be called your son; treat me like one of your hired hands."

His own verdict is that he has forfeited his place. The father has not said so.

Matthew 7:3-5 shows a related habit, a standard held up to others and missed in oneself: "Why do you see the speck in your neighbor's eye but do not notice the log in your own eye?"

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The parable is about repentance and the second passage about judging others, and reading them for the roots of the inner critic is ours.

Buddhism

SN 36.6, the Arrow Sutta, describes how suffering is added to. When an unlearned person experiences painful physical feelings, they "sorrow and wail and lament, beating their breast and falling into confusion. They experience two feelings: physical and mental. It's like a person who is struck with an arrow, only to be struck with a second arrow."

The second arrow is the reaction. The sutta is speaking of physical pain, but it names the pattern the inner critic follows: a first hurt, then the extra hurt of what we do with it.

SN 3.4 adds that treating oneself "like an enemy" is how a person fails to love themselves.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The sutta is about physical pain, and the use here for self-attack is ours.

Philosophical

The philosophers find the root in our own principles and judgements, not in the event. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." The disturbance of self-blame, on this view, comes from the rule we apply: that every mistake should be punished.

Aristotle describes how a person's relation to themselves breaks down. In Nicomachean Ethics 9.4, bad men, "having nothing lovable in them", and "their soul is rent by faction", and "bad men are laden with repentance". For Aristotle this follows from wickedness. Applying it to people who are only hard on themselves would go beyond his text.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)
  • Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics 9.4 (external link) (W. D. Ross's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The Aristotle chapter number is the one on the page and was not checked beyond that. Reading Epictetus's point as about self-blame, and the Aristotle passage as relevant at all, is ours and is offered with that caution.
