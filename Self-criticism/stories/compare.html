Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how self-criticism works and how people loosen its grip.

Leila's story

Leila, a nurse, gave the wrong time on a handover note. A colleague caught it within minutes and no harm was done.

On the drive home the voice started: "You're careless. You always do this. They'll stop trusting you." By bedtime she had gone through the whole shift, picking out every small slip. She slept badly, and the next day she double-checked everything twice and was slower than usual.

What helped:

  1. She noticed the voice. A friend asked, "Would you say that to a new nurse?" Leila realised she would not.
  2. She sorted the message. Useful: check times against the chart before writing. Not useful: "you're careless".
  3. She spoke to herself by name. "Leila, that was a slip. The fix is to check the chart."
  4. She wrote down one fix and stopped. The fix went on a sticky note, and the rest of the evening was hers.
  5. She noticed it was not lifting. After weeks of poor sleep and dread before shifts, she spoke to her doctor and was referred to a counsellor.

The voice did not go. It became shorter, and it started to say something she could use.

What it shows: The mistake needed a fix, not a sentence. Sorting the message, using a kinder voice and stopping at one fix changed what the critic was for.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If self-criticism is relentless or comes with low mood or anxiety, please see a doctor or therapist.

Hinduism

Arjuna, freed from doubt

The Gita begins with Arjuna overwhelmed and ends with him steady. The setting is a conversation with Krishna before a battle.

Along the way Krishna names the mind as friend or enemy: "For those who have conquered the mind, it is their friend. For those who have failed to do so, the mind works like an enemy" (6.6). And near the end he gives his last instruction: "Abandon all varieties of dharmas and simply surrender unto Me alone. I shall liberate you from all sinful reactions; do not fear" (18.66).

Arjuna answers: "O Infallible One, by Your grace my illusion has been dispelled, and I am situated in knowledge. I am now free from doubts, and I shall act according to Your instructions" (18.73).

What it shows: The poem moves a person from being stuck to acting. It is not about an inner critic, and Arjuna's crisis is about duty; the story is read here for the move from fear to steadiness.

Upanishads

Yama on grief and the Self

The Katha Upanishad is a conversation between a boy, Nachiketas, and Death (Yama), who teaches him about the Self. It has no narrative of self-criticism, so this is a short passage from the teaching, not a tale.

Yama says: "The wise who knows the Self as bodiless within the bodies, as unchanging among changing things, as great and omnipresent, does never grieve" (1.2.22).

He also says that the Self is not reached through learning alone: "That Self cannot be gained by the Veda, nor by understanding, nor by much learning" (1.2.23). And then he adds the condition: "But he who has not first turned away from his wickedness, who is not tranquil, and subdued, or whose mind is not at rest, he can never obtain the Self (even) by knowledge" (1.2.24).

What it shows: The teaching has two sides. One is a Self that does not grieve, and the other is a call to turn away from what is wrong. It is not about an inner critic, and the link is ours: turning away from a fault is asked for, and grieving over and over is not.

Sources

  • Katha Upanishad (external link) 1.2.22-24 (Max Müller's translation)

Christianity

The son who rehearsed his sentence

In Luke 15, a younger son asks for his share of the property, spends it and is left hungry. "When he came to his senses" he plans his return: "Father, I have sinned against heaven and before you; I am no longer worthy to be called your son; treat me like one of your hired hands" (15:17-19).

He sets off. "But while he was still far off, his father saw him and was filled with compassion; he ran and put his arms around him and kissed him" (15:20). The son begins his speech ("I am no longer worthy to be called your son").

The father does not let him finish it. He says: "Quickly, bring out a robe—the best one—and put it on him; put a ring on his finger and sandals on his feet... for this son of mine was dead and is alive again; he was lost and is found!" (15:22-24).

What it shows: The son has already passed sentence on himself, and the father does not agree with it. The parable is about repentance and welcome and not about the inner critic as such; it shows a self-verdict set aside.

Buddhism

King Pasenadi asks who loves themselves

In SN 3.4, King Pasenadi tells the Buddha of a thought he had in private retreat: "Who are those who love themselves? And who are those who don't love themselves?"

His answer: "Those who do bad things by way of body, speech, and mind don't love themselves. Even though they may say: 'I love myself', they don't really. Why is that? It's because they treat themselves like an enemy." And those who do good do love themselves, "even though they may say: 'I don't love myself'", because "they treat themselves like a loved one".

The Buddha says: "That's so true, Great King! That's so true!" and repeats the king's words, adding verses that begin: "If you knew your self as beloved, you'd not yoke yourself to wickedness. For happiness is not easy to find by someone who does bad deeds."

What it shows: Caring for yourself is shown in what you do, more than in what you say about yourself. The sutta is about conduct and not self-talk, so it speaks to the inner critic only indirectly: harshness in words is not the same as care.

Philosophical

Epictetus's three kinds of person

In the Enchiridion, Epictetus says that disturbance comes from our own judgements. "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things. Death, for instance, is not terrible, else it would have appeared so to Socrates."

Then he sorts people into three kinds: "An uninstructed person will lay the fault of his own bad condition upon others. Someone just starting instruction will lay the fault on himself. Some who is perfectly instructed will place blame neither on others nor on himself."

He is not saying that faults do not matter. He is saying that the one who has learned stops treating blame as the answer, and looks at the principle behind the disturbance.

What it shows: Self-blame is a step up from blaming everyone else, and Epictetus puts it in the middle. The passage is about disturbance and not about an inner critic; the point for self-criticism is that the aim is to see the principle, not to find the culprit.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14, and the passage is given as that page prints it (including "Some who is perfectly instructed", sic). The sentence about faults not mattering is our gloss, and not Epictetus's words.
