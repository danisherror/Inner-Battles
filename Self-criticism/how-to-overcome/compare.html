Inner battle

Self-criticism

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is to keep honest self-assessment and drop the contempt.

  1. Notice the voice. Give it a name or a tone ("the critic") so it is something you hear and not simply who you are.
  2. Ask whether it is useful. "Is this telling me something I can fix, or just attacking me?" Keep the first kind of message and let the second go.
  3. Speak to yourself as to a friend. Zessin and colleagues describe self-compassion as a "caring attitude... in the face of failures and individual shortcomings". Ask what you would say to a friend with the same slip.
  4. Try distance in your self-talk. Kross and colleagues found that using non-first-person language and your own name during introspection enhanced self-distancing and lowered distress in stressful social tasks. That evidence is about social stress, not about self-criticism.
  5. Expect help to exist. Gilbert and Procter piloted a group therapy approach, compassionate mind training, for people with high shame and self-criticism. A pilot study is not proof it works, and it was not read in full.
  6. Do not use the critic as your only motivator. Whether kindness motivates as well as harshness was not tested in the sources read here.
  7. Get help if it is relentless. A doctor or therapist can help with constant self-attack, low mood or anxiety.

Sources

  • Zessin, Dickhäuser and Garbade (2015), Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 7(3), 340-364. doi:10.1111/aphw.12051 (external link)
  • Kross et al. (2014), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 106(2), 304-324. doi:10.1037/a0035173 (external link)
  • Gilbert and Procter (2006), Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy 13(6), 353-379. doi:10.1002/cpp.507 (external link)

Zessin and Kross were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14). For Gilbert and Procter only the title and details were checked (Crossref). Steps 1, 2 and 6 are common advice, not tied to a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita's way is to make the mind a friend through discipline, and then to put down the weight.

  1. Elevate, do not degrade. 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."
  2. Train the mind. 6.6: "For those who have conquered the mind, it is their friend."
  3. Do not fear. 18.66: "Abandon all varieties of dharmas and simply surrender unto Me alone. I shall liberate you from all sinful reactions; do not fear."
  4. Trust the resolve. 9.30-31: even "the vilest sinners" who worship with exclusive devotion "are to be considered righteous because they have made the proper resolve", and "no devotee of Mine is ever lost".

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The verses are about devotion and the mind, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for relentless self-attack.

Sources

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to shifting from the one who suffers to the one who watches.

  1. See the other bird. Mundaka 3.1.2: "when he sees the other lord contented and knows his glory, then his grief passes away."
  2. Shake off the verdicts. 3.1.3: "then he is wise, and shaking off good and evil, he reaches the highest oneness, free from passions".
  3. Know the Self. Katha 1.2.22: "The wise who knows the Self as bodiless within the bodies, as unchanging among changing things, as great and omnipresent, does never grieve."
  4. Do not skip the turning. Katha 1.2.24 says that one who "has not first turned away from his wickedness" cannot obtain the Self. Changing what is wrong still matters.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. The passages describe a path of knowledge, and applying them to a harsh inner voice is ours.

Christianity

The Bible's answer to the condemning heart is to point to a greater one.

  1. Remember who is greater. 1 John 3:20: "whenever our hearts condemn us, for God is greater than our hearts, and he knows everything."
  2. Receive the verdict. Romans 8:1: "there is now no condemnation for those who are in Christ Jesus."
  3. Remember how far. Psalm 103:12: "as far as the east is from the west, so far he removes our transgressions from us."
  4. Go home. In Luke 15, the son gets up and returns, though his words are about unworthiness. The father is already running to meet him (15:20).
  5. Look at your own eye first, kindly. Matthew 7:5 asks for honesty about oneself before judging others; it is not a call for self-attack.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. Christian care for a person weighed down by guilt also includes pastoral and medical help, which these verses do not discuss.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts point to treating oneself as one would treat someone dear, and to not adding the second arrow.

  1. Know yourself as beloved. Dhp 157: "If you knew your self as beloved, you'd look after it so well."
  2. Treat yourself as you instruct another. Dhp 159: "If one were to treat oneself as one instructs another, the well-tamed indeed would tame: for the self, it seems, is hard to tame."
  3. Do not treat yourself as an enemy. SN 3.4: those who treat themselves "like a loved one" are those who love themselves.
  4. Feel the first arrow only. SN 36.6: the learned disciple feels the pain but they "don't sorrow or wail or lament". The practice is to notice the second arrow and put it down.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. SN 3.4 and SN 36.6 are not about harsh self-talk, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for relentless self-attack.

Philosophical

The philosophers advise moving from blame to understanding, and returning without disgust.

  1. Look to your principles. Epictetus, Enchiridion 5: "Men are disturbed, not by things, but by the principles and notions which they form concerning things." Ask which rule you are punishing yourself under.
  2. Move up the ladder of blame. In the same chapter the uninstructed blame others, the beginner blames himself, and the "perfectly instructed" blame "neither on others nor on himself".
  3. Return. Marcus Aurelius: "when thou hast failed, return back again, and be content if the greater part of what thou doest is consistent with man's nature, and love this to which thou returnest."
  4. Treat the practice as a remedy. "Do not return to philosophy as if she were a master", but as one who applies "a bit of sponge and egg" to sore eyes.

This is related to self-criticism and not identical to it. These are rules for living, and applying them to a harsh inner voice is a reading.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 5 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)
  • Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5 (external link) (George Long's translation)

The quotations were read at MIT Classics on 2026-11-14. The Marcus text has "thou bast failed", corrected to "hast". The page for Epictetus names Carter, but see the note in what it is on wording.
