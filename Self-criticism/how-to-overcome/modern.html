Self-criticism

A harsh inner voice that punishes mistakes instead of helping you improve.

How to overcome it

The aim is to keep honest self-assessment and drop the contempt.

  1. Notice the voice. Give it a name or a tone ("the critic") so it is something you hear and not simply who you are.
  2. Ask whether it is useful. "Is this telling me something I can fix, or just attacking me?" Keep the first kind of message and let the second go.
  3. Speak to yourself as to a friend. Zessin and colleagues describe self-compassion as a "caring attitude... in the face of failures and individual shortcomings". Ask what you would say to a friend with the same slip.
  4. Try distance in your self-talk. Kross and colleagues found that using non-first-person language and your own name during introspection enhanced self-distancing and lowered distress in stressful social tasks. That evidence is about social stress, not about self-criticism.
  5. Expect help to exist. Gilbert and Procter piloted a group therapy approach, compassionate mind training, for people with high shame and self-criticism. A pilot study is not proof it works, and it was not read in full.
  6. Do not use the critic as your only motivator. Whether kindness motivates as well as harshness was not tested in the sources read here.
  7. Get help if it is relentless. A doctor or therapist can help with constant self-attack, low mood or anxiety.

Sources

  • Zessin, Dickhäuser and Garbade (2015), Applied Psychology: Health and Well-Being 7(3), 340-364. doi:10.1111/aphw.12051 (external link)
  • Kross et al. (2014), Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 106(2), 304-324. doi:10.1037/a0035173 (external link)
  • Gilbert and Procter (2006), Clinical Psychology & Psychotherapy 13(6), 353-379. doi:10.1002/cpp.507 (external link)
