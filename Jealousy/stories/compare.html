Jealousy

A story, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Note: This is an invented composite story, not a real case. It is built from patterns people commonly describe, so you can see how jealousy works and how people loosen its grip.

Tomas's story

Tomas's partner, Priya, moved to a new team. She came home talking about a colleague who had made her laugh. Tomas smiled, then spent the evening running the sentence over in his head.

Within a week he was reading her messages when she was in the shower. He asked about her lunches. When she stopped answering, he said she was hiding something. Priya, who had hidden nothing, began to stay quiet.

What helped:

  1. He named the fear. A friend asked what he was afraid of. "That she will find someone better and I will not see it coming."
  2. He sorted what he knew from what he imagined. He knew she had a new job and a colleague who was funny. The rest was a story.
  3. He said the feeling. "I have felt insecure since you changed teams. I do not think you have done anything. I wanted to tell you instead of checking."
  4. He stopped checking. The first fortnight was the hardest, and he went for a walk when the urge came.
  5. He noticed it was not lifting. After weeks of poor sleep and a mind that would not rest, he spoke to his doctor and was referred to a counsellor.

The fear did not vanish. It got smaller, and it stopped giving the orders.

What it shows: The jealousy was about a fear of loss, not about anything Priya did. Naming it and saying it aloud did what checking could not.

Sources

  • Illustrative composite. The steps reflect the advice in How to overcome it and are not drawn from a specific study. If jealousy is constant, or leads you to control or fear a partner, please see a doctor, a therapist or a domestic abuse service.

Hinduism

Two roads in one chapter

The Gita has no story of jealousy, so this is a passage from Krishna's teaching and not a tale. In Chapter 2 Krishna describes a road down and a road back.

The road down begins in attachment: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger" (2.62).

The road back is described a few verses on: "But one who controls the mind, and is free from attachment and aversion, even while using the objects of the senses, attains the Grace of God" (2.64). Then: "By divine grace comes the peace in which all sorrows end" (2.65).

Krishna gives a picture of that peace: "Just as the ocean remains undisturbed by the incessant flow of waters from rivers merging into it, likewise the sage who is unmoved despite the flow of desirable objects all around him attains peace, and not the person who strives to satisfy desires" (2.70).

What it shows: Attachment is not the same as having things or loving people, and the Gita's point is that peace comes from not being driven by them. It is not about jealousy, and the link is ours: the fear of losing someone belongs to the road down.

Upanishads

The bees and the honey

In the Chandogya Upanishad a father, Uddalaka, teaches his son Svetaketu in parables. The ninth section begins: "As the bees, my son, make honey by collecting the juices of distant trees, and reduce the juice into one form, And as these juices have no discrimination, so that they might say, I am the juice of this tree or that, in the same manner, my son, all these creatures, when they have become merged in the True (either in deep sleep or in death), know not that they are merged in the True."

The next section gives a second picture, the rivers: "as those rivers, when they are in the sea, do not know, I am this or that river". After each, the father says: "It is the Self, and thou, O Svetaketu, art it."

What it shows: The parable pictures separate things that are one in the end. It is not about jealousy, and the link is ours: jealousy depends on telling "mine" from "theirs", and the parable shows juices that cannot say whose tree they came from.

Christianity

The elder son at the door

In Luke 15 the younger son has come home and his father has held a feast. The elder son was in the field: "as he came and approached the house, he heard music and dancing." A slave tells him: "Your brother has come, and your father has killed the fatted calf because he has got him back safe and sound" (15:25-27).

"Then he became angry and refused to go in. His father came out and began to plead with him" (15:28). The elder son answers with his record of service and a complaint: "when this son of yours came back, who has devoured your assets with prostitutes, you killed the fatted calf for him!" (15:30).

The father replies: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours. But we had to celebrate and rejoice, because this brother of yours was dead and has come to life; he was lost and has been found" (15:31-32).

What it shows: The elder son stands outside his own father's house because he thinks his place has been taken. The parable is about welcome and repentance and not about jealousy as such; it shows the father going out to him and telling him that all the father has is his. The parable ends there, and the text does not say whether he went in.

Buddhism

Sakka asks what binds the world

In DN 21, Sakka, the king of the gods, comes to the Buddha with questions. The first is: "Good fellow, what fetters bind the gods, humans, titans, dragons, centaurs—and any of the other diverse creatures—so that, though they wish to be free of enmity, violence, hostility, and hate, they still have enmity, violence, hostility, and hate?"

The Buddha answers: "Lord of gods, the fetters of jealousy and stinginess bind" them. Sakka asks where jealousy and stinginess come from, and the Buddha says: "The liked and the disliked, lord of gods, are the source of jealousy and stinginess. When the liked and the disliked exist there is jealousy and stinginess. When the liked and the disliked don't exist there is no jealousy and stinginess."

Sakka replies to the first answer: "That's so true, Blessed One! That's so true, Holy One! Hearing the Buddha's answer, I've gone beyond doubt and got rid of indecision."

What it shows: Even the king of the gods is asking how to be free of enmity. The sutta traces jealousy to likes and dislikes and then on to desire. It is about jealousy and stinginess in general and not about the fear of losing a person.

Philosophical

Epictetus and the ship

In Enchiridion 7 Epictetus gives a picture. "Consider when, on a voyage, your ship is anchored; if you go on shore to get water you may along the way amuse yourself with picking up a shellfish, or an onion. However, your thoughts and continual attention ought to be bent towards the ship, waiting for the captain to call on board."

Then he turns it to life: "So it is with life. If, instead of an onion or a shellfish, you are given a wife or child, that is fine. But if the captain calls, you must run to the ship, leaving them, and regarding none of them."

His advice is to enjoy what you are given, and to keep your attention on the ship.

What it shows: Epictetus asks us to hold even our dearest people as given and not owned, which takes away the thing a rival could steal. It is a hard saying and it is about loss, not about jealousy; the link is ours. It does not say to love less, and it says nothing about how to treat a partner.
