Jealousy

What it is, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

Jealousy is the fear, and the hurt, of losing something you value to a rival, who may be real or imagined. Most often it is someone's love or attention. It is different from envy, which is wanting what another person has: envy involves two people, jealousy three. Jealousy shows up in romantic relationships, and also between friends, siblings and colleagues.

Smith and Kim, reviewing the research on envy, define it as "an unpleasant, often painful emotion characterized by feelings of inferiority, hostility, and resentment caused by an awareness of a desired attribute enjoyed by another person or group of persons". Parrott and Smith's 1993 paper is titled "Distinguishing the experiences of envy and jealousy", so researchers treat the two as separate experiences.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers:

  • A drop in the stomach when your partner laughs at someone else's message.
  • Asking "who were you with?" and not quite believing the answer.
  • Checking a phone, a profile or a location.
  • Feeling replaced when a friend makes a new friend.

Jealousy is not a diagnosis. If it is constant, or leads you to check, follow or control someone, a doctor or therapist can help.

Sources

  • Smith and Kim (2007), "Comprehending envy," Psychological Bulletin 133(1), 46-64. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.1.46 (external link)
  • Parrott and Smith (1993), "Distinguishing the experiences of envy and jealousy," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 64(6), 906-920. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.64.6.906 (external link)

Hinduism

Hinduism has no single word in the Bhagavad Gita for jealousy as the fear of losing someone to a rival, but the Gita describes the person who is free of its roots. Gita 12.13-14: "Those devotees are very dear to Me who are free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate. They are free from attachment to possessions and egotism, equipoised in happiness and distress, and ever-forgiving."

The translator's commentary on these verses says of such devotees: "If they harbor envy toward others, it is tantamount to harboring envy toward God Himself."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses speak of malice, envy and attachment to possessions, and do not describe the fear of losing someone to a rival. Reading them as speaking to jealousy is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not name jealousy, and the closest they come is a warning against grasping at what belongs to another. Isha Upanishad 1: "All this, whatsoever moves on earth, is to be hidden in the Lord (the Self). When thou hast surrendered all this, then thou mayest enjoy. Do not covet the wealth of any man!"

The verse says to give up the sense of owning and then enjoy. It does not say to stop loving.

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verse is about coveting wealth, which is closer to greed and envy than to the fear of losing someone to a rival. Reading it as speaking to jealousy is ours.

Christianity

Christian texts speak of jealousy as a force that is hard to stand against. Proverbs 27:4: "Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming, but who is able to stand before jealousy?"

The Song of Solomon links it with love. In the King James Version, 8:6 reads: "love is strong as death; jealousy is cruel as the grave". The NRSV renders the same line "passion fierce as the grave".

The Bible also calls God "a jealous God" (Exodus 20:5). That is a statement about God's claim on his people, and this article is about human jealousy, so it is not treated here.

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. Proverbs and the Song of Solomon speak of jealousy as a power, and do not define it as the fear of losing someone to a rival.

Buddhism

In the Buddhist texts jealousy is joined with stinginess, the clinging to what is one's own. In the Dhammapada (209) the Buddha describes a person who forgets their goal and "cling[s] to what you hold dear, jealous of those devoted to their heart's goal".

In the Sakka sutta (DN 21), the god Sakka asks what binds beings who wish to be at peace, and the Buddha answers: "the fetters of jealousy and stinginess bind the gods, humans, titans, dragons, centaurs—and any of the other diverse creatures—so that, though they wish to be free of enmity, violence, hostility, and hate, they still have enmity, violence, hostility, and hate."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The translation renders the Pali as "jealousy", but the word is generally described as envy, and the fear of losing a loved one to a rival is not the sutta's topic.

Philosophical

Spinoza gives one of the clearest definitions of jealousy in philosophy. In the Ethics (Part III, Proposition 35): "If anyone conceives, that an object of his love joins itself to another with closer bonds of friendship than he himself has attained to, he will be affected with hatred towards the loved object and with envy towards his rival."

In the note that follows he names it: "This hatred towards an object of love joined with envy is called Jealousy, which accordingly is nothing else but a wavering of the disposition arising from combined love and hatred, accompanied by the idea of some rival who is envied."

Spinoza names jealousy directly, so no "related, not identical" caveat is needed. His account is one philosopher's definition of the emotion and not the view of philosophy as a whole, and it says nothing about what to do about it.
