Jealousy is the fear, and the hurt, of losing something you value to a rival, who may be real or imagined. Most often it is someone's love or attention. It is different from [envy](../../Envy/what-it-is/modern.html), which is wanting what another person has: envy involves two people, jealousy three. Jealousy shows up in romantic relationships, and also between friends, siblings and colleagues.

Smith and Kim, reviewing the research on envy, define it as "an unpleasant, often painful emotion characterized by feelings of inferiority, hostility, and resentment caused by an awareness of a desired attribute enjoyed by another person or group of persons". Parrott and Smith's 1993 paper is titled "Distinguishing the experiences of envy and jealousy", so researchers treat the two as separate experiences.

Everyday signs, as people commonly describe them and not as findings from these papers:

- A drop in the stomach when your partner laughs at someone else's message.
- Asking "who were you with?" and not quite believing the answer.
- Checking a phone, a profile or a location.
- Feeling replaced when a friend makes a new friend.

Jealousy is not a diagnosis. If it is constant, or leads you to check, follow or control someone, a doctor or therapist can help.
