Jealousy

Root causes, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The sources read for this article do not give a full account of where jealousy comes from, so most of what follows is common explanation and not finding.

  • Fear of losing something that matters. Jealousy needs something valued and a sense that it is at risk, whether or not the risk is real.
  • Insecurity. A shaky sense of your own worth makes a rival's presence feel like proof that you will be left.
  • Past hurt. Being betrayed or left before can make later ambiguity feel like a repeat.
  • Attachment style. A 2025 study of 265 people in Chile and Spain measured digital jealousy and found "anxious attachment and closeness were significant predictors of jealousy, explaining nearly 30% of its variance". Sharpsteen and Kirkpatrick's 1997 paper is titled "Romantic jealousy and adult romantic attachment", which points the same way.
  • Feeling loved. In the Chilean part of that sample, "feeling loved negatively predicted jealousy": people who felt more loved reported less.

These are associations in questionnaire samples. They do not show that anxious attachment causes jealousy, and the study measured jealousy about online behaviour, not jealousy in general.

Sources

  • Fernández et al. (2025), "What Is the Link of Closeness and Jealousy in Romantic Relationships?" Behavioral Sciences 15(2), 132. doi:10.3390/bs15020132 (external link)
  • Sharpsteen and Kirkpatrick (1997), "Romantic jealousy and adult romantic attachment," Journal of Personality and Social Psychology 72(3), 627-640. doi:10.1037/0022-3514.72.3.627 (external link)

Fernández et al. was read in the abstract (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14). Sharpsteen and Kirkpatrick was checked for title and publication details only (Crossref) and not opened. The first three bullets are common explanations and cite no study.

Hinduism

The Gita describes how a mind slides from attachment to anger. Gita 2.62: "While contemplating on the objects of the senses, one develops attachment to them. Attachment leads to desire, and from desire arises anger."

Gita 2.63 follows the slide: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

Gita 2.71 names what stands opposite: "That person, who gives up all material desires and lives free from a sense of greed, proprietorship, and egoism, attains perfect peace."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses are about attachment to the objects of the senses in general. Reading the sense of "mine" over a person as one case of that chain is ours.

Upanishads

The Katha Upanishad names seeing difference as the thing that keeps a person circling. Katha 2.1.10: "What is here (visible in the world), the same is there (invisible in Brahman); and what is there, the same is here. He who sees any difference here (between Brahman and the world), goes from death to death."

And 2.1.11: "Even by the mind this (Brahman) is to be obtained, and then there is no difference whatsoever. He goes from death to death who sees any difference here."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses are about the difference between Brahman and the world, not between two people. Reading "difference" as the sense of a rival who is other than me is ours.

Christianity

The Bible does not give a theory of where jealousy comes from, but two passages touch the roots.

The first is fear. 1 John 4:18: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear; for fear has to do with punishment, and whoever fears has not reached perfection in love."

The second is the feeling of being overlooked. In the parable of the two sons, the elder son says to his father: "For all these years I have been working like a slave for you, and I have never disobeyed your command, yet you have never given me even a young goat so that I might celebrate with my friends" (Luke 15:29).

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. 1 John 4:18 is about love and fear of punishment, and the elder son's complaint is a grievance and not a fear of loss. Reading them as roots of jealousy is ours.

Buddhism

DN 21 traces jealousy back through a chain. Sakka asks about the source of jealousy and stinginess, and the Buddha says: "The liked and the disliked, lord of gods, are the source of jealousy and stinginess."

Then, question by question: "Desire is the source of what is liked and disliked." "Thought is the source of desire." "Judgments driven by the proliferation of perceptions are the source of thoughts."

The Sutta Nipata (4.11) gives a similar line: "Quarrels and disputes come from what we hold dear". Asked where what we hold dear comes from, it answers: "What we hold dear in the world spring from desire".

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The suttas speak of jealousy, stinginess and quarrels together, and not of the fear of losing a person to a rival. Reading the chain as the roots of that fear is ours.

Philosophical

Epictetus puts the root in treating things that belong to others as our own. Enchiridion 1: "Remember, then, that if you suppose that things which are slavish by nature are also free, and that what belongs to others is your own, then you will be hindered."

And in 14: "If you wish your children, and your wife, and your friends to live for ever, you are stupid; for you wish to be in control of things which you cannot, you wish for things that belong to others to be your own."

Spinoza adds the other half. The lover will "endeavour, as far as he can, to imagine the loved object as most closely bound to him", and this endeavour is "checked by the image of the loved object in conjunction with the image of him whom the loved object has joined to itself" (Ethics III.35, proof).

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. Epictetus is speaking of things outside our control in general, and Spinoza of the structure of the emotion. Reading Epictetus's point as the root of jealousy is ours.
