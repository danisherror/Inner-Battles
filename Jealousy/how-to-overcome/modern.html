Jealousy

The fear of losing someone or something to a rival, and the hurt and suspicion that follow.

How to overcome it

The aim is to feel the fear without letting it run your behaviour.

  1. Name the fear under the jealousy. "I am afraid I will be left" is truer, and easier to work with, than "I know something is going on".
  2. Separate what you know from what you imagine. Write down what happened and what you have added to it.
  3. Say the feeling, not the accusation. "I felt left out tonight" invites a conversation; "who were you with?" invites a defence.
  4. Cut back the checking. Elphinston and Noller linked jealous thoughts and "surveillance behaviors" with relationship dissatisfaction. That is an association, but checking also keeps the worry alive, which is a common observation and not a finding here.
  5. Ask for reassurance, but not on a loop. A 2025 study found that in the Chilean sample "feeling loved negatively predicted jealousy", which suggests feeling secure matters. It was not a test of treatment, and asking again and again is not the same as feeling loved.
  6. Build your own footing. Friends, work and interests that do not depend on one person make a rival less of a threat.
  7. Do not use jealousy to control. Where it leads to isolating or dominating someone, De Sousa and colleagues point to addressing "maladaptive expressions of jealousy". Stop, and get help.
  8. Get help if it is constant. A doctor or therapist can help with jealousy that fills your days, and a domestic abuse service can help if you are the one who is afraid.
