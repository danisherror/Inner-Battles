Inner battle

Jealousy

How to overcome it, read through each lens side by side.

TopicWhat it isRoot causesEffects on lifeHow to overcome itA story▦ All sections
Lenses to show

Modern

The aim is to feel the fear without letting it run your behaviour.

  1. Name the fear under the jealousy. "I am afraid I will be left" is truer, and easier to work with, than "I know something is going on".
  2. Separate what you know from what you imagine. Write down what happened and what you have added to it.
  3. Say the feeling, not the accusation. "I felt left out tonight" invites a conversation; "who were you with?" invites a defence.
  4. Cut back the checking. Elphinston and Noller linked jealous thoughts and "surveillance behaviors" with relationship dissatisfaction. That is an association, but checking also keeps the worry alive, which is a common observation and not a finding here.
  5. Ask for reassurance, but not on a loop. A 2025 study found that in the Chilean sample "feeling loved negatively predicted jealousy", which suggests feeling secure matters. It was not a test of treatment, and asking again and again is not the same as feeling loved.
  6. Build your own footing. Friends, work and interests that do not depend on one person make a rival less of a threat.
  7. Do not use jealousy to control. Where it leads to isolating or dominating someone, De Sousa and colleagues point to addressing "maladaptive expressions of jealousy". Stop, and get help.
  8. Get help if it is constant. A doctor or therapist can help with jealousy that fills your days, and a domestic abuse service can help if you are the one who is afraid.

Sources

  • Elphinston and Noller (2011), Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 14(11), 631-635. doi:10.1089/cyber.2010.0318 (external link)
  • Fernández et al. (2025), Behavioral Sciences 15(2), 132. doi:10.3390/bs15020132 (external link)
  • De Sousa et al. (2026), Child Abuse & Neglect. doi:10.1016/j.chiabu.2026.108253 (external link)

All three were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14). None tested a treatment for jealousy. Steps 1, 2, 3, 6 and 8 are common advice, not tied to a study read here.

Hinduism

The Gita points to a loosening of possessiveness, and to goodwill toward others, held together by a steady mind.

  1. Loosen the sense of "mine". Gita 2.71: "That person, who gives up all material desires and lives free from a sense of greed, proprietorship, and egoism, attains perfect peace."
  2. Practise goodwill toward the rival too. Gita 12.13-14 describes the devotees who are "free from malice toward all living beings, who are friendly, and compassionate", and "free from attachment to possessions and egotism".
  3. Stay level in loss and gain. The same verses say "equipoised in happiness and distress".
  4. Make the mind a friend. Gita 6.5: "Elevate yourself through the power of your mind, and not degrade yourself, for the mind can be the friend and also the enemy of the self."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses are about detachment and goodwill in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant jealousy, or for jealousy that leads to controlling someone. Taking "proprietorship" to mean the sense of owning a person is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads point to seeing the same Self in everyone, so that a rival is no longer wholly other.

  1. See beings in the Self. Isha 6: "And he who beholds all beings in the Self, and the Self in all beings, he never turns away from it."
  2. Look at the unity. Isha 7: "When to a man who understands, the Self has become all things, what sorrow, what trouble can there be to him who once beheld that unity?"
  3. Drop the sense of difference. Katha 2.1.11: "Even by the mind this (Brahman) is to be obtained, and then there is no difference whatsoever."
  4. Hold what you have lightly. Isha 1: "Do not covet the wealth of any man!"

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses are about the Self and unity, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant jealousy, or for jealousy that leads to controlling someone. Applying them to a rival is ours.

Christianity

The Bible points to love that is secure enough not to grasp.

  1. Let love push out fear. 1 John 4:18: "There is no fear in love, but perfect love casts out fear."
  2. Practise the kind of love that is not envious. 1 Corinthians 13:4-5: "Love is patient; love is kind; love is not envious or boastful or arrogant or rude. It does not insist on its own way."
  3. Grow what the Spirit grows. Galatians 5:22-23: "the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, generosity, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control."
  4. Hear the reassurance. In the parable, the father says to the elder son: "Son, you are always with me, and all that is mine is yours" (Luke 15:31).

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. These verses are about love, fear and the fruit of the Spirit in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant jealousy, or for jealousy that leads to controlling someone.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts point to loosening the clinging that jealousy grows from.

  1. See the chain. Dhp 213-216 trace sorrow and fear back through attachment, relishing, desire and craving. Dhp 215: "Sorrow springs from desire, fear springs from desire; one free from desire has no sorrow, let alone fear."
  2. Ask what a feeling grows. DN 21 says of happiness: "should you know of a happiness: 'When I cultivate this kind of happiness, unskillful qualities grow, and skillful qualities decline.' You should not cultivate that kind of happiness." The sutta says the same of sadness and equanimity. Applying the test to jealous thoughts is ours.
  3. Understand what the states depend on. Snp 4.11: "Knowing that these states are dependent, and knowing what they depend on, the inquiring sage, having understood, is freed, and enters no dispute."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The passages are about desire and clinging in general, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant jealousy, or for jealousy that leads to controlling someone. The Dhammapada's counsel is read here as about clinging, which is a reading and not a claim about loving less.

Philosophical

Epictetus's advice is to know what kind of thing you hold, and to hold it as a loan.

  1. Remember what you are fond of. Enchiridion 3: "If you kiss your child, or your wife, say that you only kiss things which are human, and thus you will not be disturbed if either of them dies."
  2. Say "returned", not "lost". Enchiridion 11: "Never say of anything, 'I have lost it'; but, 'I have returned it.'" And: "While he gives it to you to possess, take care of it; but don't view it as your own, just as travelers view a hotel."
  3. Stop wishing for control that you do not have. Enchiridion 14: "you wish to be in control of things which you cannot".
  4. Work on what is yours. Enchiridion 14: "if you wish to have your desires undisappointed, this is in your own control."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. Epictetus is speaking of loss and of what is outside our control, and nothing here replaces a doctor or therapist for constant jealousy. Other people are not things to be returned, and the point taken here is only that another person's choices and life are not ours to own, which is ours.
