Jealousy

Effects on life, read through each lens side by side.

Lenses to show

Modern

The research read here is small and mostly correlational, and it shows jealousy and relationship trouble travelling together.

  • Checking and dissatisfaction. Elphinston and Noller studied undergraduates in romantic relationships and found that "Facebook intrusion was linked to relationship dissatisfaction, via jealous cognitions and surveillance behaviors". Jealous thoughts and checking sat on the path between heavy social-media use and unhappiness with the relationship.
  • Control. De Sousa and colleagues studied 279 adolescent couples and found that "higher jealousy in both partners was associated with increased self- and partner-reported domination victimization". Controlling behaviour is a form of dating violence, and these researchers conclude that prevention should address "maladaptive expressions of jealousy".
  • What this does not show. These are questionnaire associations in particular groups. They do not show that jealousy causes control or dissatisfaction, and they say nothing about every jealous person.

Commonly described effects, not findings from these papers: arguments, pushing away the person you fear losing, poor sleep, and a mind that will not rest. If jealousy leads you to control a partner, or a partner's jealousy frightens or isolates you, a doctor, a therapist or a domestic abuse service can help.

Sources

  • Elphinston and Noller (2011), "Time to face it! Facebook intrusion and the implications for romantic jealousy and relationship satisfaction," Cyberpsychology, Behavior, and Social Networking 14(11), 631-635. doi:10.1089/cyber.2010.0318 (external link)
  • De Sousa et al. (2026), "Interpersonal trust and control victimization in adolescent romantic relationships: the moderating role of jealousy," Child Abuse & Neglect 180, 108253. doi:10.1016/j.chiabu.2026.108253 (external link)

Both papers were read in the abstracts (Europe PMC, 2026-11-14), not in full, and the publication details were checked in Crossref. The last paragraph cites no study.

Hinduism

The Gita traces where an unchecked mind ends. Gita 2.63: "Anger leads to clouding of judgment, which results in bewilderment of memory. When memory is bewildered, the intellect gets destroyed; and when the intellect is destroyed, one is ruined."

The translator's commentary on 6.6 says of the enemies of the mind: "the demons sitting within our own mind have the ability to make us live in constant wretchedness". It lists "lust, anger, greed, envy, illusion" among them.

By contrast, Gita 12.15 describes the one who is "not a source of annoyance to anyone and who in turn are not agitated by anyone".

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses do not mention jealousy, and the commentary names envy and not jealousy. Reading the chain as the toll of jealousy is ours.

Upanishads

The Upanishads do not describe the effects of jealousy, but they describe grief as the lot of a person caught up in the world. The Mundaka Upanishad (3.1.2) says that a person "sits grieving, immersed, bewildered by his own impotence", and that when he sees the other, "then his grief passes away".

Katha 2.1.10 gives the longer reach of the same blindness: "He who sees any difference here (between Brahman and the world), goes from death to death."

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The passages are about grief and the cycle of death, and do not mention jealousy. Reading them as its toll is ours.

Christianity

The Bible shows what jealousy does to people and to the places they live.

  • It is hard to stand against. Proverbs 27:4: "Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming, but who is able to stand before jealousy?"
  • It breaks things. Galatians 5:19-21 lists "enmities, strife, jealousy, anger, quarrels, dissensions, factions, envy" among "the works of the flesh".
  • It wears the body. Proverbs 14:30: "A tranquil mind gives life to the flesh, but jealousy makes the bones rot." (See also Envy.)

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The Galatians list sets jealousy beside envy and other vices, and Proverbs 14:30 is also used in the Envy articles, and which of the two the Hebrew word means was not checked. They name its effects and not the fear of losing a rival.

Buddhism

The Buddhist texts name sorrow, fear and conflict as what comes from holding things dear.

  • Sorrow and fear. Dhp 212: "Sorrow springs from what we hold dear, fear springs from what we hold dear; one free from holding anything dear has no sorrow, let alone fear."
  • Quarrels. Snp 4.11: "Quarrels and disputes come from what we hold dear, as do lamentation and sorrow, stinginess, conceit and arrogance."
  • Enmity. DN 21: beings bound by jealousy and stinginess "still have enmity, violence, hostility, and hate", though they wish to be free of them.

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. The verses are about clinging in general, and do not describe a jealous person in particular. Reading them as the toll of jealousy is ours.

Philosophical

The philosophers describe the disturbance jealousy causes in the mind and in the relationship.

  • Lament and disturbance. Epictetus, Enchiridion 1: if you take what belongs to others as your own, "You will lament, you will be disturbed, and you will find fault both with gods and men."
  • A divided mind. Spinoza calls jealousy "a wavering of the disposition arising from combined love and hatred".
  • Pain on both sides. Spinoza adds that "a jealous man is not greeted by his beloved with the same joyful countenance as before, and this also gives him pain as a lover". The jealous person's behaviour changes how they are met.

This is related to jealousy and not identical to it. Epictetus is speaking of disturbance in general, and Spinoza describes the emotion as he understood it. Neither is a study of what jealousy does to a particular person or relationship.

Sources

  • Epictetus, Enchiridion 1 (external link) (Elizabeth Carter's translation)
  • Spinoza, Ethics, Part III, Proposition 35 note (external link) (R. H. M. Elwes's translation)

The quotations were read on 2026-11-14 at MIT Classics and in the Project Gutenberg text. The MIT page names Elizabeth Carter, though the exact edition was not established. The Spinoza sentence about the beloved's countenance is from the note, where Spinoza says it comes into play "in the case of love for a woman"; it is given here without that restriction, which is ours.
